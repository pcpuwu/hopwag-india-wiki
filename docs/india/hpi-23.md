## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **The Vedānta-Sūtra (Brahma-Sūtra / Uttara-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra / Śārīraka-Sūtra)** — the founding text of Vedānta, composed in current form likely in the **5th century CE** [00:02:20].
- **Bādarāyaṇa** — the putative author whose "decisive view" the sūtra endorses [00:07:18].
- **Śaṅkara** — the **8th-century** non-dualist (Advaita) Vedāntin whose commentary dominates how the text is read [00:01:35].
- **Jaimini** — the prior-Mīmāṃsā master, here cast as the foil whose ritual-focused view gets corrected [00:07:21].
- **The early Upaniṣads** — especially the **Chāndogya** and the **Great Forest (Bṛhadāraṇyaka)** Upaniṣad — the texts the sūtra interprets [00:04:24].
- **The Sāṃkhya school** — the rival Brahmanical system, with its unintelligent material principle **pradhāna**, made the target of sustained critique [00:13:43].

## From Mīmāṃsā to Vedānta: prior vs. posterior exegesis
- [00:00:30] Following on from the Mīmāṃsā episodes: even if you weren't persuaded that Vedic ritual brings its promised fruits, the intellectual harvest of Jaimini and his successors should convince you that a tradition devoted to **exegesis of sacred texts** can be philosophically fruitful. And if you're still the stubborn type, there's more to come, because we aren't done with Mīmāṃsā in the broader sense.
- [00:01:08] Jaimini, Śabara, Kumārila and Prabhākara represent **Pūrva** ("prior") **Mīmāṃsā**. But it was **Uttara** ("posterior") **Mīmāṃsā** that gave rise to one of the most famous branches of Indian philosophy. Uttara-Mīmāṃsā is also called **Vedānta**, and like Mīmāṃsā it eventually subdivided into rival approaches.
- [00:01:35] The most famous approach is that of the most famous Vedāntin, the **8th-century** thinker **Śaṅkara** — his system is **non-dualist** or **Advaita Vedānta**, a name many listeners will have met before finding the podcast.

## Reading the founding text before Śaṅkara
- [00:01:50] Just as it was hard to excavate Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā without being guided (and sometimes misled) by the commentator Śabara, so it is with Śaṅkara's reading of Uttara-Mīmāṃsā (which from here on is simply called **Vedānta**). The power of Śaṅkara's philosophy makes it tempting to *equate* Vedānta with that philosophy.
- [00:02:08] But just as the series tried to recover Jaimini's Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra in its own right, it now devotes an episode to the **founding text of Vedānta**, composed in its current form several centuries before Śaṅkara — likely in the **5th century CE**.

## A book of many names — one source in many guises
- [00:02:25] Predictably the book is called the **Vedānta-Sūtra**, also the **Uttara-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra**. But it has yet more titles. Its primary subject, announced in its very first line, is **brahman** — so it is sometimes the **Brahma-Sūtra**. And because it speaks of the nature of a self that possesses a bodily form (**śārīraka**), it has been called the **Śārīraka-Sūtra**.
- [00:02:52] This welter of titles could hardly be more appropriate: the core mission of the Vedānta-Sūtra is to investigate **a single source of all things, presenting itself in many guises** — and that source is, of course, brahman.
- [00:03:08] The sūtra doesn't merely argue that brahman is the source of all things; it also explores the relationship between our **individual selves** and this transcendent principle, tells us how we can come to **know** brahman, and promises **liberation** from the snares and karmic consequences of bodily existence to whoever attains that knowledge.

## A text written in code
- [00:03:25] Described that way it sounds like a systematic work of philosophical cosmology — but if so, the system is hard to extract. Like the sūtra texts at the origins of other Indian traditions, the Vedānta-Sūtra at times "seems almost to be written in code." Śaṅkara's authority looms partly because we *need* his help and that of other commentators to understand it.
- [00:03:56] The sūtra form is partly to blame, but another reason is that the Vedānta-Sūtra is a philosophical treatise that **doubles as a work of exegesis**, devoted to the texts at the end of the Veda — hence *Vedānta* — namely the **Upaniṣads**.
- [00:04:13] To be more specific: texts called Upaniṣads kept being produced alongside and long after the sūtra (later Upaniṣads are themselves an expression of the Vedānta tradition). But the Vedānta-Sūtra interprets a **select group of early Upaniṣads** — especially the **Chāndogya**, but also the **Great Forest** Upaniṣad and others. It thus revisits passages discussed earlier in the series, such as the dialogues featuring **Yājñavalkya**. Such key passages came to be known as the **"great sayings,"** receiving especially focused treatment from later Vedāntins.

## "Because of abiding and eating": the two birds
- [00:04:57] The sūtra is hard to read because it **rarely identifies the passage it interprets**. You must know the Upaniṣads so well that even a single key word reveals which text is at stake — or you fall back on the commentators.
- [00:05:15] Example: one sūtra says simply, **"because of abiding and eating."** The context shows it is meant to convince us that brahman holds of all things, but the remark stays incomprehensible until you realize it alludes to a passage describing **two birds** — one eating, the other sitting quietly. The eating bird symbolizes the **individual soul** engaged in worldly action; the quiet bird is **brahman**, which simply abides unchanging.
- [00:05:50] This is the interpretive project that dominates the text: it reads the sacred writings as putting forward a **single coherent doctrine centred on brahman**. For later Vedāntins the sūtra itself, and for good measure the **Bhagavad-Gītā**, joined the Upaniṣads as fundamental sources — which is why Śaṅkara commented on the Gītā as well as on the sūtra and several early Upaniṣads.

## Exegesis as the battlefield
- [00:06:19] This brahman-centric approach was controversial and rejected by other Brahmanical schools. When the Vedānta-Sūtra refutes those schools, it typically concentrates on showing that the **Vedānta reading is better grounded in the sacred texts**.
- [00:06:35] This is not faith triumphing over reason; it shows the **opponents are themselves committed to the truth of the Upaniṣads**. Exegesis is the ground the sūtra *chooses* on which to wage its philosophical battles.

## Bādarāyaṇa vs. Jaimini — and the technique of the sūtra
- [00:06:46] Though it shares the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra's interpretive aims, the Vedānta-Sūtra is not just a "second installment" that moves on to the Upaniṣads. The difference shows in the men credited with the two texts.
- [00:07:05] The Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra was supposedly written (or compiled) by **Jaimini**, yet he is mentioned there in the third person. The same happens in the Vedānta-Sūtra, which names and reports the ideas of its own putative author, **Bādarāyaṇa**.
- [00:07:22] At several points Bādarāyaṇa's ideas are contrasted with Jaimini's. In the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra it is Jaimini who usually gets the last decisive word; in the Vedānta-Sūtra it is **Bādarāyaṇa** whose view is allowed to triumph — presumably why tradition credits him with authorship.
- [00:07:43] Safer to use the technical terms of sūtra literature: Jaimini's opinion is treated as the initial or **prima facie view, the pūrva-pakṣa**, only to be corrected by the more adequate view, the **siddhānta**, of Bādarāyaṇa.

## The shift of emphasis: from ritual to liberation
- [00:08:02] Bādarāyaṇa's authoritative position has consequences. Where Jaimini placed all his focus on **Vedic ritual**, Bādarāyaṇa is more interested in the prospect of **liberation through the knowledge of brahman**. At one point we're told a person with such knowledge can dispense with things like **lighting ritual fires**.
- [00:08:20] In keeping with this, Bādarāyaṇa is less attached to the life of the **householder** and tends to emphasize the superiority of the **ascetic life** of renouncers. The knowledge of brahman imparted in the Upaniṣads is valued **for its own sake** — not, as Jaimini would have it, as a supplement helping us grasp the purpose of ritual.
- [00:08:50] There are other disagreements. Bādarāyaṇa thinks **even the gods** can achieve liberation by knowing brahman, whereas Jaimini restricts liberation to humans and even **denies the existence of gods**. A core Mīmāṃsā doctrine is that the **Veda had no origin but has always existed**; that idea appears in the Vedānta-Sūtra too, but so does the idea that the **Veda has its source in brahman**.

## Brahman as cause — objections answered
- [00:09:16] It is typical of the Age of the Sūtra that the theory develops through **dialectical argument**: Bādarāyaṇa (or whoever was finally responsible for the text) constantly raises objections against himself, then refutes them. The weightiest concern the **nature of brahman as a cause**.
- [00:09:37] Objection: how can all things be produced by brahman, given that brahman is **transcendent** above those things? It seems impossible that a cause give rise to things utterly unlike it. Reply: such things are simply **known to happen**. Bādarāyaṇa doesn't supply an example, but Śaṅkara's commentary offers cases where the living emerges from the dead (a scorpion "spontaneously generated" from a pile of manure) and the inanimate comes from the living (we grow hair and fingernails).
- [00:10:15] Objection: why would brahman **bother** to produce the world at all? For no real reason — out of **mere sport or play** that brahman does so.
- [00:10:22] Objection: but wouldn't brahman have had good reason *not* to cause a world so full of **suffering and unjust inequality**? True, concedes Bādarāyaṇa, but this is **not brahman's fault**: all suffering is punishment for wicked deeds, perhaps committed in past lives. Since the **cycle of rebirth** has gone on endlessly, any particular suffering can always be referred back to earlier actions that justify it. (Here Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta agree the world has always existed much as now — though Bādarāyaṇa insists this eternal world has a source in brahman, a notion foreign to Jaimini.)

## The conceptual sequence of creation
- [00:11:12] The world caused by brahman may be permanent, but it has a kind of **conceptual sequence** that recalls the Upaniṣads. When **Yājñavalkya** was challenged by his female interlocutor **Gārgī** to name the fundamental principle on which all things are "woven," he eventually satisfied her by saying it might be **space**.
- [00:11:40] Naturally Bādarāyaṇa accepts this and infers that **space is the very first effect of brahman** — or, at another point, that space is even **identical** to brahman. From space emerge further principles reminiscent of **ancient Greek cosmology**: the sequence **air, fire, water, earth**, produced one after another with brahman as ultimate source. When the world cycle finally ends, they return to brahman, **dissolving in the reverse order** of their generation.

## Brahman as efficient and material cause
- [00:12:16] So the universe is like an effective stand-up comedian: it has great material — the best possible material, namely **brahman itself**, which is both the **moving (efficient) cause** of all things and in some way the **stuff out of which** things are made. Taking a leaf from **Aristotle**, brahman is both the **efficient and material cause** of the world — captured in the dictum that brahman is "the support of heaven, earth, and all other things."
- [00:12:52] Though it takes on the forms of other things, brahman is in itself **formless**. The Upaniṣads characterize it variously (freely admitted in the sūtra), but many characterizations are **metaphors** — a favorite being that brahman is like the **sun or single light** whose rays fall on and illuminate many things. The sacred text may even mislead, as when it suggests brahman has **size**. In fact it does not: nothing other than it exists, so it is **everywhere**, pervading all things rather than occupying any particular dimensions.

## The quarrel with Sāṃkhya — milk from grass
- [00:13:33] This brings a fine example of the "orthodox" Hindu schools entering **heated disputes** with one another. Bādarāyaṇa's insistence that brahman is the **material** cause of all things brings him into direct conflict with the **Sāṃkhya** school, which is named explicitly and made the target of a sustained critique.
- [00:13:55] Sāṃkhya sees the world as **evolving naturally from an underlying material stuff called pradhāna**. Though Sāṃkhya too positions itself within the Brahmanical tradition, here it seems **diametrically opposed** to Vedānta: far from recognizing an intelligent, divine creator mind manifesting as all things, it has everything generated from pradhāna, an **unintelligent** stuff.
- [00:14:29] Against this Bādarāyaṇa invokes the most familiar proof of a **guiding intelligence** in the universe — its striking, apparently **providential order**. He appeals "not so much to a stock example as a livestock example": getting the world we see out of pradhāna without brahman would be like getting **milk out of grass without the involvement of a living cause, namely a cow**.
- [00:14:57] But the dispute is not only philosophical. Bādarāyaṇa wants us to see not just that the Sāṃkhya theory makes no sense, but that it **cannot make sense of key Upaniṣadic texts** — a point that emerges especially in Śaṅkara's commentary, which delves into extensive exegesis to show Sāṃkhya misreads the background passages.

## Against materialism — and the self that is not the body
- [00:15:19] The sūtra also sets its face against more radical **materialism and naturalism**. Not only do the Vedas reject such attitudes, but if we relied on sensation and material causes alone we could never attain the **liberation** so dear to Bādarāyaṇa. Materialist explanations fail for the world as a whole and also for the **human individual**: the individual's self cannot be reduced to the body but is in fact **independent of it**.

## Brahman and the individual self: identity held in tension
- [00:15:54] Here comes what may be the single most famous teaching of the Vedānta-Sūtra, famous partly for its central role in Śaṅkara: that **brahman has a very intimate relationship to the individual self**. What sort of relationship exactly is not an easy question.
- [00:16:09] Śaṅkara's **Advaita Vedānta** insists **nothing is real apart from brahman** — including every individual self. So the apparent fact that each of us is distinct from other things and from the ground of all existence is actually an **illusion**.
- [00:16:33] Śaṅkara can draw on compelling evidence: Bādarāyaṇa suggests there is ultimately **no difference between brahman as cause and the things it creates**; on the self, he says brahman is **identical with our vital breath**, and instructs us to grasp brahman by **meditating on it as our own self**.
- [00:16:56] Yet other passages seem to draw a **firm distinction** between the individual self and the highest self that is brahman. We glimpse here a debate already raging *before* the sūtra was written: earlier scholars who commented on the Upaniṣads held differing views — e.g. that brahman is simply the **material constituent** of the individual self, or that the individual self is **distinct** from brahman but becomes identical to it **once liberated** from the body.
- [00:17:37] Perhaps the best conclusion on the basis of the sūtra itself is that the relation between brahman and the individual self is **held in tension between sheer identity and difference**. One's impression depends on which passage one reads — and certain passages "want to have it both ways," as when brahman is said to relate to individual selves **like a snake to its coils**.

## Where we go next
- [00:17:59] As Vedānta develops, we'll meet someone who definitely **doesn't want to have it both ways**. After a short summer break the podcast returns in September with "only one way forward" — the **non-dualism of Śaṅkara and Advaita Vedānta**.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Vedānta-Sūtra** — the founding text of Vedānta (Uttara-Mīmāṃsā), composed in current form likely **5th c. CE**; cryptic, code-like, both philosophy and Upaniṣadic exegesis. Also called the **Brahma-Sūtra** (its first line is on brahman), the **Uttara-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra**, and the **Śārīraka-Sūtra** (treating the embodied self).
- **Bādarāyaṇa** — putative author; the sūtra's **siddhānta** (settled view) is given to him, triumphing over Jaimini's.
- **Pūrva- vs. Uttara-Mīmāṃsā** — "prior" Mīmāṃsā (Jaimini, Śabara, Kumārila, Prabhākara; ritual exegesis) vs. "posterior" Mīmāṃsā = **Vedānta** (exegesis of the Upaniṣads, focused on brahman).
- **Jaimini** — the Mīmāṃsā master here cast as the **pūrva-pakṣa** (initial view); ritual-focused, householder-oriented, restricts liberation to humans and denies gods.
- **Śaṅkara** — **8th-c.** non-dualist whose commentary dominates the reading of the sūtra; founder of **Advaita Vedānta**.
- **brahman** — the single formless source of all things; both **efficient and material cause** of the world (Aristotle's terms), pervading everything, intimately related to the individual self.
- **the early Upaniṣads** — esp. the **Chāndogya** and **Great Forest (Bṛhadāraṇyaka)**; the texts the sūtra interprets, often by a single key word (the "great sayings").
- **"because of abiding and eating"** — a one-line sūtra alluding to the **two birds**: the eating bird = the individual soul in worldly action; the quiet bird = brahman abiding unchanged.
- **liberation through knowledge of brahman** — Bādarāyaṇa's central goal; valued for its own sake, lets one dispense with ritual fires, available even to the gods, favoring the ascetic over the householder.
- **brahman's "sport or play"** — brahman creates the world for no real reason but mere play; the world's suffering is not brahman's fault but karmic punishment across endless rebirths.
- **the creation sequence** — space (first effect, even identified with brahman, after Yājñavalkya's answer to Gārgī) → air → fire → water → earth, dissolving back into brahman in reverse at the cycle's end.
- **Sāṃkhya / pradhāna** — rival Brahmanical school deriving the world from an **unintelligent** material principle (pradhāna); refuted via the world's providential order — "milk from grass without a cow."
- **identity-vs-difference of self and brahman** — the sūtra's most famous, unresolved teaching; held "in tension," brahman relating to individual selves "like a snake to its coils."

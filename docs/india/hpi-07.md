## Setting the scene: who's talking
- [00:00:17] Adamson opens with the usual credits — the *History of Philosophy in India* with Jonardon Ganeri and Peter Adamson, supported by King's College London and the LMU in Munich — then introduces the guest: **Brian Black**, a lecturer in religious studies at **Lancaster University**. Today is an interview about the **Upaniṣads**, the texts discussed over the last few episodes.
- [00:00:50] Adamson sets up the central puzzle: the Upaniṣads are "routinely described as a kind of commentary on earlier Vedic texts," yet when you actually read them they don't read like commentaries at all — they're remarkably **heterogeneous** in their material. He asks Black to explain how exactly they relate to the earlier Vedic literature.

## How the Upaniṣads relate to the Vedas
- [00:01:11] Black agrees they are not, technically, commentaries — they *say* a lot about earlier Vedic texts, but that's different. He breaks Adamson's question into two: their **heterogeneity** and their **relation to the Vedas** more broadly.
- [00:01:41] On heterogeneity: the Upaniṣads were composed **over a long stretch of time**. None of the dates for ancient Indian texts are anywhere near exact, but the scholarly consensus puts their composition between roughly **700 BCE and 200 BCE**. Anything written across five centuries won't carry a single systematic position.
- [00:02:22] They were also composed by members of **different Vedic schools**, often in **direct competition** with one another. Many Upaniṣads talk about the **self** (ātman) or **ultimate reality** (brahman) and share basic concerns, but individual Upaniṣads — or even different sections of the same one — can differ in interpretation or emphasis, precisely because their authors were vying over who had the best teaching of the self or of ultimate reality.
- [00:03:09] On their relation to the Vedas: the Upaniṣads **are part of the Vedas** — the **fourth and final section**. So they can't be "commentaries on" the Vedas because they themselves *are* Veda. But the relationship to the *earlier* sections is complex. By claiming Vedic status they claim authority for themselves, and like the Vedas they privilege the **Brahmin caste**, the **Vedic ritual**, and the Vedas themselves.
- [00:03:53] Yet at the same time they **criticize** earlier Vedic material. Some sections attack Brahmin ritualists for paying too much attention to ritual and not enough to the new philosophical ideas about the self, ultimate reality, the breath, and so on — or they criticize Brahmins for being **Brahmin in name only**, without really understanding the teachings. So the Upaniṣads are simultaneously part of the Vedas and critical of earlier Vedic material.
- [00:04:43] Adamson finds this combination striking — presenting yourself as sacred literature while reflecting critically on an earlier text. It may happen in other religious traditions, but it's an unusual structure within Vedic literature: a **layered series of texts**, the last of which (the Upaniṣads) reflects on the others. Black confirms there are **four layers** in all, the Upaniṣads being the last.

## Who wrote them, and for whom
- [00:05:12] Adamson asks about **authors and intended audience**: the authors claim religious insight and the power to deliver a revealed or sacred discourse — who were they, and who were they talking to? Or, given the long span of composition, is that even a single question?
- [00:05:48] Black: we don't really know who wrote them, but the internal evidence strongly suggests the authors were **male Brahmins**. Many of the earlier Upaniṣads also claim to be **secret or esoteric** — you can only learn an Upaniṣad in the proper way, from the proper teacher, after proper initiation. That tells us something important: they do **not** claim **universal** status.
- [00:06:28] He contrasts this with later Hindu texts like the **Bhagavad-Gītā**, which present themselves as teachings available to everybody. The Upaniṣads — though Black thinks their subject matter is in fact relevant to everybody and worth reading today — do **not** claim that status for themselves. So the primary authors were Brahmins and the primary audience was Brahmins.
- [00:07:10] But there's a second audience. Especially in the earlier Upaniṣads — the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka**, the **Chāndogya**, the **Kauṣītaki** — there seems to be an attempt to address a **royal / kingly community** as well. Black gives reasons.
- [00:07:35] First, several passages claim that the knowledge in them **originally came from outside Brahmin circles** — from kings, the kingly class, or even a god or goddess.
- [00:07:55] Some of these claims look mostly **rhetorical**. In the **Chāndogya**, a king claims to deliver a teaching never before known by Brahmins — but what he then teaches is almost verbatim material already found in older grammatical texts. So it reads more as a rhetorical device than a statement of historical fact. Still, the very fact that the text wants to **bring a kingly audience into the conversation**, sometimes crediting the knowledge to them, is significant.
- [00:08:49] Adamson recalls passages where the kingly character tells the Brahmin, "Actually, I'll explain the teaching to you, rather than you to me" — and one passage (perhaps in the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka / Great Forest** Upaniṣad) where it's expressly noted that this reversal is **unusual** but will be allowed anyway.
- [00:09:10] Black reads these passages — admitting interpretation here is speculative — as a way of **reinforcing Brahmanical knowledge**: making the king the *mouthpiece* of the teaching the Brahmins are promoting, to bolster the Brahmins' own status.
- [00:09:38] A second way kings appear as audience is in the **benefit-statements** that often follow a teaching. Upaniṣadic passages frequently end by stating what the knowledge enables. Many promise things like **immortality** or a long life — but some promise rewards aimed squarely at **royalty**: that you will **defeat all your enemies**, **expand your territory**, even (in one case) **"smash all your enemies to bits."** That kind of payoff strongly suggests the teachings were directed outward toward the **royal class**, not Brahmins alone.
- [00:10:53] Adamson jokes that "smash your enemies" is not a benefit a Buddhist would advertise. Black agrees Buddhists don't make those claims exactly — but notes that **Buddhist sources** do, in some cases, seem to have had a **royal audience** in mind too. (Adamson previews a later episode on **Aśoka the Great**, whose inscriptions seem influenced by Buddhism.)

## "Religious," "sacred," "philosophical" — competing labels?
- [00:11:19] Adamson raises a worry some listeners may have: the Upaniṣads are sacred texts and foundational texts for a religion (Hinduism). Is that consistent with calling them **philosophical**? Are the two roles in competition?
- [00:11:51] Black: words like **sacred**, **religious**, and **philosophical** are *our* very modern categories. The Upaniṣads themselves don't draw those lines between the kinds of material they discuss. So things that look contradictory to us — what we'd expect from a "religious" versus a "philosophical" text — wouldn't have been contradictions at all to the composers and early listening communities (or, he adds, to most other ancient texts).
- [00:12:41] His comparison: **Plato** is taught in philosophy departments, but one could argue Plato was also a **deeply religious thinker** in whom religion plays an important role — we just don't tend to read him that way. Go back to the ancient world, in Greece or India, and our own distinctions often don't quite work.
- [00:13:12] Black is candid that he himself **often approaches the Upaniṣads as philosophy** — not the only way he reads them, but a frequent one — and that this is a **hermeneutic / interpretive choice** on his part as a modern reader and teacher. He thinks it's an important choice: if we labeled the Upaniṣads as *merely* religious, then made the further assumption that they are therefore "Hindu texts" specific to Hinduism, many readers who aren't from India or don't have a Hindu background would **miss out** on the insights and questions the texts raise — questions relevant to everyone's life but potentially "closed off" by an overly religious framing.
- [00:14:30] So none of these labels — sacred, philosophical — works completely, but each has its place in a series on world philosophy. Reading the Upaniṣads as philosophy reveals **overlaps** in questions, themes, and methods of inquiry, both with other ancient texts and with our own philosophical inquiries today.
- [00:15:04] Adamson sharpens the point into a two-way claim: approaching the Upaniṣads as philosophy says something **about the Upaniṣads** (there's philosophy there) *and* something **about philosophy** (a text like an Upaniṣad can count as a philosophical text). Black agrees emphatically: reading them as philosophy lets us notice things we'd miss through a purely religious lens, and at the same time prompts us to **reflect on — and perhaps expand — our own category of philosophy.**

## The dialogue form: what "dialogue" means
- [00:15:58] Adamson flags the feature most likely to be cited as evidence the Upaniṣads are philosophical: they are intensely **dialogical** — exchanges between teacher and student, between two sages, between competing sages, between sages and kings. This inevitably evokes **Plato** and his philosophical dialogues from antiquity. Black has edited a book on dialogue in the Upaniṣads and ancient Indian traditions; Adamson asks what he means by "dialogue" and why it matters for how we read these texts.
- [00:16:49] Black's primary use of "dialogue" is **descriptive and literary**: portions of the Upaniṣads are presented as a conversation between named (and sometimes unnamed) speakers, often with emphasis on the speakers' **interaction** with one another.
- [00:17:25] Dialogue is a crucial **literary mechanism** in ancient Indian religious and philosophical texts, used to **frame whole works**. Some Upaniṣads open as a dialogue and present their entire content as one — he names the **Praśna**, the **Maitrī**, and the **Kaṭha** Upaniṣads as texts literally organized as dialogues. These are thought to be **later** Upaniṣads.
- [00:18:11] Interestingly, the **earlier** Upaniṣads — the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka** and the **Chāndogya** — which contain some of the **liveliest and most engaging dialogues**, don't necessarily have as many dialogues overall as the later ones. So the technique of using dialogue to structure an *entire* text seems to have **developed over time**; it isn't there from the very beginning.
- [00:18:49] After the Upaniṣads, later texts — the **Mahābhārata**, the **Rāmāyaṇa**, and throughout the **Purāṇas** — are likewise organized as dialogue, the whole text framed as a conversation. So the book's starting point is simply to notice how **important and recurring** dialogue is in organizing textual material.

## What dialogue *does*
- [00:19:24] But dialogue isn't always doing the same job; it has several different uses, and on any given occasion may do some and not others.
- [00:19:46] In the Upaniṣads, one main function is to **mimic or demonstrate the text's orality**. There is heavy emphasis on the **lineage** of teachings — almost every Upaniṣad includes a long list of teachers and students across several generations. Any given dialogue can be read as a **snapshot of one point in a lineage**, re-emphasizing that the Vedas were originally composed and transmitted **orally**.
- [00:20:35] A second function: dialogue **confers authority** by telling us **who the teacher is**. The Upaniṣads see the emergence of important **philosophical personalities** — **Yājñavalkya**, **Uddālaka Āruṇi**, **Śāṇḍilya** — who recur not only in the Upaniṣads but in earlier texts like the **Brāhmaṇas**. So when the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka** delivers famous teachings about the self, karma, or rebirth, the teaching carries weight not only from its content but because **Yājñavalkya said it** — much as Buddhist teachings gain authority as the **words of the Buddha**.
- [00:21:44] A third function: dialogue puts philosophical or religious teaching in a **social context**. Black says this interested him while writing his book on the Upaniṣads, because **Indian philosophy has a reputation** for being conducted by solitary figures — the image that springs to mind is someone going off **alone into the forest** to practice yoga. Yet in some of the very *oldest* philosophical texts we **don't** see that: the main philosophical content unfolds in **social situations**, with people interacting. Even where a scene isn't a debate — sometimes it's just a teacher telling a student how it is, and the student listening attentively — there's still a sense that the ideas are placed in a setting where they **could be contested**.

## Are the dialogues actually *philosophical*? (Adamson plays devil's advocate)
- [00:22:58] Adamson pushes back: any honest reader admits the Upaniṣadic dialogues are not obviously philosophical in the **Platonic** sense. In Plato you get Socrates walking an interlocutor through a sequence of argument; in the Upaniṣads you often get **riddling or mysterious pronouncements**, with the interlocutor only saying "yes," "no," or "try again" — so you don't seem to have **two genuine partners in an argument**. Is that a reason to deny the Upaniṣads are genuinely philosophical, or is our job as readers to **reconstruct the underlying arguments** behind the riddling remarks?
- [00:24:02] Black: first, the Upaniṣads "don't care whether we call them philosophy or not." Some dialogues are more philosophical than others, but yes — it is **our job** to look closely, at *all* the material (not only the dialogues), to uncover whatever **philosophical methods** are there.
- [00:24:33] He makes two moves. First, although the dialogues are what draw attention to the Upaniṣads as *possibly* philosophical, he'd argue the dialogues are only **one of several sections** in the text that count as philosophical: the Upaniṣads also engage in **introspection, observation, and experimentation**, none of which require a dialogue.
- [00:25:18] Second, even among dialogues, some are frankly **not very philosophical**, but others very much are. In a recent paper he's examined how some dialogues carry a **dialectical structure** — the same kind of dialectical structure visible later in Indian philosophy. So even a dialogue that *doesn't* look like a contested exchange can still set up the **structure of an argument**, even if the characters themselves aren't visibly arguing.
- [00:26:07] His example of that structure: one character presents a **thesis**, the next offers a **counter-argument**, and a third section either tries to **bring the two together** or accepts a **lingering tension** between them. So the Upaniṣads aren't explicit, as a Platonic dialogue is, about *how* they're being philosophical — but read closely enough, a good case can be made that they are.

## Women characters in the Upaniṣads
- [00:26:49] Adamson notes Black mentioned recurring characters, and turns to the **women** who occasionally appear among the male sages — adding that there will be a whole later episode on the role of women in classical Indian texts, but since Black has written on this, he asks for his view.
- [00:27:24] Black finds it "really refreshing" to see them. The texts are still **primarily about men**, but the female characters appear in **positive ways** — and, tellingly, the text doesn't make a **big deal** of their presence, as if the authors would **expect** to see women, at least on some occasions, included in **public philosophy**.
- [00:28:09] He reads this on two levels. It may reflect a **historical reality** in which some women did participate in philosophy in ancient India — we can't know for sure, but he sees no reason to rule it out. But many female characters also have a **symbolic function** in the text.
- [00:28:40] The symbolic point ties back to the Upaniṣads' critique of earlier Vedic ritual. To be a full participant in the **Vedic ritual** you had to be not only a Brahmin man but a man **with sons** — the sons would carry on your ritual duties after death. **Yājñavalkya**, the main character of the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka** and the figure **most critical** of former Vedic practices, also claims that you **don't need sons** in order to be enlightened and to teach about the self.
- [00:29:53] And Yājñavalkya turns out to be the character **most likely to converse with women**: he has a long conversation about the self with his **wife** in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, and he's involved in a debate in which **Gārgī**, a female character, takes part. So we both see women appear (possibly reflecting a real history of women in philosophy) **and** learn something about Yājñavalkya: his engaging women in philosophical discussion is, Black argues, part of his **larger critique of early Vedic ritual practice**.

## The Upaniṣads, Buddhism and Jainism: who influenced whom?
- [00:30:44] As a wrap-up — the podcast is about to move on to **Buddhism** — Adamson asks Black to situate the Upaniṣads relative to the other developments of roughly the same period: to what extent do we see **influence, even mutual influence**, between some Upaniṣads and early Buddhist texts?
- [00:31:12] Black calls it a hotly contested question with **two main positions** in current scholarship.
- [00:31:26] **Position one:** the Upaniṣads were composed **before** the rise of Buddhism and exerted a **large influence** on it — and on other movements of the time like **Jainism** and **Ājīvikism**.
- [00:31:49] **Position two:** Buddhism, Jainism and Ājīvikism emerged in a region **further east** than where the Upaniṣads were composed. The Upaniṣads come from around what's now **Delhi and Vārāṇasī**, whereas Buddhism arose in roughly what is now **Bihar** — so in that eastern region the Upaniṣads may not have been well known, and these new movements shouldn't be read as **reactionary** teachings against them.
- [00:32:42] Black places himself **in the middle**. On one hand, to understand early Buddhism you **can't** treat everything as a reaction to the Vedas; the old analogy that "**Buddhism is to Vedic religion as Protestantism is to Catholicism**" simply **doesn't work**. On the other hand, he finds it hard to believe the Upaniṣads had **no** influence on at least *some* early Buddhist and Jain teachings.
- [00:33:28] His example: even though some Buddhist scholars deny that the **doctrine of not-self (anattā)** was a response to the Upaniṣadic ideal of the self, Black finds it hard not to see a relationship between the two — and notes that other Buddhist material reveals at least a **shared philosophical and literary world** that both traditions emerged from.
- [00:34:10] More concretely: some dialogues in our **earliest Buddhist texts** are **the same dialogues found in the Upaniṣads**, just with the characters **renamed** and the closing teaching a little different. He also points to the well-known **motif of one's head shattering apart if one loses a philosophical debate** — its oldest references are in earlier Vedic material (the **Brāhmaṇas**), it appears prominently in the Upaniṣads, and it recurs in early Buddhist literature. So some **contact** is clearly going on.
- [00:35:00] Black's own **hunch**: the Upaniṣads probably **predate** the earliest Buddhist material by at least a generation or two. But even if not, he sees the Upaniṣads, early Buddhism and early Jainism as emerging from a **shared world** in which these people **knew of each other's teachings** and were constantly articulating their own positions **in response to their rivals**.
- [00:35:32] Adamson closes: next they'll look ahead to Buddhism in a couple of episodes, but first — before leaving Vedic material — the next episode turns to the **grammatical analysis** of Vedic language and the "astounding work" of the grammarian **Pāṇini**. He thanks Brian Black for coming on, and invites listeners to rejoin him and **Jonardon Ganeri** next time.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Brian Black** — lecturer in religious studies at Lancaster University; the episode's guest, author of a book on the Upaniṣads and editor of a volume on dialogue in ancient Indian traditions.
- **Upaniṣads** — the **fourth and final** layer of the Vedas; heterogeneous texts composed **c. 700–200 BCE** by male Brahmins of competing Vedic schools; claim Vedic authority yet criticize earlier Vedic ritualism.
- **Veda / Vedic layers** — a layered series of four sections, the last of which (the Upaniṣads) reflects critically on the earlier ones; an unusual self-reflexive structure.
- **brahman / ātman** — ultimate reality and the self; the recurring subjects the Upaniṣads' rival schools competed to teach best.
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad ("Great Forest")** — an early Upaniṣad with the liveliest dialogues; home of Yājñavalkya and famous teachings on the self, karma and rebirth.
- **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** — early Upaniṣad; contains a king's (rhetorical) claim to teach knowledge unknown to Brahmins.
- **Kauṣītaki Upaniṣad** — early Upaniṣad addressing, in part, a royal audience.
- **Praśna / Maitrī / Kaṭha Upaniṣads** — later Upaniṣads literally organized as a single framing dialogue.
- **Yājñavalkya** — major Upaniṣadic sage, fiercest critic of old Vedic ritual; claims one needs no sons for enlightenment; converses with women (his wife; and Gārgī).
- **Uddālaka Āruṇi / Śāṇḍilya** — recurring Upaniṣadic philosophical personalities, also found in the Brāhmaṇas.
- **Gārgī** — female character who takes part in a debate with Yājñavalkya; evidence (real and symbolic) for women in public philosophy.
- **dialogue** — a literary mechanism that mimics the texts' orality, confers authority by naming the teacher, sets teachings in a social context, and can carry a dialectical thesis–counter-argument–synthesis structure.
- **Brāhmaṇas** — earlier Vedic texts; source of the "head-shattering" debate motif later seen in Upaniṣads and Buddhism.
- **Bhagavad-Gītā** — later Hindu text that, unlike the secret/esoteric Upaniṣads, claims to be a teaching available to everybody.
- **Buddhism / Jainism / Ājīvikism** — roughly contemporaneous movements; debated whether they were influenced by the Upaniṣads or arose independently further east (Bihar vs. Delhi–Vārāṇasī).
- **not-self (anattā)** — Buddhist doctrine Black suspects stands in some relationship to the Upaniṣadic ideal of the self.
- **Aśoka the Great** — later king whose inscriptions seem influenced by Buddhism; cited as evidence Buddhists too had royal audiences in mind.

## A cloud, a giraffe, and a crocodile — the parable of Liza
- [00:00:36] Jack and Jill, idle and lazy, lie on their backs watching clouds and argue: Jill says one cloud looks like a crocodile, Jack insists it's a dead ringer for a giraffe. A third child, Liza, who has more conscientiously fetched her water, settles the dispute by pointing out that the cloud is simply a cloud — the animals are the work of their imaginations, "superimposed" on the cloud.
- [00:01:13] Liza pushes further: even the *concept* of cloud is something we superimpose on our perceptions. So what *are* we perceiving — just something fluffy and white? No: the object of perception is **ineffable**, and even "fluffiness" and "whiteness" are themselves mental constructions. With that she departs (and, Jack notes, "there's a hole in her bucket"). Liza, it turns out, has been reading **Dignāga**.

## Dignāga and his works
- [00:01:48] Liza's enthusiasm is well placed: Dignāga is "one of the most original thinkers in the whole history of Indian philosophy." His ideas transformed not just Buddhist thought but the theories of rival schools — later thinkers grappled with them and with their reworking in his great follower **Dharmakīrti**, and even his opponents quietly adjusted their own systems to absorb his approaches and distinctions.
- [00:02:21] His greatest work is the **Pramāṇa-samuccaya**, "collected verses on the sources of knowledge." He also wrote two short treatises: the **Hetucakra** ("study of the Wheel of Reasons"), an innovative analysis of fallacious argumentation, and the **Ālambana-parīkṣā** ("examination of the foundations of experience"), a work of just eight verses offering a radical rethink of Buddhist ideas about perception. The episode begins with this little treatise.

## Direct realism and the Nyāya
- [00:02:53] Common sense says we use perception to grasp ordinary objects in our environment — clouds, giraffes, crocodiles. Philosophers rarely leave common sense alone; at the very least they coin a fancy name, here **direct realism**.
- [00:03:18] In the Indian tradition direct realism was upheld by the **Nyāya** school. They were led into some strange claims of their own — that we can also perceive universals and absences — but their basic point was that common sense is right: we really do perceive *whole objects*, not merely the nearest surface of a thing, or bare flashes of colour, wafting scents and momentary noises.

## Why external things can't be the objects of perception (the Ālambana-parīkṣā)
- [00:03:43] Dignāga prefers to *revise* common sense — unsurprising for a Buddhist, but he goes further, criticising earlier Buddhist theories as insufficiently sceptical. He follows in the footsteps of **Vasubandhu** (who may have been his teacher), developing the **Yogācāra** approach to perception. He argues that the immediate objects of perception cannot be external things — not even the fleeting atomic **dharmas** recognised in **Abhidharma** Buddhism.
- [00:04:11] The Buddhists call the supposed object "out there" the **ālambana** — the foundation, ground or objective support of the perceptual experience. Dignāga sets two demands on anything that could be the real object of a perception. **First**, it must be the *cause* of the experience: to see a giraffe, the giraffe must cause my visual perception. But causation alone isn't enough — if a mad scientist causes my perception of a giraffe, the scientist is the cause yet I am not *seeing* the scientist. **Second**, then, the perception must *match* the thing perceived — my experience must have "the same form" as that thing. How could I be perceiving a giraffe if the thing I claim to perceive has the shape of a crocodile? Unlike Liza's bucket, this looks watertight.
- [00:05:22] Dignāga now draws a surprising result: external things can't satisfy both demands. Buddhists hold that clouds, giraffes and crocodiles are made of **atoms**. Atoms do seem the sort of thing that can *cause* perception, so they tick the first box — the atoms making up a cloud cause me to see the cloud. But I do not *see* the atoms; atoms aren't perceptible, and even if observable under special conditions, it isn't atoms I see when my gaze falls on a cloud. So the second requirement fails: the atoms don't *match* the perception in form.
- [00:05:56] Maybe we don't see individual atoms but a **conglomerate** of them — the collection making up a crocodile. That fixes the second condition, since the conglomeration does have the matching form. But sadly the conglomeration is "nothing over and above the atoms," so it can't be a *distinct* cause, and the first condition fails. Analogy [00:06:17]: from a distance you see a **forest** — the conglomerate, the wood and not the trees — yet it is the trees that cause your perception, because in reality there are only trees to do the causing.
- [00:06:34] A third option: perhaps perception is caused not by the atoms or their conglomerate but by some **power or shape** in the atoms. Consider a **rainbow** — there's no real arc of colour there; it is composed of water droplets, each helping produce in you the vision of a rainbow. Interesting, but Dignāga makes short work of it: if the perception-causing feature belongs to the atoms *intrinsically*, the same atoms rearranged should still cause the same perception — yet when the airborne particles shift, you no longer see a rainbow. And consider the more radical recombination when "a crocodile eats and digests a giraffe," the giraffe's atoms going to build the crocodile's body — crocodiles who feast on giraffe meat don't start to look more like giraffes.
- [00:07:40] Dignāga concludes that what we take to be ordinary objects of perception are really only products of the human mind. What we perceive he calls **knowable interior forms** — internal forms that both *cause* our experience and *provide its content*, something nothing outside us can do.

## Perception and inference; particulars and universals (the Pramāṇa-samuccaya)
- [00:08:06] For a fuller, somewhat different account, turn to the mature **Pramāṇa-samuccaya**. Its first chapter distinguishes two ways of apprehending the world: **perception** and **inference** (alternatively, experience and reasoning). This isn't original to him — Vasubandhu's brother **Asaṅga** had defined *three* sources of knowledge: perception, inference and **testimony**. Dignāga simply takes over the list and drops the third item.
- [00:08:41] The two remaining cognitions, perception and inference, correspond respectively to the **individual** and the **general** — to particulars and universals. This sounds like common sense: there's the particular white cloud on one hand, and the universal feature of *whiteness* (appearing in this cloud, that cloud, and an alarming number of the hairs on your head) on the other.
- [00:09:06] But Dignāga insists universals do *not* exist in nature, only in our minds. Like the **nominalists** discussed in the medieval-philosophy podcast, he thinks universals are mere concepts and that everything real is singular or individual. He takes it to be a straightforward contradiction that one universal could be in many instances — a single whiteness in many clouds — so we certainly cannot *perceive* universals.
- [00:09:36] As for what we *can* perceive, you might expect one individual cloud, or the individual white colour of a given cloud — but even to say that is to deploy the very universal concepts ("white," "cloud") that are fantasies of the mind. Instead, the objects of perception are **inexpressible / ineffable**. The closest we can come to imagining it is something like a perceived flash of colour — better, an entirely *unclassified* flash of visual awareness. Given the critique of external objects, such a flash is born in the mind, not by any external object: a particular, indefinable, momentary object of experience within our mental life.
- [00:10:24] So much for perception. What about **inference**? Precisely *because* universals are mental constructs, we are able to think about relationships between concepts like colour, whiteness, cloud and giraffe — that white things are coloured, that some clouds look like giraffes, that giraffes are not white. This lets us organise concepts into hierarchies, like genera and species in the logic of **Aristotle**: giraffe falls under animal, white under colour. (This will prove central to his theory of language.) Human thought is largely the exploration of relationships between mentally constructed concepts; what lies under these constructions is not external objects but particular perceptions.

## Perception cannot err
- [00:11:08] Note that Dignāga is *not* a radical sceptic denying all knowledge. On the contrary, he thinks we have **immediate knowledge through perception** — a direct, undistorted sensory acquaintance uncontaminated by imagination. To secure this he even stipulates that perception can *never* be in error. Every apparent perceptual error — seeing a rope as a snake, a crocodile as a giraffe — is put down to the distorting intervention of **concepts**.
- [00:11:57] For this reason he complains about the **Nyāya** definition of perception as cognition that "arises from sense objects and is unerring and inexpressible": for Dignāga the definition is not so much wrong as *redundant* — all true cognition is perception, and it is always unerring and inexpressible already. Error arises only when concepts, which are necessarily *general*, get superimposed onto perceptual data that is necessarily *particular and unique*. Pure, unconceptualised perception is therefore invariably error-free.

## Bracketing the external world — Dignāga vs Dharmakīrti
- [00:12:53] If we saw a rope and correctly classified it under the concept "rope," that too would involve mental construction, not just immediate perception. But Dignāga isn't denying the reality of the rope, or of *something* out in the world; he simply **brackets** the question of whether external things exist. Since they can't be the objects of perception (failing to both cause and match the perception), the knowledge we have through perception neither vindicates nor denies external things. Perception is entirely internal to the perceiver.
- [00:13:29] His follower **Dharmakīrti** differs on two points. First, where Dignāga might more accurately be called a **phenomenalist**, Dharmakīrti rejects the reality of external things outright, adopting an explicitly **idealist** theory. Second, on error: for Dignāga all perceptual experience is free from illusion, but Dharmakīrti insists accurate perception must be not merely free from conceptual structure but *also free from illusion* — so there can be unconceptualised yet *erroneous* perception.
- [00:14:10] Dharmakīrti gives three examples. (1) **Jaundice**: I see objects with a yellow colour they don't have — a defect of vision, not conceptual construction, since such defects can affect even concept-less small children and animals. (2) The **whirling torch**: someone spins a torch on a rope and I see only a continuous circle of light — pure visual error, because human vision can't distinguish the torch at each point of its trajectory. (3) **Self-motion**: when a vehicle begins to move it can look as if we are stationary and the world is moving. Dharmakīrti's example uses a boat; the modern reader may think of sitting in a train when the train on the adjacent platform appears to slide off when in fact yours is moving. (Faithful listeners may recall a similar boat example in a **14th-century** medieval debate.)
- [00:15:39] So Dharmakīrti adds an extra clause to Dignāga's concept of perception — but at a cost: he can no longer locate *all* error at the conceptual level, and the elegance and explanatory power of Dignāga's theory is compromised, "albeit in the quest for truth."

## Language without universals — the apoha theory
- [00:16:00] Dignāga's nominalism faces an obvious objection: language is full of references to universals, so if there are no universals, how does language latch onto the world? Before Dignāga, philosophers had thought hard about word meaning. Take "the most Indian of examples," the word **cow**. We grasp its meaning despite its varied uses: in "the cow is standing" or "bring me the cow" it seems to stand for a particular individual cow; in "a cow is an animal" or "a cow is shorter than a giraffe" for something generic — a generic cow, or the class of all cows; in "he's playing with cows" (of a child playing with toys) or "this is a cow" (pointing to a picture) for anything with the *shape* of a cow.
- [00:17:04] The great Nyāya thinker **Gautama** concluded that the meaning of "cow" is a *composite* of all three: the individual cow, the cow-shape, and the universal cowhood. Commenting on him, **Vātsyāyana** seems to read this as "cow" being *systematically ambiguous*, taking different meanings in different uses. The later Naiyāyikas **Uddyotakara** and **Jayanta** read the sūtra differently: an utterance of "cow" refers to an individual cow *insofar as* it possesses cowhood — the word has a "deep logical form" meaning roughly "that thing being a cow" (a demonstrative plus a predicate), so "the cow is white" is true so long as the thing picked out is both a cow and white.
- [00:17:55] Dignāga replaces this complicated story with a simple, powerful analysis, given in the fifth chapter of his **Pramāṇa-samuccaya**, drawing on the conceptual *hierarchy* discussed earlier. In any hierarchy concepts are organised by **containment and exclusion**: the concept *colour* contains *white* (anything red is also coloured), while *white* excludes *blue* (nothing is both white and blue). The old answer was that this conceptual hierarchy mirrors a hierarchy of *things in nature* — the world is organised a certain way, our classifications reflect it, and individual words refer to things on the basis of the universals those things instantiate (that thing is a cloud because it instances cloudness, not cowness).
- [00:19:02] Dignāga's alternative is the **apoha** theory — the theory of **exclusion**. Words need not correspond to anything external; their meaning is determined merely by their *location in the conceptual hierarchy*. He compares it to how groups form identity by excluding outsiders: someone may struggle to say what it positively means to be British or American, while being all too ready to exclude others for having the wrong religion, skin colour or accent.
- [00:19:41] So too with everyday words. What exactly is a **cloud**? A "rather nebulous idea," hard to define positively — many shapes, colours, made of water or other vapours. Dignāga simply says "cloud" gets its meaning through its *relations* to other terms: the concept includes cumulus, nimbus and cirrus, is itself included under "meteorological phenomena," and excludes concepts like *giraffe* (even a giraffe-shaped cloud is not really a giraffe). Exclusion also handles compound expressions: "**white cloud**" simply excludes *more* than "cloud" — it excludes both non-clouds and non-white things, whereas "cloud" excludes only non-clouds.
- [00:20:46] The great advantage: Dignāga does without real universals. Applying "cow," we aren't saying a thing instantiates a general feature in the world; we are just *discriminating* it from what is dissimilar — from whatever is *not* a cow. Universals are "concealed **double negations**": to say something is a cow is to say it is *not a non-cow*. Language works by positioning concepts relative to one another.
- [00:21:21] Objection: what about **proper names** — Bessie, Gina, Hiawatha? Dignāga answers that we never apply such names to individual objects of perception (those can't be expressed in language at all); the proper name of a certain cow or giraffe actually refers to a *combination of many individual perceptions*. So "Bessie" is just as much a conceptual construction as the universal "cow."
- [00:21:53] This is another advantage: the theory of language *meshes* with the account of perception. We never reach a concept so fine-grained that it excludes everything except a single particular — particulars are inexpressible and can only be directly perceived, while concepts and words (even proper names) always work at the level of mental construction. We move internally within that conceptual world when we infer that whatever is a cow is not a giraffe though both are animals, or that a white cloud is a cloud. Such movements are the work of inference and reason — Dignāga's logic, the subject of the next episode.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Dignāga** — radically original Buddhist philosopher; argues perception's objects are internal "knowable interior forms," that universals are mind-made, and (via apoha) that words mean by exclusion within a conceptual hierarchy.
- **Pramāṇa-samuccaya** — "collected verses on the sources of knowledge," Dignāga's greatest, mature work; chapter 1 distinguishes perception from inference, chapter 5 gives the apoha theory of language.
- **Ālambana-parīkṣā** — "examination of the foundations of experience," an eight-verse treatise arguing external things cannot be the objects of perception.
- **Hetucakra(ḍamaru)** — "study of the Wheel of Reasons," his short, innovative analysis of fallacious argumentation.
- **ālambana** — the supposed external foundation/ground/objective support of a perceptual experience.
- **pramāṇa** — a source or means of knowledge; Dignāga keeps two (perception, inference) and drops Asaṅga's third (testimony).
- **pratyakṣa (perception)** — grasps the individual/particular; immediate, internal, and (for Dignāga) incapable of error.
- **inference** — grasps the general/universal by exploring relations between mentally constructed concepts arranged in hierarchies.
- **svalakṣaṇa (the particular)** — the unique, momentary, ineffable object of perception; a bare "unclassified flash of awareness," inexpressible in words.
- **direct realism** — the view (held by the Nyāya) that we perceive whole external objects; what Dignāga rejects.
- **Nyāya school / Gautama / Vātsyāyana / Uddyotakara / Jayanta** — the direct realists and their competing analyses of the meaning of "cow" (composite of three; systematic ambiguity; "that thing being a cow").
- **dharmas / atoms / Abhidharma** — the fleeting atomic constituents external-object accounts appeal to; Dignāga shows they can't both cause and match perception.
- **Vasubandhu / Yogācāra** — Dignāga's (possible) teacher and the idealist-leaning Buddhist tradition he develops; Asaṅga is Vasubandhu's brother.
- **apoha (exclusion)** — Dignāga's theory that words mean by their place of inclusion/exclusion among concepts, not by corresponding to real universals; universals are "concealed double negations" (a cow = not a non-cow).
- **Dharmakīrti** — Dignāga's great follower; turns phenomenalism into outright idealism and adds that accurate perception must be free from illusion as well as concepts, conceding non-conceptual error (jaundice, whirling torch, apparent self-motion).

> **In brief:** A survey of how Indian thinkers theorised **kāla** (time): from the awe-inspiring cosmic world-cycles of the Mahābhārata, through the Vedic tradition's tendency to demote time as unreal or merely mental (Bhartṛhari, Sāṃkhya, Yoga), to the lone realist holdout — Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika — which treats both space and time as eternal substances and builds out Praśastapāda's "impetus" (vega) theory of motion.

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Lagadha** — **5th-c. BCE** author who calls astrology "the science of time," the discipline by which "he who knows it knows all" [00:02:00].
- **The Mahābhārata** — where the destructive cosmic world-cycle is thematised and time (**kāla**) becomes "practically synonymous with death" [00:03:55].
- **Bhartṛhari** — grammarian-philosopher whose *Vākyapadīya* gives a broadly Vedāntic account of time as an instrument through which the one brahman manifests as the many [00:06:56].
- **Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika** — the lone *realist* on time and space; treats both as eternal, ubiquitous substances [00:09:48].
- **Praśastapāda** — Vaiśeṣika thinker (**6th c. AD**) who develops the theory of **vega** (impetus) to explain motion [00:15:09].

## Astrology, the "science of time"
- [00:00:37] A historian "should never dismiss as unserious ideas that historical figures took seriously." Take **astrology**: now reduced to newspaper horoscopes printed beside the comic strips, but in former ages "serious business" — a way of legitimising political rule and an important discipline.
- [00:00:59] We now sharply distinguish **astronomy** (a science) from **astrology** (a pseudoscience), but in ancient and medieval societies an expert in one was likely expert in the other — the **2nd-century AD** scientist **Ptolemy** leading the way.
- [00:01:15] Astrology is a star example of ideas travelling between cultures: **Babylonian** astronomical techniques for time-measurement reached Indian astrologers in the **Mauryan** period; **Greek** astrological writing was translated into Sanskrit in the **2nd century AD**; and by the **4th–5th centuries** Indians were even deploying the Greek theory of **epicycles** (planets seated on smaller rotating spheres carried by larger celestial ones).
- [00:01:46] Beyond cultural exchange, astrology "embodies a particular view of the cosmos and our place in it" — it is, among other things, the study of time. The **5th-century BCE** author **Lagadha** saw this: "The Vedas went forth for the sake of the sacrifices. The sacrifices were established as proceeding regularly in time. Therefore, he who knows astrology, this science of time, knows all."
- [00:02:17] His point: astronomical measurements fixed the **auspicious moment** for Vedic rituals. It is by studying the motions of the stars that Indians — and we — divide time; the days and years of our lives are "astronomically defined."

## Cosmic world-cycles: kalpa, yuga, and a day in the life of Brahmā
- [00:02:40] The ancient Indian scientists didn't stop at days and years — they envisioned vastly larger spans: "the day in the life of God, or **Brahmā**." This brings us to a notorious feature of Indian thought, the **cosmic world-cycle**.
- [00:02:57] Terms like **kalpa** or **yuga** carry the vague sense of "extremely long periods of time with which the Indians measure the age of the universe." **Kalpa** — a word already in **Aśoka**'s inscriptions, meaning simply "a very long time" — became the name for one **Brahmā-day**.
- [00:03:18] Indian deities had to be patient: recall **Indra** waiting **101 years** for instruction from **Prajāpati**. But that is nothing beside Brahmā: his single day lasts the whole cosmic cycle, reckoned at **4.32 billion years**, and he lives for **72,000** such days — "you're going to need a calculator with an extra-wide screen." Each of his days sees the cosmos created, endure, and finally be destroyed.

## Time as destroyer: kāla becomes death
- [00:03:55] The idea of a *destructive* world-cycle does not yet appear in the early Vedas but is thematised in the **Mahābhārata**. The expert **Luis González Reimann** notes that by the end of the Vedic period and the composition of the Mahābhārata, "the importance of time as a destructive force had reached new heights," and the word **kāla** ("time") "had practically become synonymous with death."
- [00:04:28] Another pessimistic idea from the Mahābhārata: each world-cycle subdivides into **four periods of unequal length**, marked by gradual degradation and loss of **dharma** — shown, for instance, in ever-shorter human lifespans. The four sub-periods, or **yugas**, are named after four unequally valuable throws in a game of **dice**.
- [00:04:51] Depressingly, we now live in the age of the least valuable throw — the **Kali** — which is why ours is the **Kali Yuga**, with an ideal lifespan of "a mere 100 years."
- [00:05:04] How close is the end? Views varied. Astrologers dated the most recent great **conjunction of the stars** that began the Kali Yuga to **3102 BC**, leaving us "hundreds of thousands of years" to go. But other texts predict a more **imminent** cosmic demise — perhaps reflecting the destabilising **foreign invasions** of India between the **Mauryan** and **Gupta** empires.
- [00:05:41] This awe-inspiring conception of cosmic time forms the background to **karmic retribution and reincarnation**: "if you escape just punishment for your misdeeds, it won't be for lack of time." Even the gods are subject to time, living through daily cycles with cosmic genesis and destruction correlated to dawn and dusk.

## Is time itself a divine, creative principle?
- [00:06:08] That the gods themselves live by cosmic days might suggest time is a divine principle, "or even a principle beyond divinity." But that notion finds full expression only in one unusual passage of the **Atharva-Veda**, where all things — including the creator god **Prajāpati** — are originated by **time itself**.
- [00:06:24] It is not a doctrine that caught on. One of the **Upaniṣads** explicitly rejects the claim that time is a creative principle, and the Vedic philosophical tradition goes further in this "metaphysical demotion of time" — by doubting whether it is **real at all**.

## Doubting time's reality: Bhartṛhari's Vākyapadīya
- [00:06:43] We might expect such doubt in **Vedānta**, especially its **Advaita** variety, where everything other than **brahman** is exposed as illusion. But one of the more interesting treatments, from a broadly Vedānta standpoint, doesn't go quite that far: a section of the *Vākyapadīya* by the grammarian-philosopher **Bhartṛhari**.
- [00:07:06] Bhartṛhari introduces time at the very outset of his treatise, explaining that it **conditions** the appearance of the one brahman as many things subject to growth and decay. On this reckoning, time is "not exactly unreal," but a **mere instrument or power** through which brahman makes itself manifest as our changing, multifarious world. So all phenomenal things are "bound to time, like birds held captive by strings."
- [00:07:40] Still, from the standpoint of brahman — and of the Vedānta philosopher — there is **no real difference** between past, present, and future. As usual in Sanskrit grammar, Bhartṛhari puts **action** at the centre: the three **tenses** are "nothing but an attitude we take towards actions." Past tense marks actions already performed, present tense actions mid-completion, future tense actions not yet undertaken.

## Time as a product of mind: Sāṃkhya and Yoga
- [00:08:16] Other Vedic thinkers are even more sceptical. **Sāṃkhya** and **Yoga** in particular claim time is "merely a product of our minds." The **Yoga-Sūtra** speaks obscurely of a knowledge arising "from our awareness of moments and succession."
- [00:08:31] Fortunately the commentary explains: a **moment** (kṣaṇa) is the smallest part of time, "just as an atom is a smallest part of matter." We mentally combine moments into an impression of flowing, continuous time — but time is "nothing real out in the world." Most of us see only from the perspective of the present moment, and so fail to realise that **past and future are also real**, even if only present events are manifest.
- [00:08:59] The **Sāṃkhya** commentators take their cue from a sūtra of the *Sāṃkhya-kārikā*: the **external senses grasp only the present**, whereas the **mental organ** can also grasp past and future. **Gauḍapāda** explains that the mind can *infer* past and future from present perceptions — the familiar examples: realising it must have **rained** because the river is swollen, or that it **will rain** because ants are carrying their eggs to safety.

## The realist holdout: Vaiśeṣika on space
- [00:09:34] So far the ancient Indians seem impressed by the immensity of time, "but not so impressed that they were willing to admit that time is actually real." But we haven't yet considered the Vedic school "most notable for its realist approach," **Vaiśeṣika**. Having defended controversial items like qualities, universals, and the self, **Kaṇāda** is "not likely to adopt an anti-realist theory of time."
- [00:10:01] His strategy in the **Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra** is previewed by how he handles **space**, which (no surprise) he also takes to be real. Space, like the self, **cannot be apprehended directly**; we infer its existence by observing things that are spatially arranged. And relative arrangement, Kaṇāda assumes, is best explained in terms of **contact**.
- [00:10:33] The example: a **young couple** holding hands — the woman is to the man's left because he holds her right hand; their relative position is secured by direct physical contact. But picture them years later, now with their **child** in the middle: the mother is again to the father's left, yet she is no longer in contact with him (she holds the child's hand). What fixes their relative position now?
- [00:11:03] Kaṇāda's answer is **still contact** — not between the parents, nor indirectly via the child, but the contact each parent has with **space itself**. Space provides the framework within which the woman can be to the left of the man. For this to work, space must be the sort of thing one can contact — which means it must be a **substance**: a unique, ubiquitous, and **eternal** substance, an instrumental cause of every physical effect, grounding the two spatial relations of **nearness and farness**. "It's thanks to the reality of space that we can say… the mother is to the father's left, or Germany is to the east of the United Kingdom."
- [00:11:59] How big is space? Kaṇāda is not entirely clear. Since space is introduced to explain relations among physical objects, it might be enough for it to be just the **size of the cosmos** — that is where all the objects are. Yet Kaṇāda also says most bodies are "long or short," which only makes sense if they have parts bounding them at the edges; space has **no parts** and hence no boundary, so how can it have a determinate size? For this reason some later Vaiśeṣikas conclude space is **limited** in extent, while others grant it a spatial dimension they call **paramadīrgha**, "maximal length."

## Vaiśeṣika on time — and the problem of the present moment
- [00:12:59] Kaṇāda's view of **time** closely mirrors his view of space: time too is a **substance** — unique, eternal, ubiquitous, an instrumental cause of every effect. Just as space grounds relative judgments of position, time grounds relative judgments about **past, present, and future**: it is thanks to time that we can say "I lived in the United Kingdom before I lived in Germany."
- [00:13:31] One difference, though: what is spatially near or far **varies from person to person**, but what is temporally near or far is the **same for everyone**. We are all "right now in contact with the same present moment," whereas each of us contacts a different region of space.
- [00:13:49] Unfortunately the **present moment** turns out to be problematic. In the **Nyāya-Sūtra**, **Gautama** reports an argument against present time "strikingly reminiscent" of the paradox of the pre-Socratic **Zeno**. Imagine an object **falling** toward the ground: divide its path into the part already traversed and the part not yet traversed. Together these make up the whole path, measured out by past and future time — so there is **no part of the trajectory left** for the present moment. The present could only be an **imaginary point** dividing elapsed time from time not yet elapsed.
- [00:14:32] Gautama replies that past and future are relative to the **present**, not to one another. He may mean that even if the present is only a **boundary**, it can still be real — "how could the present moment divide past from future if it were imaginary?" He also notes that if there were no present moment, **nothing could be known through the senses**, since the external senses grasp things only in the present.

## Motion and impetus: Praśastapāda's theory of vega
- [00:15:02] As the falling object shows, debates about time are "inextricably bound up" with debates about **motion**. Beginning with **Praśastapāda**, early Vaiśeṣika philosophers took a serious interest in projectiles and moving bodies — and again there's a striking parallel with Greek thought: his ideas are comparable to the celebrated theory of **impetus** in a near-contemporary, **John Philoponus**. Both lived in the **6th century AD** ("unless, of course, time is unreal").
- [00:15:41] For Praśastapāda, **motion** is the cause of conjunction and disjunction, and comes in varieties each explained by a **quality** of the moving body: **heaviness** causes downward motion, **fluidity** causes flowing motion, and so on. Insisting that motions are caused by qualities and **not by prior motions** avoids an **infinite regress** in which every motion needs a previous one.
- [00:16:10] To explain why motion *continues* once started, Praśastapāda invokes a quality called **vega** — impetus or speed: a tendency or disposition of the body, comparable to **elasticity**. Impetus explains why falling things keep falling; elasticity, why bodies snap back to their original shape after being stretched.
- [00:16:41] First illustration — **a fruit falling from a tree**. While on the branch the fruit has the quality of **weight**, but it is counteracted by contact with the tree via its stem. The stem breaks; no longer impeded, weight makes the fruit move to a new position — in Kaṇāda's terms, from contacting one part of the substance of space to contacting the part just below. But weight only **initiates** the fall. To explain what happens next, we say that at that same moment an **impetus** is produced in the fruit, causing further downward motion, and so on for every subsequent moment of the fall.
- [00:17:38] This is why **vega** is best translated "impetus," not confused with **inertia**: the point is not that a moving body simply continues unless impeded, but that there is an **internally impressed quality** in the falling fruit — just like the internal elasticity that makes a rubber ball spring back after being squeezed.

## The javelin, and the limits of the theory
- [00:18:04] Praśastapāda's second example, the **javelin**, is interesting because it moves **despite** its weight (the fruit moved *because* of weight). The throw gives the javelin both an initial **motion** and an initial **impetus**; the impetus counteracts its weight so it can keep travelling **upward**. Eventually the impetus is exhausted through **contact with the air**, weight takes over and imparts a **downward** impetus, and the javelin falls — now "behaving like a particularly long, sharp piece of fruit."
- [00:18:45] Thrown at an angle, it also moves **horizontally**; its continued flight is caused by the impetus imparted by the thrower, depleted by air contact. "The harder you throw, the more impetus you impart" — which determines how far it travels: enough impetus, and "you just might win an Olympic medal."
- [00:19:10] Later Vaiśeṣikas extend the theory to other motions — an **iron needle** toward a **magnet**, the **upward flickering of flames**, the **circulation of air** — assuming in each case some quality that initiates motion. But weight, fluidity, and elasticity don't fit these, so they resort to **adṛṣṭa**, literally "the unseen," postulated to account for such movements.
- [00:19:43] This "may look more like magical thinking than scientific explanation," but we could read it as "admirably open-minded": realist as ever, Vaiśeṣika assumes there is some **genuine property** in things making them behave as they do, even if we can't yet identify it — and empirical investigation may reveal more. The worry is that **adṛṣṭa** later assumes more speculative roles (explaining why atoms start moving at the creation of the universe, or our fate in the afterlife) — developments "the down-to-earth Praśastapāda would probably not have approved."

## A naturalistic school — and where we go next
- [00:20:27] Despite such developments, it's fair to call Vaiśeṣika in general **down-to-earth**, or better, **naturalistic**: it offers accounts of the physical world and the human place in it while "avoiding appeals to supernatural forces." **God is given no significant explanatory role**, at least in early Vaiśeṣika.
- [00:20:49] But if you like naturalism, an even more appealing school is coming. The groups examined so far all gave **some** role to non-physical things — gods, brahman, the human self or mind. Next on the agenda is a group "who shocked their contemporaries by banning **all** supernatural, non-physical entities from their worldview": the **Cārvāka** school, who traced their philosophy to a figure from the time of the Buddha named **Pāyāsi**. "It's only natural that you should join us to hear all about him."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **kāla** — time; in the Mahābhārata it becomes "practically synonymous with death," a destructive force.
- **kalpa** — a "very long time" (word already in Aśoka's inscriptions); the length of one **Brahmā-day**, the full cosmic cycle (≈ **4.32 billion years**).
- **yuga** — one of four world-ages of unequal length, named after dice-throws; ours is the **Kali Yuga**, the worst, dated from **3102 BC**.
- **Lagadha** — **5th-c. BCE** author who called astrology "the science of time" — knowing it, "he who knows… knows all."
- **Atharva-Veda** — contains the unusual passage in which all things, including Prajāpati, are originated by **time itself** (a doctrine the Upaniṣads reject).
- **Bhartṛhari** — grammarian-philosopher; in the *Vākyapadīya* time is not unreal but an **instrument** through which the one brahman manifests as the changing many; the three tenses are just attitudes toward **action**.
- **Sāṃkhya & Yoga** — hold time is a **product of the mind**; the *Yoga-Sūtra* commentary defines a **moment (kṣaṇa)** as the smallest part of time, atom-like, combined mentally into apparent continuous flow.
- **Gauḍapāda** — Sāṃkhya commentator: the senses grasp only the present, but the mind **infers** past and future (swollen river → it rained; ants moving eggs → it will rain).
- **Vaiśeṣika** — the realist school: treats both **space** and **time** as unique, eternal, ubiquitous **substances**, instrumental causes of every effect.
- **Kaṇāda** — author of the *Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra*; grounds spatial relations (left/right, near/far) in **contact with space-as-substance** (the couple-and-child example).
- **paramadīrgha** — "maximal length," the spatial dimension some later Vaiśeṣikas attribute to space when debating its size.
- **Gautama** — in the *Nyāya-Sūtra* reports (and answers) a **Zeno-like** argument that the present moment is merely an imaginary boundary between past and future.
- **Praśastapāda** — Vaiśeṣika thinker (**6th c. AD**, contemporary of **John Philoponus**); explains continued motion by the quality **vega** (impetus), illustrated by the falling fruit and the thrown javelin.
- **vega** — impetus: an internally impressed quality that keeps a body moving; distinct from inertia.
- **adṛṣṭa** — "the unseen"; postulated to explain motions (magnet, flame, air) not covered by ordinary qualities — open-minded realism, or magical thinking.
- **Cārvāka / Pāyāsi** — the materialist school (next episode) that bans all non-physical entities, traced to **Pāyāsi**, a figure from the time of the Buddha.

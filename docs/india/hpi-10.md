> **In brief:** What kind of thing is the Buddha's teaching? Through the Buddha's own images of the raft and the poisonous snake, the episode probes whether his dharma is only instrumentally valuable (and so indifferent to truth), how this bears on lying, silence, and compassion, and why the value of both teaching and virtue depends on how far along the path one has travelled.

## Recap: a journey without a traveller, but a journey nonetheless
- [00:00:36] Last time covered what the Buddha bequeathed to later Indian philosophy. With his doctrine of **no self**, the Buddha rejected the Upaniṣadic search for the self as misguided — the century-and-more the god **Indra** spent on that search was a waste of time, since what he sought never existed.
- [00:01:01] Yet with the **four noble truths** the Buddha implicitly affirmed the Upaniṣadic conception of philosophy as being about **path and purpose**. There may be no journey of self-discovery, but there is a journey nonetheless. Its destination is **nirvāṇa**, the extinction of attachment and desire.
- [00:01:25] So the function of the Buddha's teaching is much like that of the Upaniṣads: to help the student along a path of **inner conceptual development**. The Buddha was at pains to make his disciples grasp this, which is why he offered two analogies for the nature of his own philosophy — a **raft** and a **poisonous snake**.

## The raft: teaching you cross over on, then leave behind
- [00:01:43] A man builds a raft to get from a dangerous place to a place of safety. Having crossed, he thinks: this raft was so helpful, suppose I hoist it on my head and carry it wherever I go. The Buddha's point: the **dharma** is like the raft — for the purpose of crossing over, not for holding onto once you are on the other side.
- [00:02:14] Hence the startling instruction: "when you know the dharma to be similar to a raft, you should abandon even the teachings. How much more so things contrary to the teachings?" Rafts are great — but only while crossing a river; once that condition is no longer met, the value disappears and the raft becomes an **encumbrance**. Attachment in any shape is a cause of suffering, and that applies even to being attached to the truth.

## The problem: is the teaching only *instrumentally* valuable?
- [00:03:04] Charming as it is, the analogy brings a problem. It appears to make the Buddha's teachings **merely instrumentally valuable** — useful because they get us where we want to go, and for that reason only, which would mean it makes no difference whether they are even **true**.
- [00:03:21] After all, beliefs don't have to be true to be useful — which is why it is the rare parent who never misleads their children (Santa's lump of coal; "everything will be all right" when sadly it won't). On this reading the **Brahminical theory of the unchanging self** might even be *true* after all; it's just that believing it brings suffering, so we are better off convinced it is false.
- [00:03:52] Worse: does the Buddha *know* his teachings could be untrue? If so, he isn't merely embroidering a useful story — he is telling a **lie**, albeit a helpful one. Surely that is not the lesson of the raft.

## The Abhaya dialogue and the missing "white lie"
- [00:04:16] To clarify the status of the teachings, turn to the dialogue with **Prince Abhaya**, who asks whether it is proper to utter speech that would be unwelcome or disagreeable to others. The Buddha says he can give no straightforward answer.
- [00:04:35] He asks Abhaya what he would do if he saw a child put a pebble in its mouth; the prince says he would remove it even at the cost of hurting the child. The Buddha then lists the kinds of speech: he would *not* say what is untrue, unbeneficial, and unpleasant; nor what is true but not beneficial; but out of compassion he *would* speak what is truthful, beneficial, and agreeable — and would know the right time to say it.
- [00:05:03] There is a **glaring omission**: speech that is beneficial *without* being true. This is no oversight — the case is awkward. The Buddha clearly doesn't think lying is always wrong (else why mention the *unbeneficial* lie but not the *beneficial* one?).
- [00:05:27] The Sri Lankan scholar **K. N. Jayatilleke** speculates that for the Buddha there simply are no such cases. The **Pāli** term for "beneficial," *attha*, means what is morally good in the sense of being **useful for attaining nirvāṇa**; and since asserting a falsehood was held to be a **moral evil**, it would be logically or causally impossible for something false (i.e. morally evil) to produce something morally advantageous. On this reading there are **no white lies, no beneficial untruths** — and indeed the Buddha is said to have discouraged his disciples from lying without exception for expedient cases.

## The tradition flirts back: the burning house
- [00:06:15] Nonetheless the Buddhist tradition kept flirting with **compassionate lying**. In the later **Lotus Sūtra**, a father gets his children to leave a burning house by promising there are toys outside. Afterward the Buddha asks his interlocutor **Śāriputra**: was the father guilty of falsehood?
- [00:06:40] Śāriputra answers no — the rich man simply made it possible for his sons to escape the fire and preserve their lives; since they survived, they had already obtained "a plaything of sorts," and all the more so since by **expedient means** they were rescued. And the Buddha agrees. So in yet another sign of the tradition's diversity, we find **conflicting messages** about the well-intentioned lie.

## The other end of the raft: looking back from enlightenment
- [00:07:13] A further issue: so far we have asked about the teaching's status for someone **mid-crossing**. But pose the same question from the standpoint of someone who has **already crossed over**. As with the paradoxical desire to lose all desire (which can be resolved by supposing that desire drops away once nirvāṇa is reached), the raft seems to say the teaching itself can be **set aside** once it has served its purpose.
- [00:07:55] This recalls **Wittgenstein**, who told us to see the propositions of his *Tractatus* as a **ladder** to throw away once we have understood. Another Buddhist image: instruction is like a **medicine** that purges our ignorance but is then expelled along with whatever it purged. This seems inevitable once we accept that philosophy's purpose is to bring us to happiness, or release from suffering — for what guarantee is there that the beliefs that make you happy are the ones that are *true*?
- [00:08:39] But remember: the Buddha traces **suffering to attachment, attachment to desire, and desire to ignorance**. He presupposes that we are freed from our predicament precisely by **understanding that predicament more fully**. To this extent the Buddha is a **bringer of truth**, even if not of straightforward doctrine. The process of enlightenment is **therapeutic**, and may eventually render the teaching otiose — but it begins by **dispelling falsehood**.
- [00:09:21] So the Buddha may, after all, hold that the truth will set you free — but not **all at once**. If you heard last episode you already know the four noble truths and should be within "touching distance" of enlightenment; yet it takes lengthy, perhaps lifelong training to give up desire and attachment. **Understanding** the four truths is the easy part (we did it in one episode); **living accordingly** is the hard part.

## The snake: take the teaching by the head, not the tail
- [00:10:01] The second analogy makes this point. To catch a poisonous snake you must take it carefully by the **head**, not the tail — so too the Buddha's words must be grasped the right way. "This is a philosophy that **bites**."
- [00:10:24] Some misguided men learn the dharma but do not inquire into its meaning; they learn it only to **criticize others and win debates**, and so do not experience the good for which it was meant. Wrongly grasped, the teachings conduce to their harm and suffering — just as a man seeking a snake who grabs its **coils or tail** gets bitten and may die "because of his wrong grasp of the snake."
- [00:11:19] The Buddha's warning echoes Jack Nicholson in *A Few Good Men*: "you can't handle the truth." These students expect the wrong thing — they think learning exists to make you **seem learned**, that knowledge is shown by winning arguments. They treat the teaching as instrumental too, but as an instrument toward the **wrong goal**: victory and esteem — ironically the very desires the teaching tells them to abandon.
- [00:12:00] To approach it rightly, we must be open to its power to **change our goals and desires**, not hope it will satisfy the goals and desires we already have.

## When to stay silent: the four kinds of question
- [00:12:11] What should a good teacher do with students who aren't ready? Lying is problematic; another option is to **remain silent** — an option the Buddha often chose. Not because he was bested in argument (as happens in the **Upaniṣads**, where a character often falls silent in defeat), but because he knows **when speaking the truth will be beneficial and when it won't**.
- [00:12:45] In the **Milinda-pañha (Questions of Menander)**, King **Menander** points to an apparent contradiction: the Buddha is said to keep nothing hidden (unlike teachers who keep things "hidden in their fists"), yet he habitually refused to answer questions. So did he teach openly or not?
- [00:13:13] **Nāgasena** replies that there are **four sorts of question**: some call for a straightforward response, some for further clarification, some should elicit a counter-question, and some should be met with **silence**. This makes sense — if asked whether the colour blue is heavier than the number four, you couldn't reasonably give a simple answer; you might respond with another question ("what makes you think colours and numbers have weight?").
- [00:13:47] When is it right to refuse to respond at all? Nāgasena says "there is no utterance or speech of the Buddhas that is without reason, without cause." So it is up to the **interlocutor to deserve an answer** — to be in a position where some answer would be productive. Confronted by someone who only wants to win debates or boast of his enlightenment, the Buddha offers **stony silence** rather than pearls of wisdom.

## Compassion: the Buddha's hallmark — and a puzzle
- [00:14:17] This carefully calibrated strategy may look like extreme **tough love**, but it is love nonetheless. **Compassion** is not just part of the Buddha's teaching, it is his hallmark — it is what distinguished him from others who achieved enlightenment, for he was not content to be liberated himself but laid down a teaching by which **all humankind** can be freed from suffering.
- [00:14:46] But here is a puzzle: the Buddha says we escape suffering by **giving up attachment** — why should that have anything to do with compassion? A person who abandons desire would refrain from much wrongdoing (why kill out of vengeance, or steal, if you crave neither?). But compassion is not merely avoiding misdeeds; it means **actively pursuing the welfare of others**. Is the Buddhist like someone who confuses medicine's first rule, "do no harm," with the whole of medicine?
- [00:15:20] Certainly not. Buddhist ethics emphasizes **skillful action** — action born of a proper understanding of things — and claims it will be morally upright and compassionate. This is enshrined in the **eightfold path**, which *begins* with **right understanding** but does not end there: one must pursue right speech, right action, and so on up to right mindfulness and concentration. Taking the right view (rejecting a stable unchanging self, seeing that attachment leads to suffering) is only the **first step**; living by it takes long training — which is why **monasticism**, searching self-criticism, and strict discipline are so central.

## Why behave well? Reincarnation, motive, and the wrong kind of reason
- [00:16:21] So good behaviour is crucial — but why, and how does it free us from suffering? One prominent answer: **bad deeds doom you to a worse next life**. Great stress falls on action that is **puṇya**, karmically fruitful; a **good next life**, rather than total liberation, is the goal of ordinary Buddhists — a feasible goal that gives them a reason to behave well.
- [00:16:50] But that looks like the **wrong sort of reason**. I should not show compassion for my own (or my future self's) sake — not just because there is no self, but because compassion should be directed at the good of those for whom I feel it. Indeed the Buddha warns that a truly good action must spring from the **right motive**, and that desire for a favourable reincarnation, like all desire, would inevitably lead to suffering. If promises and threats about the next life play a role, it may again be like the **raft**: useful for those still making progress, not for those who have arrived.

## Is compassion itself a raft to discard?
- [00:17:34] A disquieting thought: might **compassion itself** be a raft or ladder discarded on arrival? Surely benevolence should characterize the perfect sage, not be left behind. Yet Buddhist texts sometimes suggest exactly this — the **Arhat**, one who has achieved full enlightenment, is said to have moved beyond puṇya and to be "no longer concerned with virtue."
- [00:18:04] On its face this clashes with the Buddha's own zeal to help others. But perhaps such passages mean only that the Arhat is no longer concerned with action **insofar as it bears on favourable reincarnation** (since he desires nothing). Or that he need not **deliberate**, having reached a state where he just naturally acts well. Or that virtues like compassion **take a different shape** depending on how far along the path one is.

## Attachment's positive flip side, and the conditional value of truth
- [00:18:38] To see why the Arhat would in fact act well, return to **giving up attachment** — which we have been treating purely negatively. To abandon greed would mean never acting from greed. But the opposite of greed is not the mere *absence* of greed; it is **generosity**. The Buddhist rejection of envy, hostility, and greed is meant to bring about **positive states of mind** opposed to these sources of suffering. Someone with no desires, not even committed to the permanence of his own self, will be far more inclined to **share whatever he has**.
- [00:19:20] This is the moral counterpart of the Buddha's gift of insight: banishing ignorance is not only giving up false beliefs but **replacing them with positive true beliefs**. And just as the Arhat is ultimately freed even from the exertion the rest of us put into being good, so the **significance and value of the Buddha's teachings depend on what stage of the path one has reached**.
- [00:19:50] Denying that the value of truth is *unconditional* should not be taken to mean the truth has value only if some condition is met and otherwise none. Rather, **what value the truth has is dependent on what condition is met** — the truth has **different values in different conditions**.

## Where we go next
- [00:20:08] One may still object that this ethics is **too centered on the individual** — the Buddha tells us much about how each person should and shouldn't live. But does Buddhism also say how we should **live together** — does it offer any **political** ideas beyond the rules of monastic life? The answer is yes.
- [00:20:38] Next time turns to a figure who can fairly be called a **philosopher king** — the legendary **Mauryan** emperor **Aśoka** — examining his attempt to rule as a Buddhist and comparing his ideas to the ancient Indian political treatise, the **Arthaśāstra**.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **dharma** — the Buddha's teaching; likened to a raft and a snake, its value tied to the purpose it serves.
- **the raft analogy** — teaching is for crossing over to safety, not to carry afterward; raises the worry that the teaching is only instrumentally valuable, indifferent to truth.
- **the poisonous snake analogy** — the teaching must be grasped "by the head"; those who learn it only to win debates "grasp it by the tail" and are bitten.
- **nirvāṇa** — the destination of the Buddhist path; the extinction of attachment and desire. The **four noble truths** are easy to *understand* but hard to *live*: understanding is the first step, lifelong training the rest.
- **Prince Abhaya dialogue** — the Buddha lists kinds of speech but pointedly omits speech that is beneficial-yet-untrue.
- **K. N. Jayatilleke** — scholar who argues the omission is principled: since *attha* (beneficial) means useful for nirvāṇa and falsehood is a moral evil, a beneficial untruth is impossible — no white lies.
- **attha (Pāli)** — "beneficial," i.e. morally good in the sense of conducive to attaining nirvāṇa.
- **Lotus Sūtra / burning house** — later text where a father lures children from a burning house with a promised toy; Śāriputra and the Buddha excuse it as expedient means — the tradition flirting with compassionate lying.
- **Milinda-pañha (Questions of Menander)** — King Menander asks how the Buddha can both hide nothing and refuse to answer; **Nāgasena** replies with the four kinds of question, the fourth answered by silence (his response to those who don't deserve or can't benefit from an answer — unlike the Upaniṣadic silence of one defeated in debate).
- **compassion** — the Buddha's hallmark, distinguishing him from other enlightened beings; not mere avoidance of harm but active pursuit of others' welfare, expressed through **skillful action** born of proper understanding.
- **eightfold path** — begins with right understanding and proceeds through right speech, action, mindfulness, and concentration; right view is only the first step.
- **puṇya** — karmically fruitful action earning a better rebirth; the ordinary Buddhist's goal, but the "wrong sort of reason" for genuine compassion.
- **Arhat** — one fully enlightened, said to have moved beyond puṇya and concern with virtue; the puzzle of whether compassion is itself a "raft" left behind.
- **conditional value of truth** — the truth's value is not unconditional but varies with one's condition; it has different values at different stages of the path.

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Kaṇāda** — the Vaiśeṣika founder; a metaphysical realist about substances, properties, universals — and about complex wholes [00:02:44].
- **The Buddhist anti-realists** — who treat chariots, books and persons as mental fabrications/constructions, not real wholes [00:03:55].
- **Bimal Matilal** — modern scholar cited: Indian philosophy grew out of the cosmogonic speculations of the Vedas and Upaniṣads [00:11:55].
- **Dharmakīrti** — late-**6th-century** Buddhist whose multicoloured-cloth argument attacks the reality of wholes [00:16:57].
- **Vātsyāyana** — paired with Kaṇāda as defending common sense with highly theoretical tools [00:20:38].

## More than the sum of its parts
- [00:00:37] The hook: you sit through an incoherent film and tell a friend "that wasn't much more than the sum of its parts." Entertainment should be more than an unconnected series of celebrity cameos or action scenes. *Chariots of Fire* becomes a classic not just for a good theme song and the beach-running opening, but for a well-structured, coherent narrative and characters whose development we follow. Coherence and structure give rise to *something new*: they produce **wholes** that are genuinely more than the sum of their parts. (Quentin Tarantino is the running counter-joke — success without that kind of coherence.)
- [00:01:29] If that's true of *Chariots of Fire*, it's true of plain old chariots. A chariot is more than a heap of axles, wheels and carriages lying around the chariot-making factory. Only once the parts are assembled into an organized structure does the chariot become a chariot — and thanks to that structure it can do what a mere heap cannot: carry **Arjuna** into war against his foes on the battlefield of **Kurukṣetra**.

## Wholes, and the self that says "I"
- [00:02:08] What goes for the chariot goes for the charioteer. A person is not a random sequence of individual thoughts and feelings but an organized unity of cognition and conception that persists over time. That is why a person can say "I" — "I saw *Chariots of Fire* last night, and I still can't get the theme song out of my head." Using "I" presupposes that experience is not a stream of disunited impressions (first the watching, later the remembering) but the experience of an enduring, united self.
- [00:02:44] This is the view of the **Vaiśeṣika** philosopher **Kaṇāda**. As established last time, he's a metaphysical realist about substances, their properties, and the universals applying to them — and he's *also* a realist about complex things. Over and above an aggregate or collection of parts there is a further object: a **whole** (*avayavin*). Wholes include the middle-sized objects of ordinary experience — the book in your hands is more than pages, covers and spine; the chariot you ride is more than axles, wheels and carriage.
- [00:03:26] Though he probably didn't know he was agreeing with Kaṇāda, **Bertrand Russell** put it nicely: a whole is "a new single term distinct from each of its parts and from all of them; it is one, not many, and it is related to the parts, but has a being distinct from theirs." Vaiśeṣika thus agrees with both Russell and common sense — "an unbeatable combination."

## The Buddhist opponent
- [00:03:55] But it faces a formidable opponent: Buddhist philosophers who take books and chariots — the objects of everyday experience — to be fabrications or constructions of our minds. They give two lines of argument. First, the concepts of things like chariots or books are built from nothing more than momentary sensory experiences; if so, there need be no persisting chariot, only a stream of fleeting impressions. Second, they doubt there's any single enduring person experiencing these impressions in sequence — a central Buddhist tenet is that there is **no enduring self**, and giving up the illusion of the self is a step on the road to liberation.
- [00:04:49] Vaiśeṣika's obvious rejoinder: if a chariot is not really a whole but just a bunch of parts, what's the difference between the chariot and a *random grab-bag* — say, the collection whose members are Gina the crocodile, an old videotape of *Chariots of Fire*, and the moon? Three things that even more obviously than the latest Tarantino effort fail to form a single coherent whole. On the Buddhist view such a random assortment is no different from a chariot — which seems hard to believe — so Vaiśeṣika concludes there's a real **metaphysical difference**: the assortment is no true whole; the chariot (or charioteer) is one real thing made of parts.
- [00:05:36] The Buddhists answer that we distinguish the two cases merely by **convention**. Human concerns and interests make it more useful to speak of a chariot than of a crocodile-plus-videotape-plus-moon, but neither "whole" is any more real than the other. Still, you don't have to beat Russell to feel Vaiśeṣika has the better of it so far: the Buddhists banish familiar objects and persons from reality, while Vaiśeṣika validates our everyday assumptions — so the burden of proof seems to lie with the Buddhists.

## Buddhist arguments for anti-realism
- [00:06:20] Realizing this, the Buddhists offer several arguments. At least some everyday objects really *are* mere sums of parts: a forest is nothing more than a bunch of trees, an army just a bunch of soldiers. And *Chariots of Fire* itself — however coherent its narrative — is actually just a series of images shown in rapid succession to produce the illusion of continuity; on a digital screen it's built from individual pixels of light that cohere into one image, like an Impressionist painting seen from a distance. Some apparent wholes are plainly assemblages whose unity is illusion or convention — so why not suspect the same in other cases?
- [00:07:25] Vaiśeṣika is *especially* vulnerable here, because the school holds that familiar objects like crocodiles and chariots are actually collections of **atoms** (*paramāṇu*). So why not say the crocodile or chariot is just an illusion created by the conjunction of atoms? Reply: Vaiśeṣika is not the reductionist sort of atomist. Individual atoms are too small to be perceived, so when I look at a crocodile I am *not* seeing a vast number of atoms and combining my experience of them into one alarmingly large animal — I cannot see the atoms at all. Therefore the crocodile in front of me must be a real thing over and above the atoms; if the only real things present were atoms, there would be nothing I could perceive.

## The inherence dilemma
- [00:08:25] Another Buddhist argument targets the relation between whole and parts. Kaṇāda's school says a whole **inheres** in its parts (loosely: resides or occurs in them) — a book resides in its pages and cover. The Buddhist forces a dilemma: does the *entire* book reside in each page? That seems impossible — if the book is entirely in page 56 it can't also be entirely in page 57, or it'd be in two places at once. So the book must reside only *partly* in each page — but then the whole is divided into as many bits as it has parts, exactly contradicting the realist's claim that the whole is a unity over and above its parts.
- [00:09:21] To escape, Vaiśeṣika must clarify what it means by **inherence** (*samavāya*). Take first the inherence of a universal in its particulars. Vaiśeṣika is convinced the universal nature *crocodile* is real — but its reality depends on there being actual particular crocodiles like Gina; remove her and all other crocodiles and the universal is gone too. The universal is *inseparable from its instances* — unlike the transcendent Forms of **Plato**, more like the *immanent* universals of the **Aristotelians**.
- [00:10:01] The whole–part case is similar but not identical. The chariot is more than axle, wheels and carriage yet couldn't exist if those parts didn't. But universals are taken to be **eternal and uncreated**: they depend on particular instances, yet there are always *some* instances around, so the universal never vanishes — the nature *crocodile* is untroubled by individual crocodiles dying, as long as others are born. Not so for wholes: a book, a videotape, or sadly a crocodile, all break up and disappear. So Vaiśeṣika treats the parts of a whole as a special kind of cause — a **substrate** — the difference being that a universal can survive when its instances don't, whereas a whole cannot survive without its particular parts surviving. Despite this, both universals and wholes stand in a relation of **inherence** to their particulars/parts, and both are subject to a special kind of **causation**: instances cause the universal, parts cause the whole.

## Causation: the running theme of Indian philosophy
- [00:11:43] This idea of inherence feeds a long-running Indian debate about **causation** — present throughout the whole podcast series. The earliest cosmological speculations in Vedic literature were fundamentally an inquiry into causes. As **Bimal Matilal** pointed out, philosophical activity in India arose out of the cosmogonic speculations of the **Vedas** and **Upaniṣads**: the all-important business of philosophy was to find some simple unitary cause for the origin of this complex universe.
- [00:12:13] The Sanskrit grammar tradition had causal reflection at its heart too: **Pāṇini's** system of **kārakas**, the relations between verb and noun, rests on a causal model in which the verb designates an event and the nouns contribute to bringing it about — as agent, as instrument, and so on.
- [00:12:39] The same idea informs Indian epistemology and the theory of **pramāṇas** — the processes that *cause* an event of awareness and knowledge. In **Nyāya** epistemology four pramāṇas are recognized — perception, inference, comparison, testimony — each explained causally: what causes a perceptual experience? what distinguishes the causal factors of true vs. false perception? under what conditions does knowledge of an argument cause knowledge of its conclusion? Anyone reading ancient Indian philosophy quickly gets used to such causal accounts — and even faster, to the same examples recurring.

## The three standard examples
- [00:13:33] Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika almost always illustrate causation with one of three cases. (1) The **ceramic pot**: its causes include the obvious potter, but also the potter's wheel and stick, and less obviously the two halves the potter joins, the contact between the pot-halves, and the contact between them and the stick. (2) A **cloth made of threads**: among its causes are the threads, the weaver, the shuttle, the loom, and various instances of contact (e.g. between the woven threads that touch). (3) **Chopping down a tree**: the felling is caused by an axe, by its contact with the tree, by the lumberjack who wields it, and so on.
- [00:14:30] Notice the parts are counted among the causes (the threads of the cloth): these are **substrate** (inherence) causes, contrasted with **non-substrate** (non-inherence) causes like the weaver or the contact between threads, and with **instrumental** causes like the weaver's shuttle. Notice too that the effect may be a *thing* (pot, cloth) or an *event* (a tree's being felled) — events too have substrates, here the tree.

## Substrate cause vs. Aristotle's material cause
- [00:15:07] Tempting to spot a parallel with **Aristotle's** four causes — doesn't the substrate cause sound like the *material* cause? Perhaps, but not quite. The *parts* of an object aren't necessarily identical with the *material* it's made from — that would be clay for the pot, wood for the chariot (and obscure 1970s martial-arts flicks in the case of a Tarantino movie). Also, an immaterial substance can't be an Aristotelian material cause, but Vaiśeṣika admits it can be a substrate cause: the **soul**, for instance, is the substrate cause of mental events.

## The impenetrability objection
- [00:15:51] Another Buddhist argument: two distinct objects can't occupy the same region of space at the same time — you can stuff a cloth inside a pot only because the pot is hollow; matter is impenetrable. But if wholes were real over and above their parts, they'd break this rule: the two halves of the pot are in exactly the same place as the whole pot, so Vaiśeṣika has to allow more than one thing in the same location.
- [00:16:27] Reply: the impenetrability rule needs qualifying. Material objects can't *usually* share a space — but this needn't hold when one **inheres** in the other as in a substrate. In fact the whole *has* to be in the same place as its parts, because whenever the parts move, the whole moves with them.

## Dharmakīrti's multicoloured cloth (and David Lewis)
- [00:16:57] Yet another unravelling, due to the late-**6th-century** Buddhist **Dharmakīrti**: a cloth woven from threads of different colours, some blue, some not. What colour is the whole cloth? Not blue (some parts aren't), not non-blue (some parts are), and "both blue and not blue" is a contradiction. So there's no colour the multicoloured cloth can be — suggesting the cloth as a whole isn't real.
- [00:17:36] Strikingly similar to an argument by the contemporary philosopher **David Lewis** against the idea that things persisting over time are wholes made of *temporal parts* (time-slices): if so, whole objects would have contrary properties — dye a white cloth red and one and the same cloth is both white (before) and red (after), so a single persisting whole would bear contrary properties.
- [00:18:17] Vaiśeṣika, "determined to weave genuine wholes into the fabric of reality," answers by defining terms. The Buddhist trades on the idea that a thing either just *has* a property or just doesn't. But there are **two ways of having a property**: **pervasive** and **non-pervasive**. A property occurs *pervasively* if it's in every part — the red of Little Red Riding Hood's cloak. It can occur *non-pervasively* — the red in a Scotsman's plaid **tartan kilt**: the kilt has *colour* pervasively (every part is coloured) but each *particular* colour only non-pervasively (no one colour appears in every part). With this distinction the Buddhist argument is like the kilt itself — impressive at first glance, but nothing underneath.

## Wholes may or may not share properties with their parts
- [00:19:17] A lesson from the multicoloured cloth: wholes may or may not inherit features from their parts. It's tempting to think they must — recall the **sampling inference** met in earlier episodes: we infer the whole sea is salty because a randomly drawn drop is salty, valid because each drop's saltiness *guarantees* the sea's. But sometimes wholes **lack** properties their parts have: a thread may be entirely blue yet belong to a cloth that isn't; a thread weighs little but can be part of a heavy tapestry. And conversely a whole can **have** properties its parts lack: a pot is a suitable vessel for making stew, while half a pot is useless; a chariot can convey Arjuna into battle (once he finally decides to fight) where no axle, wheel or carriage can. And per Vaiśeṣika the pot, cloth and chariot all have the property of **visibility** despite being made of *invisible* atoms.

## Common sense defended by theory
- [00:20:38] A curious upshot: Kaṇāda and **Vātsyāyana** apparently just wanted to defend common sense against the Buddhists, yet ended up adopting highly theoretical, speculative ideas. Distinguishing pervasive from non-pervasive properties, or classifying ways one thing adheres in another, doesn't fly in the face of intuition the way Buddhist teachings can — but it's hardly plain common sense either. Just as **Nyāya** makes sophisticated epistemological proposals to uphold the reliability of everyday inferences, **Vaiśeṣika** offers a sophisticated metaphysics to defend the reality of everyday things — pots, chariots, textiles. Under dialectical pressure the Vaiśeṣikas were provoked into ever subtler distinctions and into recognizing new real entities such as inherence and absences. Taken together the theory stays a single, admirably coherent system — "appropriately enough, more than the sum of its parts."

## Where we go next
- [00:21:48] So far the system has been treated under **metaphysics**, since Vaiśeṣika is above all an attempt to classify and understand reality. But many of its ideas really fall under **physics** — notably its **atomism**, which in ancient Greek thought (and still today) counts as physical science, not metaphysics — and Kaṇāda has ideas about other physical fundamentals like **space** and **time**. Until those are covered the whole story hasn't been told: next time, a final part of the series on the Vaiśeṣika school.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Vaiśeṣika** — the school defended here; metaphysical realism about substances, properties, universals — and about complex **wholes**, validating everyday objects against Buddhist anti-realism.
- **Kaṇāda** — Vaiśeṣika's founder; holds that a whole is a real new thing over and above its parts, and that the self is an enduring unity (what licenses saying "I").
- **avayavin (whole)** — the real composite object — chariot, book, person — that is one thing, not a heap, and can do what no part can alone.
- **paramāṇu (atom)** — the imperceptibly small constituents; Vaiśeṣika is an atomist but *not* a reductionist, since unperceivable atoms can't be what we see.
- **samavāya (inherence)** — the special relation by which a whole resides in its parts (and a universal in its instances); clarified to escape the "entire book in each page" dilemma.
- **substrate cause** — the parts of a whole, treated as a special cause; like an immanent universal's dependence on instances, but a whole (unlike an eternal universal) dies when its parts do.
- **pervasive vs. non-pervasive property** — two ways of having a property: in every part (red cloak) vs. only in some parts (tartan kilt); defuses Dharmakīrti's colour argument.
- **the Buddhist anti-realists** — treat wholes and the self as conventional mental constructions; deny any enduring self, as a step toward liberation.
- **Dharmakīrti** — late-**6th-century** Buddhist; argues a multicoloured cloth can have no colour, so the whole isn't real.
- **causation** — the running Indian theme; per **Bimal Matilal**, Indian philosophy grew from the cosmogonic search (in **Vedas**/**Upaniṣads**) for a unitary cause of the complex universe.
- **kāraka** — Pāṇini's grammar of verb–noun relations, built on a causal model (agent, instrument, etc.).
- **pramāṇa** — means of knowledge, understood causally; **Nyāya** recognizes four (perception, inference, comparison, testimony).
- **substrate / non-substrate / instrumental causes** — the standard taxonomy illustrated by the three stock examples: the ceramic **pot**, the woven **cloth**, the felled **tree**.
- **Aristotle (parallel)** — the substrate cause resembles but isn't the *material* cause: parts aren't always the material, and immaterial substances (the soul) can be substrate causes.
- **Vātsyāyana** — paired with Kaṇāda as defending common sense with deeply theoretical metaphysics.

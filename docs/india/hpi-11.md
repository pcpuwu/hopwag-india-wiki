> **In brief:** Ancient India did have political philosophy. The *Arthaśāstra* attributed to Kauṭilya — a vast manual of statecraft for the Mauryan court — oscillates between high-minded moralism (duty, justice, the king's virtue) and cold Machiavellian pragmatism (spies, deception, the "stick" of punishment); the same tension is dramatized in the legend and the edicts of the emperor Aśoka, who turns from tyrant to Buddhist moral exemplar yet rules by Dharma rather than abandoning power.

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **The *Arthaśāstra*** — a long, elaborate treatise on statecraft attributed to Kauṭilya; the central text of the episode [00:01:19].
- **Kauṭilya** — the supposed author, chief minister to Chandragupta Maurya; reports rival opinions and stakes out moderate middle positions [00:05:02].
- **The *Dharmaśāstras* / *Manu-Smṛti*** — Brahminical law-books of roughly the same period, foil to the *Arthaśāstra* [00:07:59].
- **Chandragupta Maurya** — founder of the Mauryan Empire, contemporary of Alexander, who supposedly ended his life as a Jain ascetic [00:05:08].
- **Aśoka** — Chandragupta's grandson; the most famous ancient Indian ruler, whose legend and edicts embody the choice between brute force and moral rule [00:14:22].

## What was daily life like? — and why the *Arthaśāstra* helps
- [00:00:41] A thought experiment: if you'd been born — or reborn — in ancient India, the series so far might tempt you to imagine yourself a learned **Brahmin** schooled in the **Vedas** and ritual, a noble **kṣatriya** warrior, a Buddhist monk or nun, or a radical renouncer of the **Ājīvika** or **Jaina** schools. But far more likely you'd have been a farmer or a cowherd.
- [00:01:12] Texts like the **Upaniṣads** or the Buddhist **Pāli canon** give only occasional, indirect glimpses of such ordinary lives. For the everyday, then, "good thing for the **Arthaśāstra**."
- [00:01:26] This lengthy treatise on affairs of state lays down detailed regulations for *all* ancient Indians, "from the king to the slave, from the Brahmin to the ascetic." Its trivia is delightful: ancient Indian cows wore bells so they could be found; cattle were at risk from crocodiles and tigers; buffalo milk yields slightly more butter than cow's milk; and a cowherd who milked his animals twice daily in the dry season would have his thumb cut off.
- [00:02:06] Beyond milk and butter, even a brief skim shows the text presents "the cream of classical Indian thought" on warfare, law, gender relations, slavery, and economics. Its interest for the *historian* is not in doubt.

## Is it philosophy? — Weber's challenge
- [00:02:36] The harder question: what is its interest for the historian of *philosophy*? Is the *Arthaśāstra* a pioneering work of political thought? Is its philosophical significance real but implicit — like **Pāṇini**'s grammar for the philosophy of language? Or does its practical orientation mean we'd be mistaken to read it philosophically at all?
- [00:02:55] After all, the Indian tradition is famous for its philosophy of the self, its metaphysics and epistemology — not for political thought. **Max Weber**, founder of modern sociology, claimed Buddhism in particular was "too otherworldly to bother with politics," and one might assume the same of ancient Indian philosophy as a whole.
- [00:03:23] That assumption is overturned by the opening sections of the *Arthaśāstra*.

## Statecraft for rulers: the seven elements and the "stick"
- [00:03:31] However much it tells us about cowherds and farmers, the text is clearly written for the **ruling elite** — part of its aim is to instruct rulers (and would-be rulers) in the principles of governance.
- [00:03:49] The reader learns of the **seven elements of a well-run state**: the ruler himself, his officials, the territory, the fortifications, the treasury, allied states, and — last but not least — **daṇḍa**, which literally means "stick" and here denotes both the army and the punishments imposed by the state.
- [00:04:14] The stick is even the subject of a whole branch of knowledge, **daṇḍanīti**, variously translated "government," "science of punishment," and "rule of law." Future kings should be educated in this art from a young age, and *Daṇḍanīti* may even have been the work's original title.
- [00:04:39] But the surviving title fits better: **artha** means advantage or success, so *Arthaśāstra* might be rendered "the Art of the Advantageous" — apt for a treatise whose fifteen books tell the ruler how to wield the stick of punishment and the military "to best advantage."

## Who wrote it? — the problem of Kauṭilya
- [00:05:02] Who wrote the *Arthaśāstra*? "If only it were that easy." The treatise presents itself as the work of **Kauṭilya**, chief minister to **Chandragupta**, founder of the **Mauryan Empire** in the northwest of the subcontinent. Chandragupta lived in the **later 4th century BCE**, a contemporary of **Alexander the Great** — and legend even has the two mighty rulers meeting face to face. So at first glance the text looks like a powerful official's advice to his even more powerful monarch.
- [00:05:33] The problems start with the second and third glances. Since the manuscript came to light in the **early 20th century**, scholars have disagreed over its dating and over the historical Kauṭilya's role in its composition. Worse, the text appears to have been later **reorganized**, so we read a much-altered work; and its original version itself drew on **earlier writings**.
- [00:06:07] Like Pāṇini compiling and building on earlier grammarians, "Kauṭilya — as we may as well call the author" — reports the political ideas of others even as he advances his own.

## The method of moderate opinion — Kauṭilya as Aristotle
- [00:06:20] This shows most clearly in passages juxtaposing a range of views on a question, where Kauṭilya's own opinion appears as the final, correct word — often because it adopts a **reasonable middle ground** between others' extremes.
- [00:06:38] Example: how should a good ruler seek advice? One view — keep his own counsel; another — be open to ideas from absolutely everyone (even a child might offer a good suggestion); a third — consult a circle of experts. Kauṭilya compromises: the king should discuss each matter with a small handful of ministers (like Kauṭilya himself, "though tactful enough not to say so"). Likewise on the council's size: where others insisted on a precise ideal number, Kauṭilya says it depends on circumstances.
- [00:07:22] This "ostentatious display of moderate opinion," set against the extremes of predecessors, "may remind you of **Aristotle** — if so, it might be because you've been listening to the right podcast."
- [00:07:35] Rival opinions aren't just mentioned but assigned to **specific, named individuals**, making these passages reminiscent of the dialogues of the **Upaniṣads** — and they may reflect actual debates at court. The impression is of lively discussion of political matters among ancient Indian intellectuals.

## The *Dharmaśāstras* and the meaning of *dharma*
- [00:07:59] That impression is confirmed by other writings of roughly the same period, collectively the **Dharmaśāstras**, including the **Manu-Smṛti** or **Laws of Manu**. These Brahminical treatises will be an important inspiration in the Hindu tradition — for instance among thinkers of the **Mīmāṃsā** school — and scholars trace resonances between them and the *Arthaśāstra* to argue which influenced which.
- [00:08:33] Philosophically, note the term **dharma** itself — "upright conduct or duty in the widest sense, moral, social, and religious." How much should we read into the contrasting titles? Is the *Arthaśāstra* really about expediency rather than morality, "about taking advantage rather than taking heed of one's duty"?

## The moralistic *Arthaśāstra* — Plato in India
- [00:09:00] At the start of the work, that's "certainly not the impression given." In a chapter describing India's stratified society, the *Arthaśāstra* defines the four classes — **Brahmin, kṣatriya, vaiśya, śūdra** — in terms of their distinctive **duties**, and likewise such groups as householders and ascetics. The state flourishes so long as each group carries out its proper duty. "And if that reminds you of **Plato**, you've definitely been listening to the right podcast."
- [00:09:32] Great emphasis falls on the ruler's **character**, with warnings about past kings who came to grief through wrathfulness, susceptibility to pleasure, and vanity; later, we're told the king's happiness is bound up with that of his subjects.
- [00:09:49] A similarly moralistic note sounds on **law**. Kauṭilya makes justice a crucial royal responsibility, and many provisions weigh not just what is expedient but what is **most just**. He adopts the principle that punishment should fit the crime — sometimes attractively (people are excused for an accident if they took reasonable precautions), sometimes less so (the fine for violating a slave's rights is smaller than for wronging a free citizen).

## The Machiavellian *Arthaśāstra* — spies, thorns, and counterfeit coin
- [00:10:41] Yet for long stretches Kauṭilya takes a "purely pragmatic, if not cynical" approach. **Religion** in particular features chiefly as a **social phenomenon** the king should use to manage his subjects — leading some to see the *Arthaśāstra* as an early instance of the materialist, even atheistic attitude later espoused by the **Cārvāka** school.
- [00:11:10] Kauṭilya has reason for his hard-headedness: he conceives political life largely as a **struggle against threats** to stability and prosperity. Internal and natural threats he calls **"thorns,"** to be removed by precautions against floods and calamities, "to say nothing of attacks by tigers and demons."
- [00:11:36] Of greater interest to him is the external threat of **enemy kings**. He offers ample advice on defeating them in war and on manipulating and deceiving them: how best to use **spies**, how to encourage subversive factions within a rival state, how an enemy king can be tricked into letting himself be captured.
- [00:12:02] On finance he is "not above" underhanded techniques for filling the treasury — such as planting **counterfeit money** on an enemy of the state as a pretext to confiscate his property. Such passages call to mind not Plato but **Machiavelli**.

## Reconciling the two faces
- [00:12:25] The tension between moralistic and Machiavellian strands could be explained by the text's **composite nature** — different authors with different mindsets. Alternatively, Kauṭilya may be a **moralist realistic enough** to know that building a just society sometimes requires the ruler to get his hands dirty. Or one could argue the reverse: the talk of dharma, of the king's admirable character, of legal justice, all serves his **practical aims** — moderate punishments and dutiful subjects are means to the stability of the community and his continued rule, not ends in themselves.
- [00:13:15] This oscillation, between the king as **stick-wielding enforcer** and the king as **moral paragon**, was embodied by Chandragupta himself: his empire was built on conquest (perhaps aided by the power vacuum Alexander left in northwestern India), yet at the end of his life he supposedly became devoted to the spiritual path of the **Jainas**. His death is even reported as a consequence of radical Jain ascetic practices — though it's also said he was poisoned by **Kauṭilya**, whose *Arthaśāstra* "just happens to mention" a debate over whether a minister should seize power on the monarch's death.

## Succession, election, and the rise of Aśoka
- [00:13:57] In the absence of an heir, the Indian tradition did recognize the possibility of the **aristocracy electing** a new leader — a custom reported by the Greek historian **Megasthenes**, perhaps mirrored in the story of **Indra** being chosen as king by the other gods. But when a son was available, power passed to him, as in the Mauryan dynasty.
- [00:14:22] Which brings us to "the most famous political ruler of ancient India," **Aśoka** — Chandragupta's grandson, who inherited and further expanded the Mauryan Empire. He presents an even more acute case of the paradigmatic choice facing the ruler: **brutal pragmatism or pious virtue**.

## Aśoka the cruel — and the conversion
- [00:14:46] The many legends have Aśoka making the *former* choice early. Known as **Caṇḍāśoka**, "Aśoka the cruel," he had 99 of his brothers killed to eliminate rivals for the throne; when he learned the 500 women of his harem mocked his ugliness, he had them all burnt alive; he built a horrific prison and torture house known as "Aśoka's Hell."
- [00:15:13] Then came a dramatic transformation — from merciless tyrant to a moral exemplar who so despised violence that he disliked even the killing and eating of animals, "a conversion, if you will, from stick to carrot." The cause, "in a word": **Buddhism**.
- [00:15:33] It was especially the Buddhist tradition that passed down and elaborated his legends. Since it was chronologically implausible that Aśoka could have met the **Buddha**, the biographers settled for claiming that in a previous life he was a humble child who offered the passing Buddha the only gift he could manage — a handful of dirt.
- [00:15:55] In his new life as king, after being converted by a Buddhist monk named **Samudra**, he turned from cruelty with the same enthusiasm he had reserved for tyranny, building **84,000 stūpas** (burial mounds) in honor of the Buddha — a number that may reflect the traditional figure for the atoms in the human body. Over his ministers' protests he gave away all his wealth, with only half a piece of fruit left on his deathbed to donate to the monks. Other tales show him physically **serving the saṅgha**, the Buddhist monastic community.

## Why the legends mattered — and the duty of kings to defer
- [00:16:36] Such stories encouraged the generosity of later monarchs and aristocrats, invited to follow Aśoka's example. But there was a deeper point: Buddhists retained the **Brahminical conviction** that rulers should not only respect wise sages but **defer** to them — prostrating at their feet, as Aśoka supposedly did before a Buddhist **arhat** named **Upagupta**.

## The edicts — a more ecumenical Aśoka
- [00:17:06] In modern times the Buddhist picture of Aśoka has been complemented and challenged by his **inscriptions** scattered across the former empire — edicts written to promulgate his policies. Composed in **Prākrit**, they became incomprehensible to later generations and were only **deciphered in the 19th century**.
- [00:17:30] To an extent the edicts confirm the legends: some lay down rules for Buddhist monks, and Aśoka clearly did identify as a Buddhist. But he is far more **ecumenical** than the legends suggest, insisting in several edicts on **harmony between different schools** rather than urging all to adopt Buddhism — sometimes seeming to stand beyond sectarian divisions entirely in his role as king. His personal conversion, too, may have been **gradual**.
- [00:18:10] There is nonetheless a "road to Damascus" moment implied: an inscription recording his **shock and remorse** at the slaughter in his war against the **Kaliṅga** kingdom — the very conflict that expanded his empire to cover most of the subcontinent.

## Aśoka as teacher, not ascetic
- [00:18:29] The Aśoka of the edicts is a moral paragon but *not* the humble ascetic of legend. Instead he is a **teacher** who exhorts his subjects to observe **Dharma**, or a **wise father** responsible for the moral education of his "children" — all who fall under his rule. He promotes Dharma by appointing special **ministers** to travel the realm as moral educators — and these are **government officials, not Buddhist monks**, again suggesting a moral authority above all sects.
- [00:19:13] The actual ethical doctrines are "nothing too spectacular": honor your parents, respect life, speak the truth, show kindness to animals and slaves. More interesting is the implication that **political authority should be wielded with an ethical purpose**. Aśoka innovates by giving traditional kingly practices a moral dimension — styling himself as one who still seeks **conquest, but now through Dharma rather than war**, and touring his lands to promote Dharma rather than to enjoy the pleasures of the realm.

## Aśoka, the Buddha, and rule by persuasion
- [00:19:49] In all this we may compare Aśoka with the **Buddha** himself — also from the **kṣatriya** ruling class, and likewise not content merely to achieve his own enlightenment but seeking to liberate others. Aśoka, however, pursues that goal *within* political life rather than abandoning it as the Buddha did: he is the king whose own merit and Dharma consist in encouraging merit and Dharma among his subjects. This echoes the most idealistic passages of Kauṭilya's *Arthaśāstra* — such as the one binding the king's happiness inextricably to his subjects'.
- [00:20:31] A more cynical reading is available: Aśoka was among the first world emperors to use **moral teaching to secure unity and obedience** — and "certainly not the last" (compare the program of public morality under **Caesar Augustus** in Rome). But even the most jaundiced eye must grant that Aśoka at least bases his rule on the **consent of the people** rather than violent coercion — as he puts it in an edict placed, "significantly enough," in the conquered land of Kaliṅga, he wishes to rule **by persuasion, not fear**.
- [00:21:13] As podcasters, Adamson notes, "we're more or less forced to follow his example" — persuading you to keep listening not by carrying a big stick but by talking softly, enticing you with the carrot of a fascinating interview next time. The guest will be **Rupert Gethin**, helping to wrap up the look at ancient Buddhism.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Arthaśāstra** — a long treatise on statecraft (15 books) attributed to Kauṭilya; "the Art of the Advantageous," ranging from cow-herding regulations to warfare, law, and economics; the episode's central text and ancient India's chief work of political thought.
- **Kauṭilya** — its supposed author and chief minister to Chandragupta; reports rivals' political views and settles on moderate middle positions (compared to Aristotle); the historical author's identity and date are disputed.
- **daṇḍa / daṇḍanīti** — the "stick": the army and state punishments; *daṇḍanīti*, the "science of the stick," is the royal art of government and may have been the work's original title.
- **artha** — advantage, success; gives the *Arthaśāstra* its name.
- **the seven elements of a state** — ruler, officials, territory, fortifications, treasury, allied states, and the army/punishment (daṇḍa).
- **four classes (varṇa)** — Brahmin, kṣatriya, vaiśya, śūdra; the *Arthaśāstra* defines them by their distinctive duties, the state flourishing when each fulfills its own (compared to Plato).
- **dharma** — upright conduct or duty in the widest moral, social, and religious sense.
- **Dharmaśāstras / Manu-Smṛti (Laws of Manu)** — Brahminical law-books of the same era; foundational for the Hindu tradition and for the Mīmāṃsā school.
- **Cārvāka** — the materialist/atheist school; some see the *Arthaśāstra*'s treatment of religion as social tool in this spirit.
- **Chandragupta Maurya** — founder of the Mauryan Empire, contemporary of Alexander, served by Kauṭilya; supposedly died as a Jain ascetic (or poisoned by Kauṭilya).
- **Aśoka (Caṇḍāśoka)** — Chandragupta's grandson; in legend a cruel tyrant converted by Buddhism into a moral exemplar building 84,000 stūpas; in his edicts an ecumenical teacher-king ruling by Dharma and "persuasion, not fear."
- **Kaliṅga** — the kingdom whose conquest, with its slaughter, prompted Aśoka's remorse and is recorded in his edicts.
- **the edicts** — Aśoka's inscriptions in Prākrit, deciphered only in the 19th century; show a more ecumenical, less ascetic ruler than the Buddhist legends.
- **Megasthenes** — Greek historian who reports the Indian custom of electing a leader when there is no heir.

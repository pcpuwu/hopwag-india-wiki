## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Cārvāka / Lokāyata** — the materialist, this-worldly school; the "true outsiders" of classical Indian thought, opposed to Vedic, Buddhist and Jain alike [00:01:37].
- **Bṛhaspati** — the figure to whom the lost *Cārvāka-Sūtra* is ascribed; the school's blunt founding voice [00:01:55].
- **Ajita Keśakambala** — a contemporary of the Buddha, credited as the originator of Indian materialism [00:04:35].
- **The Epicureans / Sextus Empiricus** — Greek comparison-cases: hedonists who weren't debauched, and skeptics whose suspension of judgement the Cārvāka does *not* share [00:06:53], [00:16:45].
- **The wolf's-footprint parable** — the school's signature anecdote about inference and gullible scholars [00:13:14].

## Why naturalism arose: over-insistence breeds dissent
- [00:00:13] When you try to persuade someone, being too insistent is usually a mistake — it makes people dig in their heels, since it is human nature to greet confident certainty with stubborn skepticism. So it was "all but guaranteed" that the Brahminical schools would provoke disagreement: by insisting so emphatically on the **truth of the Vedas** and their indispensability on the path to wisdom and liberation, those thinkers invited contradiction.
- [00:01:07] Contradiction came above all from the **Buddhists and Jains** (covered soon). But first Adamson looks at an even more radical response.

## Cārvāka / Lokāyata: turning the sūtra form against itself
- [00:01:13] This school used the very **sūtra-and-commentary form** made familiar by the Brahminical schools — but used it to *reject everything those schools held dear*. It was equally hostile to Buddhists and Jains, making the Cārvākas "the true outsiders of classical Indian thought," champions of a philosophy and a way of life grounded firmly **within this world**.
- [00:01:37] Called **Cārvāka** or **Lokāyata**, it took its cue from early materialist ideas already circulating around the time of the **Buddha**.

## The lost text, and a hearing at Akbar's court
- [00:01:55] The key text is ascribed to a man named **Bṛhaspati** — the *Cārvāka-Sūtra*. It is **lost**, but can be partly reconstructed from quotations preserved by angry rivals; later Cārvāka thinkers and the verses encapsulating their teaching also survive only indirectly. Of all the traditions examined so far, Cārvāka is "by far the least well-preserved" precisely **because of its irreverence**.
- [00:02:28] Yet it was still known in **16th-century India**, when it was explained to the Mughal Emperor **Akbar** that the Cārvākas regard *paradise* as a state in which a man lives as he chooses, free from another's control, and *hell* as the state in which he lives subject to another's rule. They admit only such disciplines as promote external order — knowledge of just administration and benevolent government.
- [00:02:56] By sheer chance the same thought surfaced in the early-**1970s** soul group The Undisputed Truth, who sang "you make your own heaven and hell right here on earth."

## Against ritual and against asceticism alike
- [00:03:02] The Cārvākas disputed *both* Vedic ritual practice and the **ascetic śramaṇa movements**, which promised that good deeds bring good fruits — in this life, another world, or a future incarnation. Bṛhaspati dismisses all of it, stating bluntly in two consecutive sūtras that **religious acts are not to be performed** and that **religion's instructions are not to be relied upon**.
- [00:03:34] The Cārvāka verses are blunter still: "There is no heaven, no final liberation, nor any soul in another world… nor do the actions of the [castes/orders] produce any real effect." Bṛhaspati: the fire ritual, the three Vedas, the ascetic's three staves, and smearing oneself with ashes are all "the livelihood of those destitute of knowledge and manliness."
- [00:04:00] He is an **equal-opportunity offender**, ridiculing the Vedic believer and the Buddhist/Jain ascetic alike. Mocking the Brahmins: if beings in heaven are gratified by our offerings here, then why not feed those standing on the rooftop down below? And mocking the śramaṇas: "the Jain, the Buddhist, dimwit, given to practicing physical hardship — who has taught you this way of leading life?"

## Ajita Keśakambala, originator of Indian materialism
- [00:04:35] Bṛhaspati was not the first to say such unpopular things. **Ajita Keśakambala**, a rough contemporary of the Buddha, held that there is "no consequence to almsgiving, sacrifice, or oblation," and that a good or bad action produces no result. (Ajita also denied that a human being has any **self or soul** surviving bodily death — next episode.)
- [00:04:56] On this basis the Cārvāka expert **Ramkrishna Bhattacharya** credits Ajita with **originating materialism in India**.
- [00:05:06] In a typical case of rivals distorting a view, Ajita is also reported to have denied the reality of *both* this world and the other world — making him sound like a radical nihilist. More likely he said only that we are **guaranteed no future fruits** from our actions in either world. In place of the Vedas', the Buddha's, or Mahāvīra's unfounded promises, the advice of Ajita and the later Cārvākas is to look **solely to the evident and immediate consequences** of what we do.

## Were they crass hedonists? The Epicurean parallel
- [00:05:48] Having rejected both ritual and asceticism and given up on any afterlife, did the Cārvākas have nothing left but **crass hedonism**? Their rivals say so. The very name *Cārvāka* may relate to **cāru**, "pleasant" — though an alternative etymology connects it to a root meaning the Cārvāka thinker is one who "chews up" or **destroys the self**. The label **Lokāyata** ("this-worldly") points the same ethical way. A Cārvāka verse: "While life remains, let a man live happily; nothing is beyond death."
- [00:06:33] The Jain author **Hemacandra**, a staunch enemy, captures their teaching with a picturesque jibe: abandoning pleasures in this world to strive for them in the next is "like licking the elbow" — leaving what is to be licked in your hand.
- [00:06:48] But, "like someone looking up the last drops of a margarita," take this with a grain of salt. Compare the Greek **Epicureans**, likewise smeared as hedonists. The Epicureans *were* hedonists — pleasure is the highest good — but they did **not** favour debauched excess: **Epicurus** held that the highest pleasure is simply the **absence of pain**, best achieved by training oneself to have **minimal desires**.
- [00:07:28] For the Cārvākas the hedonist reading may be even more misleading. The best collection of *Cārvāka-Sūtra* fragments makes **no mention of pleasure**, and other critics fail to confirm Hemacandra's portrait — they would hardly have missed such a "golden opportunity." In fact a standard complaint is that the Cārvākas had **no positive teaching at all** — not what you'd say of committed hedonists. More likely they simply didn't bother challenging ritualism's basic premise (that we want concrete rewards); their point was that **rituals are no way to get them**: if you want fruits, harvest them here and now.

## The other contribution: epistemology and inference
- [00:08:17] It would be misleading to think Cārvāka was only forthright rejection without argument. On at least two central topics they made real contributions: the **theory of the self** (next time) and **epistemology**, especially the **reliability of inference**.
- [00:08:40] Recall that the **Nyāya** school recognized inference as a **pramāṇa** (source of knowledge) — e.g. inferring a tiger in the trees from its growl. Cārvāka responds critically, but because the evidence is so poor it is unclear how radical that response was. What is certain: they accepted Nyāya's **first** source of knowledge, **sense perception**. The trickier question is whether they reject inferences built on perception, or accept them in some circumstances.

## Bṛhaspati and "everyday inference"
- [00:09:27] Later commentators in the tradition accepted inference. One, named **Purandara**, is reported to say the Cārvākas **accept inference** but object to anyone using it **beyond the limits of perceptual experience**. Some scholars read this as a *softening* of an original extreme empiricism — only sense perception gives knowledge — perhaps to stay competitive with rival epistemologists.
- [00:10:03] But the balance of evidence suggests Bṛhaspati himself already admitted **everyday inference**. His compressed sūtras say: perception is the means of right knowledge, since that means must be **non-secondary**; "it is difficult to ascertain an object by means of inference," and "there is no means of knowledge for determining the other world."
- [00:10:28] One could read this as **sensation alone** being a pramāṇa — and indeed the Cārvākas were sometimes called **pramāṇaikavādins**, "those who accept only one pramāṇa." The implicit argument: perception alone is **primary**, whereas inference (or testimony) is **secondary**.

## Tiger and rice: perception double-checks
- [00:10:54] If you hear a tiger sound, you can be certain there's a tiger *sound*, because you are immediately aware of it; but inferring a real **tiger** from it, or trusting someone's testimony, falls below the standard of certainty.
- [00:11:10] Nonetheless such belief-forming can be perfectly acceptable. Perhaps you don't much mind about certainty — or you can use perception to **double-check** the belief. This is everyday life: you believe eating rice will end hunger because it has before, and you can satisfy any doubt **right along with** the hunger, by eating some rice.

## The inferences Cārvāka refuses
- [00:11:37] The problem is whether such inferences extend to topics like the **other world** or an **incorporeal self** — precisely the inferences **Nyāya** and other Vedic schools wanted to make, in the service of arguing for the soul and liberation. These are exactly what Cārvāka is committed to **refuting**.
- [00:12:06] Here the claims are controversial and there's **no way to check them by perception** — we can't visit the other world to see if it's there. So Cārvāka might even welcome the inferences of **scientific explanation**, since those are hypotheses checkable against empirical evidence. The Cārvāka verses make the point: "Who will deny the validity of inference when one infers fire from smoke? Even ordinary people ascertain what is to be proven by such inferences, though they may not be pestered by the logicians." But "inferences that seek to prove a self, God, an omniscient being, the other world… are not considered valid by those who know the real nature of things." Everyday people do fine **so long as their minds are not vitiated by cunning logicians**.

## The parable of the wolf's footprint
- [00:13:14] A beautiful anecdote makes the point better than any argument. A married couple: the **husband a materialist**, the **wife a devout believer**. Unable to convince her by argument, he tries another approach — when no one is watching, he uses his fingers to scratch marks in the dust of a crossroads, **mimicking a wolf's footprints**. When the marks are found, the local scholars solemnly agree they "can only be explained by a wolf that has come into the village out of the forest." The husband then triumphantly tells his wife: consider, too, the case of these footprints of the wolf.
- [00:13:58] This version explains an otherwise incomprehensible Cārvāka verse comparing learned scholars' teachings to saying "look at the footprint of the wolf."
- [00:14:10] It may *look* like a caution against everyday inference — wolves are perceptible, unlike the other world or an immaterial self, and inferring a wolf from its tracks is hardly exotic reasoning. The verse even prefaces the footprint line with "man consists of only as much as is within the scope of the senses."
- [00:14:38] But a **14th-century** author, **Somatilaka**, stresses that the dupes include the **self-styled scholars** who informed everyone else a wolf must be about. He says: "these people, knaves in the garb of the pious, deceive normal folk" by convincing them of the infallibility of certain inferences and testimonies, "enticing them away with the hope of enjoying pleasures to be attained after reaching heaven, and produce blind faith in pious acts." On this reading the parable is **not** a wide-ranging skepticism about all inference — it casts doubt on the **misleading promises of the Vedic learned men**.

## Not skeptics: the Sextus Empiricus contrast
- [00:15:29] The puzzle about the scope of Cārvāka's critique echoes **Hellenistic skepticism** — but Adamson hastens to add the Cārvākas are **clearly not skeptics** in the Greek sense, since they **accept one authoritative pramāṇa**, sense perception. In that respect their epistemology fits the **Epicureans**, who likewise grounded all knowledge in sensation.
- [00:16:03] Just as it's controversial how to read Cārvāka's critique of inference, it's controversial how to read the Hellenistic skeptics: on one reading they withheld assent from **all** beliefs; on another, only from the **abstruse, technical** claims of the dogmatic schools. On the reading defended here, Cārvāka lines up with the **second** — happy with everyday inferences, hostile only to the ambitious inferences of their Vedic and śramaṇa rivals.

## The deeper error — and negative dogmatism
- [00:16:45] There is a more fundamental difference from Greek skepticism as defended by **Sextus Empiricus**, whose skepticism was a **suspension of judgement** about (at least) philosophical doctrines. But it's "abundantly clear" the Cārvākas did **not** suspend judgement about the fruits of ritual or a self independent of the body — they **judged, and very harshly**: it's all bunk.
- [00:17:13] But **on what basis?** To say there is no watertight inference or testimony for these things is far from showing them **false**. That would be like seeing a mark that looks like a wolf's footprint, realizing a husband *could* have made it in a marital dispute, and leaping to the conclusion that **no wolf** made it. More abstractly: for the Cārvākas it seems that "if there is no definite proof of something, then it should be taken not to exist" — and that looks like a **crass philosophical error**.
- [00:17:52] Two responses on their behalf. **(1)** For something like the other world or the independent self, **proof is unavailable in principle** — unlike the wolf, which perception could in fact track down and verify. A world reached only after death would be **completely inaccessible** to perception, the source of all knowledge; and it makes a certain sense to reject belief in things that are **in principle unknowable**, since there could not possibly be any evidence for them. A stringent skeptic like Sextus would still call this **negative dogmatism** — the truly skeptical move is to make **no judgement either way** — but the lack of even *possible* evidence may have been enough for Bṛhaspati, and "might be enough for us too."
- [00:19:06] **(2)** The other response is to build a **rival positive theory**. In the case of the self, this is precisely what the Cārvākas do — not content with calling the immortal self unproven, they offer "an innovative theory of the mind, with startling similarity to ideas current in modern-day philosophy." If you're "hungry like the wolf" to hear it, you'll have to join the next episode.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Cārvāka / Lokāyata** — India's materialist, this-worldly school; rejected Vedas, afterlife, soul and karmic fruits; the "true outsiders," opposed to Brahminical, Buddhist and Jain teaching alike.
- **Bṛhaspati** — figure to whom the lost *Cārvāka-Sūtra* is ascribed; declared religious acts not to be performed and religion's instructions not to be relied upon.
- **Cārvāka-Sūtra** — the school's lost key text, reconstructable only from quotations preserved by hostile rivals.
- **Akbar** — 16th-c. Mughal emperor to whom the Cārvāka doctrine was explained (paradise = living free of another's control; hell = living under another's rule).
- **śramaṇa movements** — the ascetic (Buddhist/Jain) traditions promising future fruits for good deeds; mocked by Bṛhaspati alongside the Brahmins.
- **Ajita Keśakambala** — contemporary of the Buddha who denied any result from sacrifice or almsgiving and any self surviving death; credited (by Ramkrishna Bhattacharya) with originating Indian materialism.
- **Epicureans / Epicurus** — Greek comparison: genuine hedonists, but who equated highest pleasure with absence of pain via minimal desire; grounded knowledge in sensation, like Cārvāka.
- **Hemacandra** — Jain critic who caricatured Cārvāka ethics ("licking the elbow" instead of what's in your hand); his hedonist portrait is unconfirmed by other sources.
- **pramāṇa** — a means/source of knowledge; Nyāya admitted perception and inference, Cārvākas were called **pramāṇaikavādins** ("accepting only one pramāṇa," sense perception).
- **inference (fire from smoke; tiger from growl)** — accepted by Cārvāka for everyday, perception-checkable cases; rejected when used to reach the unverifiable (self, God, the other world).
- **Purandara** — later Cārvāka commentator who accepted inference but barred its use beyond perceptual experience.
- **wolf's-footprint parable** — a materialist husband fakes wolf tracks; the village scholars are fooled, showing the unreliability of the learned men's inferences and promises (fullest version by **Somatilaka**, 14th c.).
- **Sextus Empiricus** — late-ancient Greek skeptic who suspended judgement; contrast-case showing Cārvāka is *not* skeptical — it judges harshly, risking what Sextus would call **negative dogmatism**.
- **negative dogmatism** — Sextus's term for denying something's existence on the grounds that it can't be proven; the philosophical trap the Cārvāka critique may fall into.

## Yes, no — and two more answers
- [00:00:35] Life is full of yes-no questions: "Will you marry me?", "Do you want fries with that?" But sometimes things are more complicated. Asked whether the Godfather movies are worth seeing, you shouldn't just say yes or no but **yes and no** — the first two are essential, the third a disappointment.
- [00:00:55] In other cases the answer is **neither yes nor no**: asked whether the *fourth* Godfather film is worth seeing, the right move is to challenge the question itself — no such film has been made.
- [00:01:05] The same holds in philosophy. Often philosophers argue straightforwardly for a positive or negative answer ("Yes, we have free will"; "No, God doesn't exist"). But just as often they argue for something more nuanced. **Thomas Aquinas'** *Summa Theologiae* is a master class in saying both yes and no: the scholastic format poses questions that can be taken in more than one way, so that in one sense the answer is affirmative, in another negative.
- [00:01:41] As for "neither yes nor no," a standard modern move is spotting a **category mistake**. Asked whether the soul is located in the brain, you might reply that the question is malformed — the soul is immaterial and has no spatial location at all.

## Classical logic and the law of the excluded middle
- [00:02:01] Despite these being standard philosophical moves, the formal study of logic has usually contented itself with the simpler yes-or-no situation. A fundamental rule of classical logic, beginning with **Aristotle**, is the **law of the excluded middle**: for any proposition, either it is true or its negation is true. Either the Godfather is worth seeing or it is not — no third option.
- [00:02:32] How could Aristotle overlook the two more complex possibilities, given that he himself was fond of the yes-and-no answer and helped inspire Aquinas' tactic? His defence: the excluded middle applies **only to meaningful propositions with clearly defined terms**. It holds so long as we are not making a category mistake (assuming the soul has a location) and not speaking too imprecisely (asking about *all* the Godfather films collectively instead of one by one).
- [00:03:07] That solution works by **limiting the range** of statements to which logic is applied. But there is another possibility: we could **expand our logic** to take in the more nuanced cases. This is what we find in Buddhism, and in particular in **Nāgārjuna**.

## The four alternatives — and refuting all of them
- [00:03:24] We have already seen Nāgārjuna use the **prasaṅga** technique to undermine dogmatic philosophical systems: he sets out an exhaustive and exclusive list of all the logically possible positions on a topic, then shows each is untenable. Typically his list has **four** possibilities, not two — his doctrine of the four alternatives, the **catuṣkoṭi**, usually translated **tetralemma**.
- [00:03:55] Nāgārjuna takes the tetralemma to be definitive of the Buddha's philosophy: "Everything is such as it is, not such as it is, both such as it is and not such as it is, and neither such as it is nor not such as it is. That is the Buddha's teaching." He admits the schema is not original to him — it appears in earlier reports of the Buddha's teaching — but he deploys it with unprecedented rigour and relentlessness.
- [00:04:33] That schema isn't the whole method, though. Typically Nāgārjuna gives the four options and then **refutes all four**. One can imagine Aristotle or Aquinas raising a quizzical eyebrow: it is one thing to say a question gets no clear yes-or-no because it is imprecise; quite another to rule out yes, no, both, *and* neither.
- [00:04:58] A good example opens the *verses on the middle way*, as if Nāgārjuna is modelling how he will argue throughout: "At nowhere and at no time can entities ever exist by originating out of themselves, from others, from both themselves and others, or from the lack of causes."
- [00:05:21] Later he applies the tetralemma to his own **doctrine of emptiness** (śūnyatā): "Nothing could be asserted to be empty, not empty, both empty and not empty, and neither empty nor not empty. They are asserted only for the purpose of provisional understanding." He refuses to endorse any thesis that would stand on a par with rival doctrines, thereby **avoiding self-refutation**. If someone misunderstands and supposes Middle Way Buddhism is a philosophy to give their allegiance to, "the snake — that is, the prasaṅga method — will coil itself around them."

## Four interpretations: first, Schayer
- [00:06:11] Because the tetralemma is so central, and because it seems to flout the rules of logic, many scholars have tried to understand it. The episode discusses — appropriately — **four** such attempts.
- [00:06:16] The Polish Indologist **Stanisław Schayer** was the first to see the tetralemma as a worthwhile logical problem. A student of **Łukasiewicz** — the Polish logician who revived medieval Aristotelian syllogistic using modern formal methods — Schayer wanted to do something similar for Nāgārjuna, reading the tetralemma within **classical propositional logic**. He took the four options to be that a disputed statement is either true, not true, both true and not true, or neither true nor not true: *p*, not-*p*, *p* and not-*p*, not-*p* and not-not-*p*.
- [00:07:03] This reading is unsatisfactory for two reasons. **First**, the third option — both true and not true — is a straightforward **contradiction**, not a genuine alternative; Nāgārjuna would be wasting effort refuting it, like someone troubling to refute the claim that there both is and is not a fourth Godfather movie. **Second**, the fourth option collapses into the third, since two negations cancel out — a point overlooked by **Sonny Corleone** when he says the FBI "don't respect nothing." Saying Luca Brasi both sleeps with the fishes and does not, is the same as saying he does not sleep with the fishes and does not *not* sleep with the fishes.
- [00:07:54] Some scholars nonetheless embrace this reading and triumphantly declare that Nāgārjuna **rejects the law of non-contradiction** — adding that this distinguishes Eastern from Western philosophy, and proves Eastern thought is essentially mystical. But that extravagant line falls at the first hurdle: Nāgārjuna **explicitly endorses** non-contradiction. Mid-refutation he says nobody can perform an action that is both existent and non-existent, "for they are contradictory; where can existence and non-existence coexist?"

## Second: Robinson on properties and classes
- [00:08:45] **Richard Robinson** proposed the tetralemma is not about whether propositions are true or false, but about whether a **property belongs to a class of objects** — bringing it closer to Aristotle, who cared about statements like "all humans are rational." Robinson could point to the lines summarizing the Buddha's teaching ("everything is such as it is, not such as it is…") and to "everything is self-caused, everything is caused by another," and so on.
- [00:09:35] The advantage: the third option is no longer contradictory. Take **having a colour**. First, maybe everything is blue (unlikely but intelligible). Second, maybe nothing is blue (comprehensible, though it would thwart advice to brides). Third, "everything is blue and not blue" — this needn't be a contradiction; it could mean *some* things are blue and some not (when **Billie Holiday** asked "Am I blue?" she was wondering which category she fell into). Fourth, "everything is both blue and not blue" — more problematic, but Robinson thinks even this could be meaningful: if anything existed it would be both blue and not blue, which is impossible, so we infer **nothing exists**.
- [00:10:42] But the reading doesn't fit the text well. Nāgārjuna gives **no warning** that the "everything" of the third option must be split into "some things blue, some not." And if the tetralemma deals **only with classes**, not individuals, Nāgārjuna couldn't apply his favourite tool to one-off dogmatic claims about particular objects — if someone said "Billie Holiday really is blue, just listen to her sing," he'd have to shrug and agree.

## Third: Jayatilleke on opposite properties and parts vs wholes
- [00:11:23] The episode prefers **K. N. Jayatilleke**, a Sri Lankan expert in early Buddhist logic, whose reading turns on two insights. **First**, the oppositions might involve **opposite properties** rather than sheer negations — black and white, rather than black and not-black. **Second**, Nāgārjuna might be exploiting the idea that a **part** of something can have a property the **whole** lacks.
- [00:11:59] Jayatilleke's geometric example: A is east of B, or west of B, or both east and west of B, or neither east nor west of B. The third — "both east and west" — looks contradictory but isn't: if B is a point and A is an east-west line of latitude running through it, part of A is east of B and another part west. The fourth — "neither" — also works: a line of latitude wholly north of the point is neither east nor west of it (the line through **Boston** is neither east nor west of New York).
- [00:12:52] You might object that Jayatilleke is cheating with the unusual properties of east and west — but the same applies to **colour**. First, some things are wholly white, like chalk. Second, others are wholly non-white, like a wholly black lump of coal. Third, however much it frustrates **Mick Jagger**, not everything is painted black: some things are white *and* non-white, like a **zebra**. Fourth, some things are **neither** white nor non-white — because it may be a **category mistake** to apply colour at all: the line of latitude through Boston is neither white, black, nor any colour.
- [00:13:46] The reading is promising because it makes the tetralemma plainly applicable in philosophy. Take the opening question of whether things are self-caused. First, perhaps every object is wholly self-caused and spontaneous (some have thought this of God). Second, perhaps every object is caused by something else (a film made by director, actors, crew). Third, things may be caused both by themselves and by others (an animal generated by its parents that can then move its own limbs). Fourth, things may be neither, because causation may not apply to them (the number four might be like this). All are respectable positions on causation — so to show the concept of causation is **empty**, each must be refuted, which is exactly what Nāgārjuna does.

## The deeper puzzle: denying all four corners
- [00:14:45] We are not out of the woods. Having made sense of the tetralemma, we must ask how Nāgārjuna can formulate an exhaustive list of four options and then **deny all of them**. Imagine someone denying a movie is good, denying it is bad, denying that parts are good and parts bad, *and* denying that it is a film for which good-and-bad don't arise (a cult film so bad it's good). Bizarre — yet this is exactly what the prasaṅga method does, rejecting even the negative options. The rejection involved must be a **rather special one**.

## Fourth: Matilal and Westerhoff on two kinds of negation
- [00:15:40] For help we turn to a fourth interpretation, put forward by **Bimal Matilal** and defended recently by **Jan Westerhoff**. It draws on an ancient Indian distinction, originally grammatical, between two kinds of negation: **paryudāsa** and **prasajya**.
- [00:15:58] The first turns one noun into another, like adding "in-" to "vertebrate" to get "invertebrate." The second is the "not" in "I do *not* say I will come to your house." The key difference: negating a **noun** still makes a **positive assertion** ("the snail is an invertebrate"), whereas negating the **verb** does not — "I do not say I will come to your house" leaves open that I might come after all; I am just unwilling to commit until I know what movie you'll be showing.
- [00:16:33] The distinction was used elsewhere, notably in the **Mīmāṃsā** theory of ritual: how to read a prohibition like "Don't slaughter a goat"? It might mean **slaughter a non-goat** — go ahead and sacrifice a cow or chicken, just not a goat (paryudāsa). Or it might tell you to refrain from any sacrificial act at all (prasajya).
- [00:17:00] The **prasajya** (verb-negating) negation works nicely with category errors. When I deny that Godfather Four is a good movie, I am not asserting it is a *non-good* movie — it doesn't exist, so it isn't a movie at all. And there are non-category-error cases too: the leading question "Have you stopped beating your wife?" A married man would want to challenge the **presupposition** behind it, even though the question is no category mistake — he is indeed someone who *could* engage in domestic abuse.
- [00:17:42] We can take Nāgārjuna to be using this **non-committal negation** when he rejects all four options. Since failing to assert a proposition differs from asserting its opposite, he can **withhold assent** from all four alternatives — showing that each involves a concept with **no purchase on reality**.

## The Buddha's silence — and the conclusion
- [00:18:06] This has a precedent in the **Buddha's own practice**: he sometimes fell silent when pressed. Asked whether the world is eternal, he did not answer; nor when asked if it is not eternal; likewise on whether the self is identical to the body or not. In one text he is silent before a series of four questions of the now-familiar structure: whether an enlightened being survives death, does not survive, both survives and does not, or neither survives nor does not.
- [00:18:45] The Buddha's silence is not uncertainty but a **rejection of the very line of questioning**. Nāgārjuna's rejection of the four alternatives likewise wants us to **dispel belief in the reality** of the thing being talked about in all four options.
- [00:19:03] Consider once more the opening of the *verses on the middle way*. The first three alternatives claim objects have some cause — internal, external, or mixed — while the fourth denies this. By refusing to agree to any of them, Nāgārjuna suggests the word "cause" **does not refer to anything at all**: it has no independent reality, no **svabhāva**. When the dogmatic philosopher offers a choice between self-causation and other-causation, "he is making us an offer we can and should refuse."
- [00:19:40] This rounds off the look at Nāgārjuna, invoked at the very launch of the series to show some ancient Indian thinkers were obviously — and very interestingly — philosophers. Because he is so important and so difficult to interpret, he gets an interview episode for a further perspective: next time features one of the four interpreters discussed here, **Jan Westerhoff**. "Now that's an offer you can't refuse."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Nāgārjuna** — Buddhist philosopher whose tetralemma method lists all four logical options on a topic and refutes every one, to show the concept at issue is empty.
- **catuṣkoṭi / tetralemma** — the "four alternatives": a claim is asserted, denied, both, or neither; Nāgārjuna treats it as definitive of the Buddha's teaching.
- **prasaṅga** — Nāgārjuna's method of laying out an exhaustive, exclusive list of positions and showing each is untenable; "the snake that coils around" anyone who treats Madhyamaka as a thesis.
- **law of the excluded middle** — Aristotle's rule that for any proposition either it or its negation is true; defensible only for meaningful, precise statements free of category mistakes.
- **category mistake** — a malformed question (e.g. asking where the immaterial soul is located); a standard ground for answering "neither yes nor no."
- **emptiness (śūnyatā) / svabhāva** — Nāgārjuna denies anything has svabhāva (independent, intrinsic reality); even "empty" is asserted only "for provisional understanding," not as a thesis.
- **Stanisław Schayer** — Polish Indologist (student of **Łukasiewicz**) who read the tetralemma in classical propositional logic; criticized because its third option is a contradiction and its fourth collapses into the third.
- **Richard Robinson** — read the tetralemma as a property belonging to a class (the "blue" example); criticized for not fitting the text and for failing on individual objects.
- **K. N. Jayatilleke** — Sri Lankan scholar; reads the corners as opposite properties plus part/whole distinctions (east-west line, white zebra), making the third and fourth options coherent. The episode's preferred reading.
- **Bimal Matilal & Jan Westerhoff** — read Nāgārjuna's rejection via two ancient Indian negations.
- **paryudāsa vs prasajya** — noun-negation that still makes a positive assertion ("invertebrate"; "slaughter a non-goat") versus verb-negation that commits to nothing; Nāgārjuna's denial of all four corners is the non-committal prasajya kind.
- **Mīmāṃsā** — ritual theory that used the two-negation distinction to interpret prohibitions like "Don't slaughter a goat."
- **the Buddha's silence** — precedent for Nāgārjuna: refusing to answer four-cornered questions (e.g. whether an enlightened being survives death) is rejection of the question, not uncertainty.
- **verses on the middle way** — Nāgārjuna's text, opening with the tetralemma on origination (from self, other, both, or no cause) and supplying the episode's running examples.

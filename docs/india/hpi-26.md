> **In brief:** An interview with Francis Clooney (Parkman Professor of Divinity, Harvard) on Vedānta as the "later Mīmāṃsā" — its layered texts (Veda → sūtra → commentary), why exegesis of scripture still counts as philosophy, whether it's better called theology, and how Śaṅkara's radical non-dualism sits within (and strains against) a tradition that submits to the Veda.

## Two halves of one tradition: Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta

- [00:00:13] Adamson introduces the guest: **Francis Clooney**, Parkman Professor of Divinity, Professor of Comparative Theology, and Director of the Center for the Study of World Religions at Harvard, here to discuss **Vedānta**.
- [00:00:43] Vedānta is also known as **Uttara-Mīmāṃsā** ("later Mīmāṃsā"), as opposed to **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā** ("prior Mīmāṃsā"). Adamson asks how the two relate and whether they form one united tradition.
- [00:01:10] Clooney starts from the **source material**, very different on each side. Mīmāṃsā works on the **Vedic ritual texts** — those uttered in ritual plus the directives for performing it — and its job, at every stage, is to "understand and show the coherence" between what the text says and the actual performance.
- [00:01:49] Vedānta is traced instead to the **Upaniṣads**, the "so-called late Vedic texts." Sharing the same ritual heritage, they are nonetheless "rather more philosophical" — concerned with the nature of reality, the self, what happens after death, "what is true or undeniable in reality." So at one level the two schools differ simply by their starting material.
- [00:02:26] But they are also alike: both have **sūtra texts** — short aphorisms that combine, organize, and clarify each body of material. On the Mīmāṃsā side, the **~2,700 sūtras of Jaimini** organize a vast ritual literature, raising the hard problems and resolving them economically. On the Vedānta side, the many Upaniṣads say many different things about the self, **brahman**, the world, and human destiny, and the sūtra attributed to **Bādarāyaṇa** aims to show "there is a coherent teaching across the Upaniṣads and not a multiplicity of meanings." Both share a "scholastic intent to show the harmony" of their texts.
- [00:03:38] Adamson notes the Mīmāṃsakas and Vedāntins themselves think of earlier and later Veda as one united body — "the Veda broadly considered." Clooney agrees [00:03:51]: any scholar sees these as **stages**, chronologically earlier and later, in "a singular development over time." The later isn't necessarily better, "but the later does seem to presume the earlier," so both sides treat early and late Veda as "integrally connected" and "together for some purpose."

## Layers of text: Veda, sūtra, commentary

- [00:04:45] Adamson reframes it as a **layering of texts**: (1) the original Vedic texts, including the Upaniṣads; (2) the sūtras that systematize them; (3) the **commentary** on those sūtras. Some of the most important figures in both schools are commentators — **Śaṅkara**, for example.
- [00:05:11] Adamson recalls that in his early Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā work, Clooney stressed "excavating the meaning" of Jaimini's sūtra — appreciating it for its own sake, if not stripping away the later commentary. How far is that possible for Bādarāyaṇa's **Vedānta-Sūtra**?
- [00:05:39] Clooney calls it a necessary question. Both sūtra texts — Jaimini's ~2,700, Bādarāyaṇa's ~500 — are "as it were, floating in space." No author simply "sat down one day and composed these vast sūtra texts without any context"; they bear the marks of **teaching traditions**, likely **reminders** to teachers and students of the topics to consider — tools "in the lively context of debating" ritual problems on one side and philosophical problems on the other.
- [00:06:35] Over time the texts detach from those contexts. We're left with the **settled sūtras**, which — if they were reminders of an ongoing discussion — "can seem rather more obscure" once the discussions stop, or to readers who were never part of them. Being memory-prompts, they're "often extremely brief," rarely full sentences, "often just a word or two" [00:07:12] — so problems of interpretation come to the fore.
- [00:07:35] And the commentators may be **separated by centuries** from the sūtras: **Śabara** two to four centuries after Jaimini, **Śaṅkara** two or three after Bādarāyaṇa. One must trust that "something coherent and continuous went on in the teaching tradition," but the gap raises the real question: "do we actually understand what the original text was intending?"

## Lost debates preserved inside the commentaries

- [00:08:12] Adamson observes that Śabara and Śaṅkara reveal **intervening debates**: just as the sūtras allude to other views, the commentators allude to people who lived *between* the sūtra and themselves. So even though those intermediate works are lost, "those other ideas are kind of preserved in the commentaries themselves."
- [00:08:34] Clooney confirms it. Both sūtra texts name **older teachers** prior to themselves, and the commentators — **Śabara** on one side, **Śaṅkara** ("the first commentator of Vedānta whose written work has come down to us") on the other — refer to other opinions, occasionally a named teacher but often vaguely, "there are some who say this... some who say that." These intervening arguments are "further developed" forms of arguments already in the (argumentative) sūtras, and the commentators are "trying to encapsulate and then take a strong opinion regarding debates that have happened before their time."

## Philosophy or theology? Exegesis as a way of thinking

- [00:09:25] Adamson presses the framing. Clooney called Mīmāṃsā "more about ritual" and Vedānta "more about philosophy," and the series treats Vedānta as philosophy. But Clooney is a professor of **comparative theology** — so is it legitimate, or *more* legitimate, to think of Vedānta texts as **theological** rather than philosophical works?
- [00:10:01] Clooney backs up. In both schools there's a distinction between the ritual and meditative **practice** and the authors' and commentators' efforts to understand, contextualize, "read according to tradition," and find claims about reality in the texts — moving into "an entire religious context" where intellectual, performative, and ethical questions interconnect.
- [00:10:52] Crucially, the structure of "having the sūtras and then having the commentary is an **allegiance to the older texts** which are considered to be sacred scripture." There is material "we are beholden to. We submit ourselves to it and work within its frame," then think "as vigorously and robustly as we can."
- [00:11:22] On the modern Western philosophy/theology distinction: though both schools have strong philosophical elements, there's "a certain boundary within which their deliberation takes place." These thinkers **will not question the authority of scripture**; they take it as presenting the problems we must deal with. That "deference to scripture" "could easily be called **theological thinking**, rather than a philosophical thinking where independence of thought" is at issue. Where there is sacred scripture and expectations of religious performance, "theology seems to be a very good word to use."
- [00:12:23] Adamson quotes Clooney back to himself: Vedānta is "an exegesis of texts and a philosophy of response to texts." That raises the worry whether **exegesis on sacred texts can be philosophy at all** — since many think philosophy must be a "disinterested, dispassionate, non-authority-laden search for truth," whereas these thinkers look like they're "just trying to tell us what a text means."
- [00:13:11] Clooney answers via the structure of Bādarāyaṇa's **Vedānta-Sūtra**: he organizes the problems and insights of the Upaniṣads to stress taking them to heart, believing the scriptures are **coherent and non-contradictory**, that their positions are preferable to rival schools (Jains, Buddhists, others), and that properly understanding the Upaniṣads on self, world, and brahman "prepares one for the arduous work of **meditation**" — pursued both on the texts and within "a certain way of life." The last part of the sūtras describes the **result**: a transformation of one's life and "an ascent to a higher reality and ultimately to union with brahman."
- [00:14:24] So given "the intensity of the debates," one can call it philosophical "if one has a rich sense of philosophy" — Clooney invokes **Pierre Hadot's philosophy as a way of life**: thought *transformative* of the thinker, "following the right ideas through to a coherent conclusion." **Śaṅkara**, **Rāmānuja**, and **Madhva** disagree on what ultimate reality is like, "but they agree that this is a **transformative philosophy**." Clarifying understanding and removing errors transform you now and prepare you for a life that "passes beyond this world," as the **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** says, not returning to ordinary existence.

## Brahman: a philosophical and a theological idea at once

- [00:15:35] Adamson notes that **brahman** seems "simultaneously a philosophical and theological idea." There's a metaphysical question — what is brahman, how does the phenomenal world relate to it? — and Śaṅkara's "very radical answer" that the world is an **illusion**, or just how brahman presents itself, sounds like a philosophical proposal "with a kind of skeptical" edge. Yet brahman is also an object of **worship**; people often think of brahman in Vedānta as **God**. So this is "either both theological and philosophical, or maybe the distinction breaks down here."
- [00:16:26] Clooney agrees that real philosophical questions arise — the **one and the many**, how **time** relates to a timeless reality, **sense-knowledge** versus knowledge beyond the senses, "a kind of **idealism**" about a pure self or ultimate reality — debated over centuries, in India "but not only in India."
- [00:17:06] What is **distinctive to Vedānta** is "a certain **skepticism about the ability of the human mind on its own**" to speculate usefully here. With so many possibilities — is brahman the same as the world or greater, is the world real or illusory, is our ordinary identity true or a falsehood — "there are as many opinions as there are teachers."
- [00:17:43] So the shared view (cutting across Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, and the rest) is that the Upaniṣads, clarified by the sūtras, give "a **correct approach** to this higher knowledge." Human questioning is valuable, "but at a certain point one must submit one's understanding... to the frame of knowledge given in the Upaniṣads as read through the sūtras" — a **transition from independent philosophizing to one that pays due deference to tradition** in all Vedānta schools.

## Is Śaṅkara even faithful to the Veda? The ritual problem

- [00:18:30] Adamson raises a tension specific to **Advaita Vedānta**. Has "philosophical speculation led [Śaṅkara] to sacrifice" what should be incumbent on him as a **Vedic exegete**? Rival commentators say exactly that: he has effectively **dispensed with ritual**, and perhaps there's no longer a coherent notion of **karma** or **dharma**, since all of that is phenomenal. If he "condemns all of that as illusory" (maybe too strong, Adamson allows), how can he make sense of "these very fundamental aspects of Vedic tradition?"
- [00:19:29] Clooney sorts it into **levels**. First, **rhetoric**: rival schools — the "different / non-different" schools and the "kind of dualist" school of **Madhva** — can be "quite polemical and quite devastating," attacking Śaṅkara as "unfaithful to the Vedas," "dismissive of tradition," "tantamount to being a Buddhist." This is "part of the either game or the serious project" of arguing about these materials.
- [00:20:20] Second, when one **separates the philosophy from the exegesis** — especially reading Śaṅkara through later teachers and modern textbooks — he can look like a system-builder "not really deferential to the Upaniṣads." But "if one actually reads his work" — the **Brahma-Sūtra** commentary, where he "arduously" struggles both to establish the **non-dualism of brahman and self** *and* "to still find a place for ritual performance," for the teacher, for scripture, "even... a sense of karma" — one sees "the struggle of an intellectual who's chosen not to leave the Vedic tradition behind."
- [00:21:23] Finally, beyond the "classic statement" in the Brahma-Sūtra commentary, one must read his **commentaries on the individual Upaniṣads** — "most notably" his "brilliant and greatest" commentary on the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad**. There he works out **brahman as self** with "very careful gradations" of how that higher consciousness becomes accessible to people who begin as committed **ritualists** — how their "dharmic practice" enables them "to move beyond dharma to a higher kind of knowledge." So he is "not simply offering an alternative philosophy divorced from the Upaniṣads," but showing "how this philosophy and liberative theory grows out of the text he's commenting on."

## Ritual as preparation for knowledge — and the Buddhist comparison

- [00:22:46] Adamson asks whether Śaṅkara could treat dharmic ritual as an **initial / preparatory stage** transcended once you achieve "identifying your consciousness with brahman." Clooney points to the **third book of the Brahma-Sūtra**, where Bādarāyaṇa's sūtras hold that "there can be no guarantee that ritual action **causes** knowledge" — many ritualists never pass "from the cause, the ritual practice, to the effect, the higher knowledge."
- [00:23:40] Yet **ritual is necessary to prepare the person** for higher knowledge. As Śaṅkara puts it: "dharmic action does not *cause* knowledge, but it **prepares the way** for the arising of knowledge." By rooting out bad ideas, ego, stray desires, and worldly interests, the difficult **discipline of ritual** makes one disciplined and attentive — and, "because it is so difficult, wants something more," and so ready to read the Upaniṣads and rise to higher knowledge. Śaṅkara, like most Vedānta teachers, would be **skeptical** [00:24:35] of someone who "with no ritual background, with no discipline" expects to just read the Upaniṣads and have something happen: the background is "only background, but it's the background one needs."
- [00:24:51] Adamson notes this is also Śaṅkara's **critique of the Buddhists** — even though "the end result sounds so Buddhist." Clooney agrees, granting it "may be quite unfair": Śaṅkara charges that the Buddhists' wrong ideas and practices "not prepare one for higher knowledge but distract one" — being "rooted in the **ego** of the individual" — whereas ritual's point is "to **purify** that ego and detach one's higher knowledge from the ego." Adamson calls it "a very ironic accusation to level at the Buddhists of all people," but sees why: without "subordinating your own self to a ritual," you might find no way to eliminate ego-based desire.
- [00:26:13] Clooney closes the thread: there's real "common ground between Śaṅkara and the Buddhists" — moving away from ritual, cultivating a better self, realizing a transformative higher reality — but what is **determinative of Śaṅkara**, who stays "within the brahminical and Vedānta fold," is that the background "has to be gone through first." Only the rare individual (perhaps carrying "karmic residues from a previous life") enters ready; otherwise the claim that "the Veda is not necessary, the ritual is not necessary" could be "simply the ego... putting aside the difficult, necessary work." For Śaṅkara, unlike the Buddhists, "the narrow path runs **through Vedic performance**."

## Beyond Śaṅkara: the other Vedāntas and the theistic schools

- [00:27:10] Adamson worries the series may mislead by **concentrating on Śaṅkara** (they've covered the Vedānta-Sūtra, Śaṅkara, and grammar-and-Vedānta in **Bhartṛhari**). Is it a "distortion" to dwell on him? Are other Vedāntins "much more interested in retaining a final place for **ritual**," so that Mīmāṃsā as a whole stays a deeply ritual-based tradition with Śaṅkara as "a kind of outlier or exception"?
- [00:28:05] Clooney returns to the starting point: for all these thinkers the Veda, ritual, sūtras, Upaniṣads, and meditation are "all connected." That forces "a very hard question about the Śaṅkara tradition" — whether, for the sake of "a purity of higher knowledge" and "an absolute transition which leaves the world and practice behind," he offered "a very fine narrative of a higher knowledge that is irreversible" but in fact **oversimplifying** — and (though he'd never say so) "improving on the Upaniṣads and on Bādarāyaṇa's sūtras." Go back to any great Upaniṣad [00:28:54] and one finds "quite a diversity of claims"; "deities appear, deities are sometimes very central," and the Upaniṣads seem "quite comfortable with the diversity of narratives" about reality.
- [00:29:18] Most scholars reading Bādarāyaṇa "in himself" find a **compromised position** — "not so much ultimate non-duality, but a kind of duality-non-duality in conjunction": a higher knowledge that "does not entirely obliterate a world of difference," still requiring ritual, leaving "some sense of distinction between self and brahman" even after liberation.
- [00:29:54] In the **theistic schools** this follows easily — e.g. **Rāmānuja**, "a few centuries after Śaṅkara," where realizing the Upaniṣads' teaching happens "in a world where there is still some differentiation," above all brahman "now as the **Lord**, whom one worships, to whom one offers **pūjā**, sacrifices, and the like."
- [00:30:18] A **historiographical** point: the great Western interest in Śaṅkara over two centuries owes something to "the brilliance of his thinking," but also to scholars "trained in traditions of **idealism**" who took his system for "the highest form of Indian thought." Those who know the Upaniṣadic heritage well may concede that "the other schools of Vedānta may actually gain insights... that Śaṅkara, for the sake of his zealous clarification, may have put aside."

## What Vedānta brings to comparative theology

- [00:31:01] Adamson asks: if idealism isn't the best lens, what does **Vedānta as a whole** bring to **comparative theology** — something distinctive, in Śaṅkara and the others, for comparative work?
- [00:31:32] Clooney answers that the "incredibly rich traditions of Vedānta" — "doubly rich" set beside Mīmāṃsā ritual theory and the whole world of the Veda — give "an extraordinarily developed, sophisticated, multi-generational tradition of interpretation," "multidimensional and very insightful about the nature of the world and reality." If one is open to calling this **theological learning** — "deferential to tradition and scripture" and open to "transformative practice" — then in Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, or other versions, "these are **magnificent theological systems**": not just "doctrines of God," but "the entire apparatus of thinking through the nature of reality, taking words seriously, taking experience seriously, taking practice seriously, and believing that in the end there is an ultimate truth one can reach." To see them whole is to see, "outside the West and outside... the Christian tradition, full-scale, developed theological understandings of the world."
- [00:32:58] For comparative work, what matters is **not** treating other traditions as "raw material... as we perpetuate our own models of theology," but realizing that "the thinkers of these great traditions of the East... are our **intellectual peers**," doing "the same kind of work of understanding reality in a whole and complete, effective way as we are ourselves doing."
- [00:33:46] Adamson agrees wholeheartedly, thanks Francis Clooney, and previews the next episode: the **Sāṃkhya** school, "which was criticized by the Vedāntins."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms

- **Vedānta / Uttara-Mīmāṃsā** — the "later Mīmāṃsā"; traced to the Upaniṣads, concerned with the nature of reality, the self, and what is undeniably true; treated here as exegesis-and-philosophy-of-response to sacred texts.
- **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā** — the "prior Mīmāṃsā"; works on the Vedic ritual texts, showing the coherence between what scripture says and how ritual is performed.
- **brahman** — ultimate reality; in Vedānta both a metaphysical question (how the phenomenal world relates to it) and an object of worship; for Śaṅkara, the phenomenal world is its illusory appearance.
- **sūtra** — terse aphorisms (Jaimini's ~2,700; Bādarāyaṇa's ~500) that organize and harmonize a vast body of material; memory-prompts for larger oral teaching debates, hence brief and obscure once the debates ended.
- **Jaimini** — author of the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra (~2,700 sūtras).
- **Bādarāyaṇa** — attributed author of the Vedānta-Sūtra (Brahma-Sūtra), arguing the Upaniṣads form one coherent teaching; read "in themselves," his sūtras suggest a duality-and-non-duality compromise rather than pure non-dualism.
- **Śabara** — early Mīmāṃsā commentator on Jaimini, centuries after the sūtras.
- **Śaṅkara** — first surviving Vedānta commentator; founder of Advaita (non-dual) Vedānta; radical illusionism about the world; charged by rivals with abandoning ritual/karma/dharma and "being a Buddhist," but in his Brahma-Sūtra and Upaniṣad commentaries struggles to keep a place for ritual.
- **Advaita Vedānta** — Śaṅkara's non-dualism: identity of self and brahman, the phenomenal world as illusion.
- **Rāmānuja** — theistic Vedāntin a few centuries after Śaṅkara; realization is possible in a world that retains differentiation, with brahman as the Lord one worships (pūjā).
- **Madhva** — dualist Vedāntin; reality not the same as the self; polemical critic of Śaṅkara.
- **Bhartṛhari** — figure linking grammar and Vedānta (covered earlier in the series).
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad** — subject of Śaṅkara's "brilliant and greatest" commentary, working out brahman-as-self in careful gradations.
- **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** — cited for the claim that the liberated one "does not return again to ordinary existence."
- **karma / dharma** — action and duty/law; located in the phenomenal realm, raising the worry of whether Śaṅkara can still accommodate them; for him, dharmic ritual *prepares* but does not *cause* higher knowledge.
- **philosophy as a way of life (Pierre Hadot)** — the rich sense of philosophy as transformative of the thinker, by which Vedānta counts as philosophy and not just exegesis.
- **comparative theology** — Clooney's project: treating Vedānta thinkers as intellectual peers doing full-scale theology, not raw material for Western models.

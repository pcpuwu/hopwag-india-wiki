Closes with Patrick reading from the *Chāndogya Upaniṣad*.

## Where philosophy actually happened — the institutions
- [00:00:17] Adamson introduces **Kit Patrick**, host of the *History of India* podcast, and proposes the theme: the relationship between ancient Indian history and ancient Indian philosophy — in particular, the **cultural and institutional context** in which philosophy was pursued.
- [00:01:13] The romantic image is of **sages withdrawing into the forest** — no institution at all. But Patrick's reply [00:01:35]: even the forest sage, if attached to a hermitage, *has* an institution. And nearer the "main end of society" there were large institutions doing something like **postgraduate education** — places students went *after* mastering the **Vedas** and the basic texts.
- [00:02:06] The oldest is probably **Takṣaśilā** (Taxila), a city in northwest India (modern Pakistan). It was **not a single university**: it was a collection of independent teachers, each specializing — one in medicine, one in **nīti** (statecraft/statesmanship), many in various religious texts. Students came from all across northern India — even the south — to find the house of the teacher who taught what they wanted and **gain admittance**.
- [00:02:54] Admitted students **lived in the teacher's house**, doing chores, fetching food and firewood — the bond was framed as a **father–son relationship**. Small operations had a dozen students; the largest had hundreds, with senior students hired as **postgraduate teaching assistants** — so the "house" became an institution in the modern sense.
- [00:03:41] Incoming students often had to **talk the teacher into taking them on**. But Patrick qualifies [00:03:46]: many were sons of kings or ministers, sent in groups and paying a fee — they got in regardless. Those needing support or a fee waiver would have had to argue harder, though Patrick can't cite firm evidence of that happening.

## Caste, fees, and the limits of "social mobility"
- [00:04:24] Adamson asks whether there was real **social mobility** — could clever lower-class people enter and become scholars? Patrick (speaking only of Takṣaśilā): the **four varṇas** are **Brahmin** (top), **Kṣatriya**, **Vaiśya**, and **Śūdra**. The top three — the **twice-born** castes — all have a **duty to be educated**, so they complete the basic education; some take it further at Takṣaśilā, and many of those are Kṣatriya or even Vaiśya.
- [00:05:12] Even some *teachers* were Kṣatriya or Vaiśya. The **Brahmin** caste owns the responsibility of teaching, but even strict rule books concede that **under extreme circumstances** you could be taught by someone of a lower caste. So [00:05:34] it's **not mobility between castes** — you don't move up — but a Vaiśya could gain **more kudos and social authority** by getting a deep Vedic education and even becoming a teacher.
- [00:05:57] **Economic** mobility, though, has little evidence at Takṣaśilā — the houses we know about specialized in taking the sons of kings, people already well-to-do.
- [00:06:14] The **Buddhist** institutions are a different story. The most famous is **Nālandā** (in Bihar); a bit later, **Valabhī** (in modern Gujarat). We have **vivid descriptions** from visiting **Chinese scholars**, who record the everyday details insiders never bothered to note. At Nālandā you were **stopped at the very gate** and quizzed — to confirm you'd mastered the **Brahminical orthodox texts**, the basic **Buddhist** texts, and the texts of other sects. Pass the test and you seem to have **earned a place** — and because it was Buddhist, **caste and social background mattered less**, making these institutions **more genuinely places of social mobility** [00:07:19].

## Kings and scholars — sincere devotion, or a luxury sports car?
- [00:07:26] Adamson turns to the **Kṣatriya** (warrior/ruler) class and its devotion to scholarship — rulers who **prostrated themselves** before Brahmin scholars. He cites the emperor **Harṣa**, who poured effort into attracting a Chinese traveling scholar to his court, even risking a fallout with an ally. Is this just **using scholars as a status symbol** — "look at my fancy new scholar" — or a sincere attempt to draw on their learning to improve rulership?
- [00:08:37] Patrick: the self-abasing king is "almost a stereotype" in ancient India, and **at least sometimes the devotion is sincere**. There are dynasties uniformly devoted to **Śiva** where one ruler turns Buddhist and devotes himself unusually; and holy men's own accounts report kings who seem **utterly sincere** (though they could be lying).
- [00:09:32] But rulers also got **concrete returns**, especially when patronizing an institution. Strikingly [00:09:54], donors often **funded sects they didn't follow**: the **merchant patrons** of the Buddhist university in modern Gujarat almost all worshipped **Śiva**, yet poured far more money into the **Buddhist** institutions than into Śiva worship — one grant to a Śiva temple against twenty or thirty to Buddhist institutions.
- [00:10:40] So the kings got **two things**. First, **highly educated advisors** — graduates who had studied every sect's texts plus works on **warfare and governance**, and who went on to staff the local court. Second [00:11:10], a **deep connection to the merchant class**: Buddhist (and other) institutions had intimate ties to rich traders — the **seṭṭhis** — so becoming the patron of an institution the merchants loved **won their hearts** and got the king "in with the rich guys." Sincerity of philosophical interest, Patrick concludes, varied person by person.
- [00:12:03] Adamson adds that rulers may also be **living up to an ideology**: looking back to **Aśoka** — pretty clearly sincere in his Buddhist/philosophical commitments, as his inscriptions show — gave them a model that made valuing scholarship part of the role. Patrick agrees [00:12:42] and says it's **older than Aśoka**: a core, stable part of the idea of **Indian kingship**, expected to have **practical consequences** — understanding the **varṇas** and **varṇāśrama-dharma** (the duties/virtues that follow from one's station) was held to make a king's people happy, so being in touch with philosophy was part of ruling well.

## The ethics of war — and the slow turn toward Ahiṃsā
- [00:13:11] Adamson raises ancient Indian texts on the **right way to wage war**, and a shift Patrick has discussed: the idea that wars should be fought so as to **minimize death and violence**. Is that an influence of the philosophical ideal of **nonviolence** — most famous in **Jainism** and **Buddhism**, but present on the **Brahminical** side too?
- [00:14:04] Patrick: the **timing suggests not**. The move away from **sacrifice** happened *centuries* before the move away from brutal warfare. During the era of **Aśoka** you have the **Arthaśāstra**, which is **full-on crooked war** — trick your opponents, punish them when they're down, crush them completely. So the **ethics of war were not in lockstep** with the broader ethics of violence; the doctrine of war-as-last-resort, fought to **spare as many lives as possible**, comes much later.
- [00:15:05] *However*, gentler elements appear from the very beginning, pointing toward **ahiṃsā**. **Megasthenes**, the Greek ambassador at the **Mauryan** court (well before Aśoka's dynasty), reports a rule that **farm workers must not be harmed or killed** — a battle might rage while laborers a mile away worked in perfect safety. There was also the long-standing idea that you'd rather **not kill a conquered king** on the battlefield, because you wanted to **reinstate** him — going around killing kings and ransacking territory was "bad form." Likewise, rules against killing those who **run away** [00:15:56].
- [00:16:17] But the **really thoughtful** ethic — *hold back as much as you can, and when you strike, kill as few as possible, even at the cost of your own honour* — is later. That ethic explicitly **licenses trickery** [00:16:31]: spies, ambushes, sneakiness — acceptable because **fewer people die**. Patrick: such tactics would have been **unacceptable** under the Arthaśāstra-era ethics of Aśoka's time; they became acceptable only when **people started to value life more than looking good**. Adamson calls it "an admirable development within Indian political life."

## Did philosophy reach ordinary people?
- [00:17:13] Adamson, prompted by the image of the field workers, asks whether philosophical ideas **penetrated the wider populace**. In European history, philosophy was an **elite** affair — medieval peasants knew little of the **scholastics at the University of Paris** (Chaucer shows people laughing at "clerks" without grasping what they did). But our assumptions about India differ — we imagine the population was **broadly acquainted with Buddhist ideas** even if not Buddhist. Walk into an ancient Indian marketplace: would people be reasonably informed about philosophical debates?
- [00:18:43] Patrick declines a straight answer (Adamson: "You philosophers"). By the **Gupta era** (toward **400–600 CE**), philosophy was **no longer uniquely upper-class**. Small societies called **goṣṭhīs** sprang up — 12 to 24 people gathering in a house (sometimes a member's, sometimes a courtesan's) for intellectual pursuits: music and **music theory**, or **philosophical debate**.
- [00:19:46] Our window into these is the first **autobiographical Sanskrit writer**, **Bāṇa**, who describes joining one and lists its **44 members** one by one — a remarkable mix: a **Jain monk**, a female toilet attendant, a dancer, a snake charmer, a **Śaiva monk**. People from all walks of life debating philosophy together — so it wasn't only the "official top rung" engaging, at least by the **late Gupta** period (probably in a large city).
- [00:20:44] But to Adamson's sharper question — would a random person in the **market**, not part of a goṣṭhī, **know what Buddhism was**? Patrick's guess (flagged as a guess; "I'm an amateur historian"): there were **two Buddhisms**. The **high-minded, official version** in the texts; and **another version**, part of the same institution but with a very different flavour.

## Two Buddhisms, and the common storehouse of tales
- [00:21:04] The evidence: the **Stūpa at Sāñcī** is carved with things you'd never expect from the monkish texts — **erotic scenes**, trees, life, dancing, **attachment to the world** — exactly what you're *not* supposed to indulge if you only read the renunciant literature.
- [00:21:58] In between sits the **Jātaka Tales** — Buddhist stories of the Buddha's former lives. But the **same stories appear elsewhere**: several are in the **Mahābhārata** and other story-collections, and tracking them down shows the *same tale carries a different moral* in each source.
- [00:22:39] Example: a Jātaka about a father who leaves his son, and the son is **bitten by a snake**. In the Buddhist version the point is the father's **non-attachment** — he is unmoved, grieves not at all. In the **Mahābhārata** the same story becomes a **legal puzzle about fault**: is the snake to blame? the boy (who got bitten while burning rubbish)? the father for leaving? [00:23:05] So there's a **common store of stories and themes** drawn on for different ends. Walk into the market and start telling a Buddhist tale and people will **recognize the story** even if they never heard it from a Buddhist — you might add a new spin, but it's from a shared storehouse. And part of that storehouse is **philosophical** [00:23:47]: everyone would know **karma**, **saṃsāra**, and even **mokṣa**.
- [00:24:01] Adamson finds it plausible and refines it. One axis is **literate vs. non-literate**, but it may be **more continuous** than that — true in Europe too: scholasticism was cloistered, yet **continuous with ordinary Christian belief** (peasants knew the **Trinity** and the **Eucharist** without the technical theology). So perhaps the difference is **how technical and how deep** the thinking about shared topics like **mokṣa** runs.
- [00:25:11] Patrick adds an Indian wrinkle: Buddhists, Jains, and Brahminical orthodoxy created **middle categories** — the **lay devotee**. Partly economic (monks have no money, so you need **rich merchants** committed to funding the monastery), but also a tier obliged to **listen to some doctrine** as official members of the **Saṅgha** — people with "one foot in the world" still made to learn some philosophy. Adamson parallels this to medieval students who "ate their vegetables" of logic before going off to work in the **chancery** or court [00:25:55] — "just like our students today."

## Should a historian of philosophy mine the stories?
- [00:26:19] Adamson notes Patrick's podcast often recounts **myths and legends**; should a historian of Indian *philosophy* do the same — read tales of talking animals for their philosophical implications? Patrick: it's tricky, precisely because these are **common stories reused for different philosophical ends** — so a historian-philosopher really only needs the **moral, not the story**.
- [00:27:17] His concrete case: a famous tale of **crows**. In the **Jātaka** version the Buddha is the **king crow**; a bird-catcher nets the flock, and the Buddha tells them to **flap as one** and escape, carrying off the net. The catcher's wife mocks him; he returns, and this time the crows **fight among themselves** (one treads on another's head), and the Buddha flies off.
- [00:28:08] In the **Mahābhārata** version there are only **two** crows, snared by a noose; they fly off together until they quarrel, and the hunter, who simply **follows them**, catches them when they fight. In yet another **Brahminical** text, the **birds win**. Same story, slightly different morals.
- [00:28:37] Crucially, the morals are **not tightly tied** to the core doctrines of their source: there's nothing of the **Bhagavad-Gītā** in the two-crows tale, nothing of **mokṣa** or enlightenment in the Buddhist one. Instead the Buddhist folk tales push **everyday virtue** [00:28:58]: good **karma** comes from simply **doing virtuous things** — don't bother **sacrificing animals**, just be a good person — which is a long way from Buddhism's core ideas. So again: shared stories serving the **everyday version** of Buddhism (and of Brahminical orthodoxy), not the high-minded theory.

## Teaching philosophy in modern India
- [00:29:43] A reveal: Patrick, who always calls himself an **amateur historian**, is by day a **philosopher** — he taught in the UK and has now moved to India to teach there. Adamson asks what that's like.
- [00:30:16] "Quite different." People ask **"why would anyone want to do philosophy?"** — a question rare in the UK, where employers like it and students do it to get good jobs. The Indian attitude is different, and so is the **conception of philosophy**: it's largely seen and taught as **history of ideas** — both Indian and Western/analytic philosophy presented as "here's an idea" rather than the Western mode Patrick prefers: *now you, the student, engage with it and respond*.
- [00:31:05] Still, he's encouraged: Indian students get **just as quickly** into the same philosophical problems, and are **just as imaginative, brilliant, and surprising** — no difference in *doing* philosophy, only in the **attitude** toward it.
- [00:31:35] On **ancient Indian philosophy** specifically, student interest is mixed: some find it brilliant and new, others are **lukewarm**, feeling they've **heard it all before** (whether or not they actually have). One reason: popular **comic-book series** in India cover the **stories** of these thinkers, so by college it can feel like "old hat." Patrick's caveat [00:32:26]: the comics carry **very little of the ideas** — "if you want the ideas, you have to go for the podcast"; the comics give only a coarse outline of the history.

## Coda — the series ends, and a reading from the Chāndogya
- [00:32:39] Adamson signs off the **entire India series** (possibly to return later), and notes the **same feed** will next carry **Africana philosophy** with co-host **Chike Jeffers**. He thanks **Kit Patrick**.
- [00:33:15] Borrowing Patrick's own habit of reading from an **original source**, they close with Patrick reading from the **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** — "one of the oldest Upaniṣads" — section seven, a dialogue between the student **Nārada** and the teacher **Sanatkumāra**.
- [00:34:02] Nārada asks to be taught; Sanatkumāra says, "Come to me with what you know." Nārada recites a vast curriculum — the **Ṛgveda**, **Yajurveda**, **Sāmaveda**, **Atharvaveda**, the corpus of histories and ancient tales (the "fifth Veda"), ancestral rites, mathematics, soothsaying, treasure-locating, dialogues, monologues, and the sciences of the gods, ritual, spirits, government, heavenly bodies, and serpent beings. "All that I have studied."
- [00:34:54] His complaint: "Here I am, a man who knows all the Vedic formulas, but is **ignorant of the self**." He has heard that **those who know the self pass across sorrow** — and he is full of sorrow; he begs to be taken "to the other side of sorrow."
- [00:35:16] Sanatkumāra's answer: all that learning is **"nothing but name."** One should **venerate the name** — for one who venerates **brahman's name** wins "complete freedom of movement in every place reached by name." [00:35:55] Nārada asks: is there anything **greater than name?** "Yes, there is" — and there the episode (and the series) leaves off, on the threshold of something beyond mere name.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Kit Patrick** — host of the *History of India* podcast; by day a philosopher, recently relocated to teach philosophy in India; the episode's interview guest.
- **Takṣaśilā (Taxila)** — probably the oldest higher-learning center, in the northwest (modern Pakistan); not one university but many independent teachers; students lived in the teacher's house in a father–son bond.
- **Nālandā / Valabhī** — Buddhist "postgraduate" institutions (Bihar; Gujarat); entry by a gate-examination, more open to lower castes — more genuinely socially mobile; described by visiting Chinese scholars.
- **the four varṇas** — Brahmin, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, Śūdra; the top three are "twice-born" with a duty to be educated; teaching is the Brahmin's role.
- **varṇāśrama-dharma** — the duties/virtues following from caste and station; a king's grasp of it was held to make his people happy.
- **Harṣa** — emperor cited for courting a Chinese scholar, even risking an alliance, to enhance his court.
- **Aśoka** — the model of a ruler sincerely committed to Buddhist/philosophical ideals (per his inscriptions); but devotion to scholarship in kingship is older still.
- **seṭṭhis** — rich merchants/traders; their patronage of (often Buddhist) institutions gave kings a route to "win their hearts."
- **Ahiṃsā** — nonviolence; war ethics drifted toward it slowly and unevenly, lagging the earlier turn against sacrifice.
- **Arthaśāstra** — statecraft text of Aśoka's era endorsing "crooked," ruthless war — evidence that war-ethics and violence-ethics weren't in lockstep.
- **Megasthenes** — Greek ambassador at the Mauryan court; reports a rule sparing farm workers in wartime, an early ahiṃsā-leaning element.
- **goṣṭhī** — small (12–24-member) Gupta-era intellectual society mixing all social ranks for music theory and philosophical debate.
- **Bāṇa** — first autobiographical Sanskrit writer; records his goṣṭhī's 44 members (Jain monk, dancer, snake charmer, Śaiva monk, a toilet attendant…).
- **Jātaka Tales** — stories of the Buddha's past lives; the same plots recur in the Mahābhārata and elsewhere with different morals — a shared "storehouse" of tales.
- **Stūpa at Sāñcī** — its carvings (erotic scenes, dancing, worldly attachment) reveal an "everyday" Buddhism unlike the renunciant texts.
- **the Saṅgha & the lay devotee** — the Buddhist/Jain "middle category" obliged to learn some doctrine while keeping "one foot in the world."
- **karma / saṃsāra / mokṣa** — the shared philosophical vocabulary even a marketplace stranger would likely know.
- **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** — one of the oldest Upaniṣads; the closing reading: Nārada (vast in learning but "ignorant of the self") begs Sanatkumāra to take him "across sorrow," and is told all his learning is "nothing but name."

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Nāgārjuna** — **2nd-century CE** Buddhist, founder of the Madhyamaka or "middle-way" philosophy; revered in Tibet as "the second Buddha" [00:01:28].
- **Verses on the Middle Way** (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*) — his central work, where dependent origination is identified with emptiness [00:01:51].
- **Refutation of My Opponents** — his second book, answering the charge that emptiness is self-refuting [00:17:48].
- **The Abhidharma school** — fellow Buddhists, Nāgārjuna's main targets, who analyse experience into momentary *dharmas* [00:03:15].
- **Candrakīrti** — his **6th-century** commentator, source of the "conventionalist" reading carried on in Tibet [00:14:13].

## The red door: intrinsic vs. dependent properties
- [00:00:32] The episode opens with the Rolling Stones' "Paint It Black" — "I see a red door and I want it painted black." Mick Jagger's lyric reports his mental state, but it also tells us something about how things *outside* us relate to our mental states. He'd like the door black; it "stubbornly insists on being a jolly red."
- [00:01:08] The commonsense view: the door and its properties are **independent of us** — of our desires and attitudes — and indeed independent of anything else. The door is red *in itself*, just as it is a door in itself, and our perception and knowledge must be true to those independent, intrinsic facts.
- [00:01:24] The **2nd-century CE** Buddhist **Nāgārjuna** would not say that. He challenges the very assumption that there are such independent, freestanding things and properties. Instead, all things are marked by **dependent origination** — which means that they are **empty**. This is the core teaching of **Madhyamaka**, the "middle-way" Buddhist philosophy.
- [00:01:51] As he puts it in the *Verses on the Middle Way*: "whatever is dependently co-arisen, that is explained to be emptiness; that, being a dependent designation, is itself the middle way."

## A controversial teaching with an enormous afterlife
- [00:02:09] Nāgārjuna's emptiness teaching is one of the most intriguing — and controversial — developments in ancient Indian thought. It has been read as mystical and skeptical, as metaphysical and anti-metaphysical, as admirably consistent and deliberately contradictory, and compared to thinkers from the ancient skeptics to Kant, Heidegger and Wittgenstein.
- [00:02:26] Its historical influence was enormous. Madhyamaka enjoyed only **limited success in India** but flourished elsewhere in East Asia, especially **Tibet**, where Nāgārjuna is revered as the **"second Buddha."**

## Who Nāgārjuna is really arguing with
- [00:02:45] His writings are powerfully opposed to the **Vedic schools** of recent episodes — he argues vociferously with **Nyāya** and **Vaiśeṣika**, among others. He seems to have been a contemporary of **Gautama** (the Nyāya founder): there are passages in the *Nyāya-Sūtra* that look as if they have Nāgārjuna in mind, and passages where Nāgārjuna seems to attack Gautama.
- [00:03:07] Yet his *principal* opponents are actually **other Buddhists**, mainly the **Abhidharma** school. One way to see his project: he takes earlier Buddhist skeptical tendencies further than other Buddhists were willing to go — "about as far as anyone could imagine going."
- [00:03:33] It was standard Buddhist fare to reject the reality of certain entities — especially **selves**, but also **wholes**, the classic example being the **chariot** that is nothing more than its parts. The Buddha sometimes seemed to admit such things exist (e.g. warning that persons survive into the next life to reap the **karmic** fruits of their actions), but most Buddhists treated this as a **concession to conventional thinking**, made to teach those who haven't yet grasped the deeper truths.
- [00:04:01] Nāgārjuna makes no such concession. He convicts **all apparent reality** of being empty, or **śūnya**. And we'd better get the idea right, given his own warning: "emptiness wrongly conceived can ruin a slow-witted person" — like a mishandled snake or a poorly executed incantation.

## Emptiness = the denial of svabhāva (self-nature)
- [00:04:30] With emptiness, Nāgārjuna is clearly contributing to **metaphysics**. To say an object is empty means denying that it has what he calls **svabhāva**, or self-nature — and that, in turn, means denying that it is **independent of other things**.
- [00:04:46] Back to the red door. It seems the redness is **intrinsic** to it: Jagger's viewing the door doesn't make it red, nor does anything else — it's just red in itself. By contrast, some properties arise only through a **relation**, e.g. the door's being six feet away from a depressed rock star.
- [00:05:04] In the *Verses on the Middle Way*, Nāgārjuna works to **undermine this contrast** between intrinsic and relative properties. He runs through a wide range of phenomena — whole and part, cause and effect, sensation and the sensed, motion and the moved — and shows that in every case what seems to have a fixed essence is in fact **relative**.
- [00:05:34] When Jagger sees the red door, he depends on the door in order to be **someone who sees**, and the red door depends on him in order to **be seen**. "Sensation is like a child, born of two parents." This is Nāgārjuna's reading of the Buddha's famous teaching that everything in the world is **interdependent** — and he sees rival Buddhist schools as insufficiently committed to it, insufficiently careful to avoid **essentialism**.

## Against the Abhidharma dharmas — and Vaiśeṣika substance
- [00:05:58] Contrast his view with **Abhidharma** Buddhist theories that invoked so-called **dharmas** — analysing all experience into fleeting individual processes. Where an Abhidharmika might say a dharma (e.g. a moment of conscious awareness) has the property of **arising**, Nāgārjuna says this makes no sense.
- [00:06:22] Arising cannot have any **intrinsic reality** that would let it ground the occurrence of the moment of consciousness — because the arising of that moment would itself have to arise or be produced, leading to a **regress**. So insofar as some Abhidharma Buddhists describe dharmas as having independent self-nature, their theory falls prey to his battery of skeptical arguments.
- [00:06:45] The same arguments also destroy a system like **Vaiśeṣika**, which envisions an independently existing **substance** bearing intrinsic properties.

## Not nihilism: the middle way between eternalism and annihilationism
- [00:06:55] What does the theory of emptiness leave behind? Some say, obviously, **nothing** — a few modern scholars and many ancient thinkers associated Nāgārjuna with **nihilism**, the view that nothing is real. But this looks more like polemical criticism than plausible interpretation: his philosophy styles itself a **middle way**, and as the Nāgārjuna expert **Jan Westerhoff** notes, "it's hard to imagine a more extreme and less moderate view than nihilism."
- [00:07:33] Instead he charts a course between two extremes prevalent in his day (and the Buddha's): **eternalism** and **annihilationism**. The **eternalist** is an essentialist who says each object exists independently, with an immutable essence. The **annihilationist** says objects don't exist at all.
- [00:07:56] Nāgārjuna's **middle position**: objects *do* exist, but not by subsisting in themselves. The nature and subsistence of each thing is determined by its **relations to other objects**. So **redness is empty** — not because there's no such thing as red, but because redness always depends on other things, e.g. the door that has the red colour.

## Śūnya = zero: a suggestive analogy
- [00:08:14] The Sanskrit word for empty, **śūnya**, has another technical sense: early Indian mathematicians used it as the name for **zero**. The point of zero in their counting system was to be a **place marker** — in 307, the zero shows the tens place is empty.
- [00:08:44] There's probably no actual historical connection between this mathematical use and Nāgārjuna's philosophical one, but there is an **analogy**. As **Bimal Matilal** remarked, in Madhyamaka, objects are like zeros: they have a value — a claim to reality — only **in relation** to something else or to the position they occupy in a complex, and so have **no absolute value**.

## Words and concepts are empty too — the prasaṅga method
- [00:09:04] One might object: redness can't be like zero, because it needs an **intrinsic essence** to secure the meaning of the word "red," which we'd refer to whenever we use it. But Nāgārjuna insists that our **words, concepts and philosophical theories** have no independent meaning, any more than objects have independent metaphysical properties. The word "redness" plays a role in our language, but there's no distinct thing in the world to which it refers.
- [00:09:45] To unmask the emptiness of words and concepts, he uses a style of argument called **prasaṅga**: showing that a given concept would imply a **contradiction** if it had independent reality or essence.
- [00:09:56] His worked example is the **round square**. "This round square is round" obviously involves a contradiction (a square being round). But I can't *deny* "a round square is round" either — if anything is a round square, it's surely round. The upshot: the round square is **not anything at all**; the phrase has no reference and no real meaning, since granting its reality lands us in absurdity. Nāgārjuna is a master at applying this to apparently **innocuous** cases and showing they too are empty.

## Causation: cause vs. causal condition
- [00:10:34] A nice example is causation. He distinguishes a **cause as such** (that *from which* something originates) from a mere **causal condition** (that *whose occurrence is necessary* for something's existence).
- [00:10:57] The causal-condition idea has an impeccable Buddhist pedigree, appearing in the **four noble truths**: there is suffering; suffering has a cause; removing the causes of suffering ends it; and there is an **eightfold path** for removing those causes.
- [00:11:13] Here the cause of suffering — **attachment** — is a *condition*: something whose continuing presence is necessary for the continuing existence of its effect. So long as you stay attached to things, you continue to suffer.
- [00:11:28] This gives another way to express emptiness: when Nāgārjuna says all things are empty, he means **all things have causal conditions on which they depend**. The existence of each thing depends on something else; **nothing is self-sufficient**.

## The four options for origination (the pot and the potter)
- [00:11:49] An opponent replies that this is obviously false: once a potter makes a pot, the pot is **self-sufficient**; many things originate from their causes and then have **independent existence**. The *Verses on the Middle Way* opens with a *prasaṅga* attack showing that the notion of **origination** itself leads to contradictions, via **four options**:
- [00:12:13] **(1) Self-causation** — a thing originates spontaneously, causing itself. But it would have to **exist first** in order to bring itself into being. Absurd.
- [00:12:29] **(2) Other-causation** — a thing is entirely originated by something else, as the potter makes the pot. But on reflection the pot's continued existence does **not** depend on the potter: the potter can die without the pot vanishing. So something else — e.g. the **clay** — must explain its persistence.
- [00:12:56] **(3) Both** — caused by itself and by something else. No: then one part of the thing depends on itself and another on something else, and we just **re-apply** the previous arguments against self- and other-causation to those two parts.
- [00:13:24] **(4) Neither** — things originate neither from themselves nor from others. No again: this amounts to admitting the thing is **permanent or eternal** rather than originated, giving up the idea that it was caused at all. (Adamson notes Nāgārjuna "is much better at saying no than at saying yes.")
- [00:13:43] The upshot: there's **no option** on which causal origination makes sense; as a concept it fares no better than the round square.

## But everyday talk may survive — the two truths
- [00:13:55] It doesn't follow that Nāgārjuna wants to **ban** all talk of origination. A predominant interpretation — beginning with his **6th-century** commentator **Candrakīrti** and carried on in the later **Tibetan** tradition — claims he's only showing that our concepts are **conventional**: fit for communication and practical purposes. The Madhyamaka critique only bites when these concepts are deployed in **philosophical theories** about how things *really are in themselves*. (Hence the **Wittgenstein** comparison: there's no problem saying "the potter makes the pot" — only when "making" is taken as an aspect of ultimate reality or a theory of causation do we run into trouble.)
- [00:15:01] This is why Madhyamaka puts so much weight on a distinction between **two truths**: truth as it seems in the conventional world of everyday transaction (**saṃvṛtisat**), and truth as things are in reality, "how things really are in themselves quite apart from their utility to us" (**paramārthasat**).
- [00:15:26] This distinction had roots in early attempts to dispel apparent **contradictions** within the Buddha's own teaching; here it is turned to a more ambitious **philosophical — or better, anti-philosophical — purpose**.

## Three ways to read Nāgārjuna
- [00:15:37] **(a) The conventionalist reading:** we cannot get at things in themselves — either because the very notion of an ultimate reality is **incoherent**, or because we simply lack the **cognitive resources** to reach things as they truly are.
- [00:15:55] **(b) The metaphysical reading** (the one suggested earlier in the episode): Nāgārjuna *does* have a metaphysical view — that **reality is always relative**. Conventional concepts and language **misrepresent** things by treating them as independent and intrinsically propertied; the theory of emptiness lets us see things rightly by **exposing their interdependence**.
- [00:16:23] **(c) The thorough-going skeptic reading:** like his near-contemporary the Greek skeptic **Sextus Empiricus**, his arguments are neither negative nor positive but **dialectical and reactive** — solely aimed at undermining other philosophers' concepts and theories, never advancing a position of his own. This fits his famous remark that his teaching involves the **abandonment of all views**.

## The self-refutation test and the *Refutation of My Opponents*
- [00:17:06] A good way to test these readings — and Nāgārjuna as a philosopher — is to ask whether Madhyamaka **collapses into self-refutation**. If all statements are empty of content or reference, the thesis seems to undermine itself: if "all statements are empty" is itself empty, it can be dismissed; if it's *not* empty, it's a **counterexample** to its own claim.
- [00:17:42] Critics must have pressed this in his lifetime — whether Gautama, the Naiyāyika, or the Abhidharma Buddhists. To answer, he wrote a second book, "winningly entitled" the **Refutation of My Opponents**, defusing the charge that the middle way is self-defeating.
- [00:17:59] To the accusation that his arguments achieve nothing if empty, he replies that **the chariot too is empty, but it can still carry wood**. And what would it mean for the emptiness thesis to be itself empty? His answer: he in fact has **no thesis**, no philosophical theory — at least not of the same type as his rivals' systems. He offers **two analogies** to show the difference between his empty statements and the statements that constitute a philosophical system.

## The silence analogy and the artificial-person analogy
- [00:18:30] **The silence analogy.** The opponent says the doctrine of emptiness is like someone who **shouts "Silence!"** — succeeding only in making a noise, defeating his own purpose. Nāgārjuna turns it around: if there *is* silence, we cannot **announce** it; the fact of silence can't be stated out loud. Likewise, if all the concepts we use to make the world intelligible are devoid of meaning, *this* cannot be announced without using those very empty concepts. But that it can't be announced doesn't mean it **isn't true** — just as there can be silence even though no one can observe it without **falsifying the very observation** they're making.
- [00:19:22] **The artificial-person analogy.** The middle-way philosophy is like the action of one **artificial person** preventing the actions of another, or a **magician** doing magic to counter another magician's spell. The point: even though "all theses are empty" is itself empty and so cannot *state* anything, it can still **show or reveal** that this is the true state of affairs. Nāgārjuna doesn't fight fire with fire; he fights **empty statements with more empty statements**.

## Emptiness is not itself a thing
- [00:19:57] These statements do **not** urge us to make emptiness into a kind of metaphysical reality. To claim the door's redness is empty is **not** to say there is "emptiness" there instead of redness — it's to **correct a mistake** about the redness, namely the error of believing redness has independent existence.
- [00:20:16] So the middle way is **not self-refuting**. Nāgārjuna's words imply something that cannot be stated — that the very words he is using are **without foundation**. This isn't just another move in the language game but an **ironic intervention** within it. Like the snake he warned about, he's "clearly a pretty slippery character."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Nāgārjuna** — **2nd-c. CE** Buddhist; founder of Madhyamaka; argues that nothing has independent self-nature, so all things are empty; revered in Tibet as the "second Buddha."
- **śūnyatā / śūnya (emptiness / empty)** — the thesis that nothing has independent existence; an object's nature is fixed only by its relations to other things. (Pun: *śūnya* is also the mathematicians' word for **zero**, a mere place-marker with value only relative to position.)
- **svabhāva (self-nature)** — intrinsic, independent reality; to be empty is precisely to **lack** svabhāva.
- **dependent origination** — the Buddha's teaching that everything is interdependent; Nāgārjuna identifies it with emptiness ("sensation is a child of two parents").
- **Madhyamaka (the middle way)** — Nāgārjuna's school, steering between **eternalism** (essentialism: things have immutable independent essences) and **annihilationism / nihilism** (nothing exists at all).
- **Verses on the Middle Way** — his central work; surveys whole/part, cause/effect, sensation/sensed, motion/moved, and opens with the four-options attack on origination.
- **Refutation of My Opponents** — his second book, answering the self-refutation charge ("the chariot too is empty, but it can carry wood").
- **prasaṅga** — his method of arguing by *reductio*: a concept implies a contradiction if it has independent reality (the **round square**; the four options for origination).
- **Abhidharma** — rival Buddhist school analysing experience into momentary **dharmas**; Nāgārjuna's chief target, since treating a dharma's "arising" as real triggers a regress.
- **two truths — saṃvṛtisat & paramārthasat** — conventional truth (everyday transaction) vs. ultimate truth (how things are in themselves); concepts are fine conventionally but misrepresent ultimate reality.
- **Candrakīrti** — **6th-c.** commentator behind the "conventionalist" reading later dominant in Tibet; concepts are conventional, the critique biting only when they enter philosophical theory (cf. **Wittgenstein**).
- **Sextus Empiricus** — Greek skeptic, near-contemporary; cited for the reading on which Nāgārjuna is purely dialectical, advancing no view ("abandonment of all views").
- **Jan Westerhoff** — modern Nāgārjuna expert; notes nihilism is the *least* moderate possible view, so a "middle way" can't be nihilist.
- **Bimal Matilal** — scholar quoted on the zero analogy: in Madhyamaka, objects are like zeros, with value only relative to position.

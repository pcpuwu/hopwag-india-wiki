## What "Tantra" really means — clearing away the clichés
- [00:00:33] The series has already dispelled misconceptions about ancient India: that it offered only mysticism with no philosophy, that yoga is mere gymnastics, that the physical world is real (per Advaita Vedānta) or that *only* the physical is real (per the Cārvākas). But the word most apt to provoke inaccurate associations is **Tantra**.
- [00:01:00] In Western countries the first and last idea people have about Tantra is that "it has something to do with sex." In India itself it is nowadays often equated with **black magic**, and even better historical knowledge has not spared it disdain and mockery.
- [00:01:13] A pioneering 20th-century Indian scholar justified studying it in pointedly medical terms: someone should take up "the diagnosis, etiology, pathology, and prognosis of the disease, so that more capable men may take up its treatment and eradication." It would be more helpful, the hosts suggest, to think of Tantra instead as a tradition **akin to yoga, in which philosophical ideas were put into practice**.
- [00:01:46] A wide range of rituals could enable the practitioner to achieve **liberation from suffering and oneness with the absolute** — not to mention feats like seeing buried objects, flying, and turning invisible. As with yoga, the connection to magic recurs, and like yoga, Tantra appeared in many guises across periods.

## A tradition cutting across schools — and the focus on Śaivism
- [00:02:08] Tantric literature spans the divides between **Hindu, Buddhist and Jain** traditions; even Hindu Tantra had numerous varieties and schools in the classical period. The episode focuses especially on Tantra within **Buddhism** and on **Śaivism** (worship of the god Śiva), and within Śaivism on the **non-dual** strand represented by **Abhinavagupta** (c. **1000 CE**), familiar from the earlier discussion of the aesthetic theory of *rasa*.
- [00:02:42] The more central aspects of Tantra are shared across these schools. At the risk of disappointing the audience, a fascination with ritualistic, spiritualized sex is *not* one of them. Writings on the topic exist (including by Abhinava), but sexual practices were not the primary focus of classical Tantra — and even when discussed, "there is more, or from a prurient point of view, less, going on than meets the eye." To quote the French scholar **André Padoux**: Abhinava "is not to be confused with the Marquis de Sade."
- [00:03:18] What characterizes Tantra instead is its **openness to participants of all classes**, often both men and women, and a range of concrete ritual practices: meditation, breath control, repetitive chants (**mantras**), the use of **maṇḍalas**, and **initiation by a guru** who serves as spiritual guide.

## The etymology — Tantra as a teaching that spreads and saves
- [00:03:42] If none of that sounds philosophical, that is because it is only the *external* scaffolding that manifests and assists the practitioner's pursuit of wisdom and liberation. A traditional etymology of **Tantra** — which originally meant simply "a teaching" or a document setting out a teaching — even encodes this: the syllable **TAN** means "spreads" and **TRA** means "saves." Tantra spreads the wisdom that saves us from ignorance and suffering.

## Ancient roots and the borrowing question
- [00:04:11] Tantric practice reaches deep into the past. The **Upaniṣads** already discussed mantras, including the central example **Oṃ**. The **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** instructs: "one should venerate the high chant as this syllable, for one begins the high chant with Oṃ. A man who utters this syllable with that knowledge enters this very syllable, the sound that is immortal and free from fear." The same text describes initiation with a guru and stresses the notion of **breath**.
- [00:04:52] But the first texts classifiable as Tantric in the full sense emerge around the **5th to 7th centuries CE**, especially within Śiva-worshipping Hindu belief. The various practices — like reciting mantras — were apparently **borrowed from Śaivism by the Buddhists**.
- [00:05:10] Each side spun an alternative history. Buddhist tantric texts, sensitive on this point, ascribe to **the Buddha** a premonition that rituals he originally prescribed would later be adopted by other schools. Meanwhile a Hindu legend held that Śaivism was a **curse** to punish those who stopped learning the Vedas properly — so Tantra was "being diagnosed as a kind of illness long before the 20th century." Even as the rituals were adopted across the spectrum of Indian belief, writers across that spectrum dismissed them as preposterous.

## Are mantras gibberish? Vasubandhu's objection and the answer
- [00:05:45] No less a thinker than **Vasubandhu** (whom we saw initiating Yogācāra Buddhism) held that **mantras are meaningless gibberish** — and looking at them, "you may feel that he had a point." Some consist of apparent nonsense syllables, each called a **seed** (*bīja*). Others repeat a meaningful but mysterious phrase, like **Haṃsa So'ham** — "I am that divine *haṃsa* bird."
- [00:06:13] Typical of this mantra is that it is a **near palindrome**, a common structure in the many formulas of Tantra (tradition counted **70 million** of them). One standard practice was to begin and end a mantra with **Oṃ** (or another basic mantra), enveloping the rest. **Breath itself** is said to be a kind of mantra, as one breathes in and out thousands of times a day.
- [00:06:40] The symmetrical structure — mimicking the outward and inward movement of breath — is a clue to the underlying **philosophical rationale**, the theory held to be the key to a mantra's success. Recitation *without knowledge* is "like casting seed upon barren stone"; that is why **initiation with a guru**, who reveals the meaning, is needed.
- [00:07:17] In general terms: as the palindromic structure suggests, mantras evoke the **cyclical production of things** — in the first instance the going-out and return of the entire universe (recalling the earlier discussion of world cycles). The scholar **Robert Yelle** wrote that the belief in mantras' efficacy is reinforced by their **imitation of the natural order**: they should be seen not as gibberish like baby-talk or birdsong, but as **poetic language symbolizing the world** and even the very origin of the world.

## Abhinavagupta's cosmology — monism with a complex world
- [00:07:53] The culmination of this thinking comes with **Kashmir Śaivism** and especially **Abhinavagupta**; an important further source is the commentary of the **13th-century** exegete **Jayaratha**. Their extensive writings had little impact on actual religious practice in Kashmir, but represent a "high watermark for philosophical reflection about Tantra." Abhinava sets out a sophisticated theory in which the **generation of words** represents — or *is identical with* — the generation of the entire universe. Understanding it requires first his **cosmology**, generated from one principle (singular).
- [00:08:39] Śaivism had long been **monotheistic** — among the first Indian systems to endorse a single divine principle. That principle, **Śiva**, is a pure, ineffable consciousness experiencing permanent bliss; trying to grasp it, says Abhinava, "is like trying to step on the shadow of your own hat while wearing it."
- [00:09:03] So far this sounds like Advaita Vedānta, and this strand of Śaivism is likewise called **non-dual**, in distinction from the **Saiddhāntika** (Śaiva Siddhānta) variety. The Saiddhāntika Śaivas acknowledged the reality of the physical world but **regretted** it, treating their rites as a means of *escaping* its suffering. For Abhinava and the non-dual Śaivas, the point is instead to **free ourselves of the ignorance** that makes us think we are distinct from the divinity of Śiva (also called spirit, consciousness, self).
- [00:09:39] This is a **monism compatible with a complex cosmology**. The universe arises from a **vibration of self-awareness**, expressing itself threefold as **consciousness, will (desire), and cognition**. This can occur only because Śiva, a male god, is one with the female god **Śakti**, who represents **energy**. As the non-dual Śaivas' punning motto has it: without Śakti, Śiva would be a corpse — **śava**. Thanks to Śakti's energy and self-directed activity, consciousness does not stay inert but goes forth as our multifarious empirical world — yet all things we see are in truth **one with the divine**. The tantric goal is to **realize one's identity with God** through rituals that represent (or simply *are*) the unfolding of all things from Śiva-Śakti.

## Language as the fabric of the universe
- [00:10:50] This returns us to mantras and language. Śiva is also called the **supreme word** (*parā-vāc*), the source of all language but as yet without articulation. This supreme word begins to manifest as the initial vibration of creative energy and self-awareness. As **Jayaratha** puts it: when the supreme word wishes to appear externally but without yet producing the multiplicity of "what expresses and what is expressed," since pure consciousness still prevails, "she is called the seeing or the visionary" — a form of the subject who sees.
- [00:11:24] Think of it as the moment a thought is entertained **all at once**, before being spelled out in words. Abhinava compares it to the **flash of understanding** posited by the grammarian **Bhartṛhari**, in which a sentence's meaning is grasped in a single instant. At a further stage, articulated language is **rehearsed mentally** before being spoken aloud and fully expressed. Abhinava argues there must be such a stage — otherwise children could never learn to speak, nor could mutes comprehend others' words.
- [00:11:58] Language emerges from the body in stages, through its energy centres, the famous **cakras**. Within each person the origin is at the **base of the spine**, where power is coiled like a snake — "coiled" translating the name of the goddess **Kuṇḍalinī**, who is responsible for the **evolution of the phonemes** from the simpler, unspoken primordial word. One text says Kuṇḍalinī is made of will, cognition and action; she "creates the garland of letters divided into 42," and dividing into 50 the garland of phonemes, "she brings forth in succession the gods and the other things."

## The Sanskrit alphabet as a map of cosmic evolution
- [00:12:50] So we have a **fusion of cosmology with philosophy of mind and language**. Crucially, the production of the world is not merely *symbolized* by the production of language but **actually is linguistic** — the universe being "pervaded by sound." Cosmic evolution can therefore be **mapped onto the Sanskrit alphabet**.
- [00:13:09] Those three initial moments in Śiva's emergence into self-awareness — consciousness, will, cognition — are connected to the three short vowels **a, i, u**. **Vowels** are associated with Śiva, the male principle; **consonants** are the female analogue — set out even more concretely as **vowels = the male seed, consonants = the womb**. A single syllable like **Oṃ**, or any vowel-consonant combination, thus represents the **mating and unity of Śiva and Śakti**. This may be Abhinava's gloss on another **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** passage: "as the leaves of a book are bored through by a pen, so all words are bored through by Oṃ."

## The sexual theme — Kaula, left-handed rites, and twilight language
- [00:13:58] This finally reaches the theme of **sexual union** — which plays a far more minor role than expected. Sexual rituals are only one aspect of a more general strand called **Kaula**, which endorsed a range of **shocking practices**: from adultery, incest and group sex to consuming forbidden things like wine and meat, and even the eating and drinking of urine and excrement.
- [00:14:33] One passage, aiming to be "as scandalous as possible," advises on liberation: "Inserting his organ into his mother's womb, pressing his sister's breasts, placing his foot upon his guru's head, the practitioner will be reborn no more." It did not take long for some authors to gloss such advice as **symbolic and allegorical**: incest with the mother as the mind's awareness of the **base of the body**; pressing the sister's breasts as focus on **heart and throat**; the foot on the guru's head as **meditation on the brain**.
- [00:15:11] How far the shocking **"left-handed" practices** were actually carried out is much debated; no doubt it varied — some took the advice literally, others symbolically, still others held both levels relevant. To bar a merely symbolic reading, some works spell out the true meaning of coded terminology, sometimes called **twilight language**.

## Spiritual aims achieved through the body
- [00:15:41] Either way, there is general agreement across Tantra that we can achieve **spiritual aims through the body**, correcting what might have looked like an unwarranted **asceticism** in both Hinduism and Buddhism. One sign: Tantra did **not demand renunciation** — unlike a Buddhist or Jain monk, a tantric practitioner could remain a **householder**. Another: many texts frankly aim at **worldly advantages** — magical feats like invisibility, victory in battle, even alchemy.
- [00:16:18] And even when sex and other rituals were practiced concretely — for one genuinely recites mantras thousands of times — the aims were always **spiritual**. The sexual act became a way of **realizing one's identity with the union of male and female** in Śiva-Śakti, involving long practice of control and often the **avoidance of climax**. As one scholar notes, the sublimation and self-mastery mean that what is occurring "bears very little resemblance to sex as we generally understand it," since Buddhist tantric assumptions and aims are "virtually indistinguishable" from those of recognizably ascetic traditions.

## The key philosophical idea — and its parallels abroad
- [00:17:09] It may seem paradoxical that such lofty aims are pursued through bodily powers. But as Abhinava says, the **body** should be seen as "full of all the paths, composed of all the divinities," and so made an object of contemplation, adoration and ritual fulfillment. This is perhaps **the key philosophical idea of Tantra**: especially in its non-dual Śaivite version, it privileges **pure consciousness as the first principle**, taking the empirical, bodily realm to be that principle's outward manifestation. We are told to engage in worldly activities **structurally identical to the workings of self-directed consciousness** and its production of all things — notably the production of language as mantras — thereby removing the otherwise impermeable **veil** that screens us from realizing our identity with the absolute.
- [00:18:04] No wonder that, when **Islam** came to India, Tantra was seen as congenial to **Sufism**. There are parallels too with **medieval Christian mysticism** and especially **Kabbalah** in Judaism — which offers strong resonances: an **alphabet-based cosmology** (the ten **Sephirot** structuring a derivation of all things from an ineffable divine principle), the deployment of **erotic language**, and, in "prophetic" Kabbalah, even the **chanting of apparently meaningless syllables**. In none of these cases are we dealing with an irrationalism that competes with rational philosophy, but with attempts to show how **philosophical ideas could be put into practice**.

## "Learn by doing" — and where we go next
- [00:18:50] It has been said that while master logicians like **Dignāga** or **Dharmakīrti** devised hair-splitting arguments to interpret the world as a void entity, tantric ideas "captured the heart of Buddhism through the back door." But in fact the tantric authors — Buddhist and Hindu alike — had **their own philosophical theories and fine distinctions**; it is just that, in their view, abstract understanding was **not enough**. We must marry it to **practical action**, recognizing that everyday — even supposedly vulgar and despised — things reflect and instantiate the structures of primordial reality. Sometimes you need to **learn by doing**.
- [00:19:31] Tantra cut across the intellectual divides of ancient India. Soon the series will turn to the relations between that culture and the world outside India. But first comes another pervasive issue across all the traditions covered — one that leaps to mind when people think of ancient Indian religion: **attitudes toward non-human animals**. It was widely considered wrong to eat them, which is why eating meat counted among the shocking left-handed tantric practices — perhaps because Indians believed in **reincarnation**, so that "by tucking into a steak, they might be eating a deceased family member." The next episode is an interview with **Amber Carpenter** about animals in classical Indian thought.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Tantra** — a tradition (like yoga) of putting philosophical ideas into ritual practice; etymology TAN ("spreads") + TRA ("saves") — wisdom that saves from ignorance and suffering. Cuts across Hindu, Buddhist and Jain lines.
- **mantra** — repetitive chant; "seed" (*bīja*) syllables or mysterious phrases like *Haṃsa So'ham*; often near-palindromes mimicking breath; meaningful only with a guru's revealed knowledge.
- **Oṃ** — the central, ancient mantra (from the Upaniṣads); used to envelop other mantras; "all words are bored through by Oṃ."
- **maṇḍala** — diagram used in tantric ritual.
- **guru / initiation** — the spiritual guide who shares a mantra and reveals its meaning; recitation without this knowledge is "seed cast on barren stone."
- **Abhinavagupta** — non-dual Kashmir Śaivite (c. **1000 CE**); theorist that producing language *is* producing the universe; the body as "full of all the paths."
- **Jayaratha** — **13th-century** commentator on Abhinavagupta; describes the "supreme word" becoming "the visionary."
- **Śiva** — for Śaivism the single divine principle: pure, ineffable consciousness in permanent bliss.
- **Śakti** — the female god, energy; "without Śakti, Śiva would be a corpse (*śava*)"; her activity makes consciousness go forth as the world.
- **non-dual Śaivism vs. Saiddhāntika** — the non-dual aims to dissolve the ignorance of feeling separate from Śiva; the Saiddhāntika accept the world's reality but seek to escape its suffering.
- **Kuṇḍalinī** — "coiled" goddess at the base of the spine; evolves the phonemes (the 42, then 50) and so brings forth gods and things.
- **cakras** — the body's energy centres through which language emerges in stages.
- **Bhartṛhari** — grammarian; his "flash of understanding" (a sentence grasped at once) is Abhinava's model for the pre-articulate moment of language.
- **vowels / consonants** — vowels (a, i, u) = Śiva / male seed; consonants = Śakti / womb; a syllable enacts their union.
- **Kaula** — strand endorsing shocking "left-handed" practices (sex, wine, meat, even urine and excrement), often read symbolically via "twilight language."
- **Vasubandhu** — Yogācāra Buddhist cited as a critic who dismissed mantras as meaningless gibberish.
- **André Padoux / Robert Yelle** — modern scholars of Tantra invoked for, respectively, "Abhinava is not the Marquis de Sade" and mantras as world-symbolizing poetic language.

## Karma even inspires dance music
- [00:00:32] Today's episode: "You Are What You Do — Karma." Adamson opens with a joke: it's the rare philosophical concept that inspires dance music. You won't find Kool & the Gang meditating on determinism, or Madonna setting a reductionist theory of mind to a disco beat.
- [00:00:54] The exception is Culture Club's **1983** hit *Karma Chameleon* — but it may be the exception that proves the rule, since a close reading of the lyrics betrays a shaky grasp of the concept. Boy George complains, "When you go, you're gone forever," which is exactly missing the point.
- [00:01:14] The ancient Indian notion of **karma** turns precisely on the idea that *when we go, we are not gone forever*. We will be reborn, and the actions we performed in this life will shape our fate in the next. The consequences of this idea for Indian philosophy and religion can hardly be exaggerated.

## Rebirth as re-death, and the project of eliminating karma
- [00:01:30] Rebirth *sounds* optimistic — surely we all fear death and would welcome living forever, even in another form. But that's not how many ancient Indians saw it. Their goal was to *escape* the cycle of rebirth, which is, after all, just as much a cycle of **re-death**.
- [00:01:55] Since karma is the factor that ties one to the next incarnation, the only route to liberation seemed to be to **eliminate all karma** from oneself.
- [00:02:05] Extreme measures followed. The early **Jainas** devised a practice that has been called *immobility asceticism*: after years of training they would try to stay completely still until they died of thirst, hunger, or exposure. The point was to avoid *any* action whatsoever — since all action, good or bad, produces karma — and the suffering of this agonizing death would cleanse the karma acquired earlier in life.
- [00:02:37] Another dissenting group, the **Ājīvikas**, were more pessimistic: the cycle of rebirth simply cannot be escaped, and there is no point trying to affect one's karma. Each individual just has to wait until the cycle of incarnations runs out — which the Ājīvikas expected to take *millions of years*. "And you thought Indra showed patience."

## Where did karma come from? The rice-trail argument
- [00:03:00] Karma and rebirth already appear in the earliest **Upaniṣads**, so it's natural to tie the idea to the Brahmanic tradition rather than these dissident groups. But the origins are debated.
- [00:03:14] The doctrine is *not* found in the **Vedas** themselves — the even earlier texts the Upaniṣads are reacting to. Some scholars assume a Vedic background anyway; others think karma is an "interloper" into Brahmanic culture.
- [00:03:31] On the interloper view, the notion of karma already existed in the northeastern region around the valley of the **Ganges**. When the upholders of Brahmanism migrated in from their homelands in the **Indus** Valley, they fell under the influence of this older tradition and absorbed karma into their teachings.
- [00:03:50] The debate has turned on surprising details — for instance the prevalence of **rice metaphors** for the cycle of rebirth, and the use of rice in rituals meant to manipulate karma. Rice is not a crop of the Indus region (where farmers grew wheat); it is characteristic of agriculture in the Ganges. This fine-grained historical analysis could support karma being an indigenous eastern idea rather than a Brahmanic one.
- [00:04:26] Wherever it was born, karma was — fittingly — *reborn* in the Upaniṣads.

## The secret doctrine: Yājñavalkya in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka
- [00:04:32] Adamson quotes again from the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad**. A man dies and his parts disperse — speech into fire, breath into the wind, sight into the sun, mind into the moon, hearing into the quarters, body into the earth, soul into space, blood and seed into water. The question: *what happens to that person?*
- [00:05:02] **Yājñavalkya** replies, "My friend, we cannot talk about this in public. Take my hand, let's discuss it in private." They leave and talk — and they talk about *nothing but karma*, and praise *nothing but karma*: "A man turns into something good by good action, and into something bad by bad action."
- [00:05:28] This is one of the *hidden* teachings that give the Upaniṣads their name. The secret doctrine, not fit for public consumption, was karma: **we are made into what we are by what we do**, and in particular it is the *moral quality* of what we do that shapes the life we will enjoy or suffer.
- [00:05:50] So here, in an early Upaniṣad, is the core idea most people (other than Boy George) associate with karma: *be good, or else.* Your moral decisions now will bring reward or punishment down the line — in the next life if not in this one.

## Is karma just moral retribution — or a prop for caste?
- [00:06:02] But is the theory merely an account of moral retribution? That doesn't sound very appealing — or very philosophical. And it gets worse.
- [00:06:15] It's tempting to read karma as a justification for the **inequality** of Brahmanic culture: the **Brahmans** rewarded for good deeds in a former life, while members of the lower castes — and *all women* — are being punished for crimes they must have committed in past lives.
- [00:06:32] The **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** supplies the material: those of pleasant conduct enter a pleasant womb, "like that of a **Brahman**, **Kṣatriya**, or **Vaiśya**," whereas those of foul behavior enter the foul womb of "a dog, a pig, or an outcast."
- [00:06:51] But, Adamson insists, this would be a reductive and inadequate understanding. Properly grasped, the doctrine is a way to **affirm human freedom** and to give us a *motivation* for acting well. To see why, it helps to set karma against two rival theories.

## Rival one: Ājīvika fatalism (Gosāla)
- [00:07:04] The first rival is held by the unorthodox ascetic **Gosāla**, leader of the **Ājīvikas**, who denied the cycle of rebirth can be escaped. Gosāla held that *everything happens exactly as fate predestines it* — so human action is ineffective and inconsequential.
- [00:07:29] The Ājīvika tradition did not survive past antiquity (unlike the other schools discussed), but Gosāla's views are recorded in the dialogues of the **Buddha**: "There is no deed performed by oneself or by others, no human action, no strength, no courage, no endurance or prowess. All beings are developed by destiny, chance, and nature." The cycle of birth and rebirth is "measured as with a bushel," fixed in length — it "can neither be lessened nor increased." Just as a thrown ball of thread unwinds to its full length, "so fool and wise alike will take their course and make an end of sorrow."
- [00:08:10] So the Ājīvikas denied that we bear any responsibility for our actions — indeed they could be read as denying that *action is possible at all*, if acting involves making rational choices.
- [00:08:24] How then should one live, if reason as a guide to action is a myth? Gosāla's answer is **asceticism**: withdraw as far as possible from the pretense of action, and live as a renunciate and forest-dweller. This was embraced especially by Ājīvikas who claimed their eons of waiting were finally over and they were living their *last* incarnation.

## Rival two: Cārvāka materialism
- [00:08:54] The opposite conclusion was drawn by the materialist philosophers known as the **Cārvāka**. Their ideas too are reported in the Buddhist canon, where they insist there is *no good or evil* in the world and that a human is nothing more than flesh and blood.
- [00:09:11] "Man is formed of the four elements. When he dies, earth returns to earth, water to water, fire to fire, air to air, and the senses vanish into space." Four pallbearers carry the corpse to the burning ground, gossiping about the dead man, "where his bones turn the color of a dove's wing, and his sacrifices end in ashes." Those who preach almsgiving, and those who maintain the existence of immaterial categories, "speak vain and lying nonsense."
- [00:09:45] So for the Cārvāka there are **no reasons to act morally, because there are no moral reasons for action** at all. As later episodes will show, this lent itself to a hedonist ethic: deliberative action is possible, but only *one* thing can guide our deliberations. Like Madonna, the Cārvāka were living in a material world, setting their sights on no goal more exalted than **pleasure**.

## Karma as the middle path: deliberative, moral action
- [00:10:13] What is the alternative to Ājīvika fatalism and Cārvāka moral skepticism? First, to hold that there *is* such a thing as **deliberative action** — action governed by reason and reflection. Second, to believe that **moral considerations** should play a role in that deliberation.
- [00:10:32] To counter fate and chance, one must show there are *moral motivations* for action. That is why karma was taught as "a simple and effective solution to a tricky problem": the doctrine requires that a person's own character be his own destiny, and that each action has its appropriate consequences. Good actions — springing from a clear-sighted mind and a virtuous disposition — yield future happiness; bad actions — from ignorance, greed, and malice — produce only suffering.
- [00:11:12] Crucially, for these consequences to bear on *my* choices, I must believe the results will affect *me*, not someone else. In Sanskrit terms, the **kartṛ** (the *agent* of an action) is the same person as the **bhoktṛ** (the *enjoyer* of its result).
- [00:11:35] So karma implies — or perhaps just *assumes* — that the world is governed by **justice**: each person is guaranteed to reap what he or she sows.
- [00:11:48] This may look like a religious conviction, but as the last episode showed, divine oversight was invoked only gradually. In the Upaniṣads it appears to be a *brute fact* that there is a moral order built into the universe; only later are divinities made responsible for dispensing justice by karmic merit and demerit. Early on, the world is simply a just place — "the law of karma is one that needs no lawgiver."

## Objection one: is this just naked self-interest?
- [00:12:24] But is this really morality on firm foundations, or just a naked appeal to **self-interest**? Each of us is told: behave well, since you and you alone will suffer the consequences of misbehaving — or, more cheerfully, if you want to flourish, behave well. The goal set before me is *my own* long-term happiness; virtue is a mere instrument for getting it.
- [00:12:54] It's welcome news that the world rewards the good and punishes the bad — the vicious winding up as dogs and pigs while the virtuous get "a womb with a view upon happiness." But the doctrine seems to give us no reason to exercise genuine **altruism**, no moral concern for others' interests. If I am only optimizing my own future well-being, I make no effort to further anyone else's.
- [00:13:17] The **Buddha** had a simple and brilliant answer: he denied there is any metaphysical distinction between **self and others**. Concern for others and concern for oneself are therefore one and the same. (The Buddhist theory of *no-self* gets fuller treatment later.) For now: it reconciles karma with altruism by *extending the scope of self-interest*.

## Objection two: but the wicked prosper
- [00:13:49] A more straightforward objection: it just isn't *true* that good conduct is the best route to happiness. We regularly see the good suffer and the wicked flourish; obviously I can advance my own interests by cheating and lying. Things would go badly if *everyone* did so, but in a world where people generally behave well, the clever and wicked prosper at the expense of the upright.
- [00:14:15] Here the apparently religious side of the doctrine — **rebirth / transmigration** — earns its ethical keep. If I worry not only about this life but the next, my calculations of what is truly advantageous change. It's hardly a winning strategy to secure riches and pleasure by devious means now, if it means being born as a dog next time.
- [00:14:47] But this *postpones* the problem rather than solving it. How can we be sure justice will be meted out in the next life for sins committed now? We still need a *guaranteed* connection between good action and what is good for the individual.

## The Bhagavad-Gītā: act, but desire no fruit
- [00:15:03] That guaranteed connection is forged in one of the most famous writings of ancient India, the **Bhagavad-Gītā** — the dialogue between **Arjuna** and **Kṛṣṇa** embedded in the **Mahābhārata**.
- [00:15:09] On the eve of a great battle, the warrior prince **Arjuna** is racked with doubt: across the field are not nameless enemies but his own kinsfolk and elders. They are guilty of heinous crimes, and both duty and social convention favor fighting — yet he cannot persuade himself it is right to kill his own family, preferring "the life of a beggar and a mendicant."
- [00:15:41] **Kṛṣṇa** intervenes to convince him he must fight, and in doing so *rejects the instrumental reasoning bound up with the karma theory*. Any action undertaken out of **desire for a result** only leads to further desires; whatever the outcome, this way of thinking ends only in suffering — one can never satisfy all one's desires, and the more one acts hoping for rewards, the more one is liable to disappointment. As Kṛṣṇa puts it: "From attachment springs desire; from desire is anger born."
- [00:16:18] The karma theory's goal was to guide deliberation by moral considerations *in order to* achieve long-term benefit. Kṛṣṇa now argues that none of this matters: whatever goal you choose to desire, you will find only further discontent.
- [00:16:36] How then should one be motivated to act, if not by desire for results? Kṛṣṇa's answer: the truly virtuous action does not aim at *any* objective, in this life or another — it is **action free from any desire for any result**. "Action alone is your proper business, never the fruits it may produce. Let not your motive be the fruit of action."
- [00:17:02] But how do I know *which* action to perform, if not by consulting a cosmic system of reward and punishment? Simple: one must do what is natural or proper to oneself — one's **svadharma**. "Here is just how a man perfects himself, by doing and rejoicing in his proper work."
- [00:17:24] So the Gītā tells us we *must* act, but without forming a desire to be fulfilled by acting. Arjuna is to act according to his **duty and nature** while remaining **detached** from any self-interest in the results. To act in a way true to one's character is to act well. The karmic consequences may still turn out good — but one should not be *motivated* by them.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **karma** — action and its moral fruits; "you are what you do." The moral quality of one's deeds shapes the life one will enjoy or suffer, in this incarnation or the next.
- **rebirth / transmigration** — the cycle in which one is reborn; experienced as a cycle of *re-death* to be escaped, not an optimistic immortality.
- **kartṛ / bhoktṛ** — the *agent* of an action and the *enjoyer* of its result; karma assumes they are the same person, so consequences fall on the one who acts.
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad** — gives the "secret" doctrine of karma in the Yājñavalkya dialogue: a man becomes good by good action, bad by bad.
- **Yājñavalkya** — the sage who insists the teaching on what survives death must be discussed privately — and it turns out to be karma.
- **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** — source of the "pleasant womb / foul womb" passage (Brahman, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya vs. dog, pig, outcast), used — Adamson argues, reductively — to cast karma as caste-justification.
- **Jainas** — practitioners of "immobility asceticism": staying motionless until death to perform *no* action and so burn off all karma.
- **Ājīvikas** — fatalist school holding rebirth is inescapable and karma cannot be affected; one simply waits out millions of years of incarnations.
- **Gosāla** — leader of the Ājīvikas; preached that all is fixed by destiny, chance, and nature, so action is inconsequential — recorded in the Buddha's dialogues.
- **Cārvāka** — materialists who reduce the human to the four elements, deny good and evil, and (later) embrace a pleasure-seeking, hedonist ethic.
- **Buddha** — answers the altruism objection by denying any metaphysical self/other distinction, so self-interest and concern for others coincide (the no-self theory, treated later).
- **Bhagavad-Gītā** — the Arjuna–Kṛṣṇa dialogue in the Mahābhārata; reframes karma by urging action with no desire for its fruits.
- **svadharma** — one's own proper duty/nature; the Gītā's guide to which action to perform once results are no longer the motive.
- **Mahābhārata** — the great epic containing the Bhagavad-Gītā; one of the foundational texts the dissident schools react to.

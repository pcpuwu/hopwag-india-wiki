## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **The Upaniṣads** — Vedic-era texts; the opening quote is from the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad [00:00:17].
- **Nāgārjuna** — radical 2nd-c. CE Buddhist, used as the proof-case that India does philosophy [00:04:53].
- **The Mahābhārata**, esp. its *Śānti Parvan* ("Book of Peace") and the *Bhagavad-Gītā* [00:11:23].
- **Jonardon Ganeri** — Adamson's co-author and the India expert behind the scripts [00:02:48].

## What this series is, and what counts as "philosophy"
- [00:00:17] Opens with the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad*: "It is one's self which one should see and hear… for by seeing and hearing one's self… one gains knowledge of this whole world." We are *exhorted* to philosophize — and promised that examining the self (**ātman**) leads to understanding everything.
- [00:01:11] The catch: the word "philosophy" never appears in the Upaniṣads — *philosophia* is Greek. So historians usually restrict "philosophy" to the tradition traceable to the Greeks (pre-Socratics → Plato & Aristotle → Romans, medievals, moderns: "Western philosophy").
- [00:01:53] But that's already too narrow: the Islamic *falsafa* saw themselves as heirs of Plato and Aristotle, and the greatest of them, **Avicenna**, hailed from what is now Afghanistan — hardly "Western." The earlier *History of Philosophy* podcast covered everyone who *used the word* philosophy.
- [00:02:42] This series goes further: a tradition that arose **independently of Greek culture** — India. Adamson explains the division of labour [00:02:48]: Ganeri has "detailed knowledge of Indian thought," Adamson "has a microphone." Ganeri took the lead on what to cover and wrote the first draft of each script; it's Adamson's voice you hear. The India episodes get a **separate feed and numbering**, with the hope of later doing Africa and China the same way. India is "not a spin-off or an afterthought."

## India really does philosophy — the Nāgārjuna test case
- [00:04:53] To show Indian texts bear "all the hallmarks of philosophy," Adamson reaches for **Nāgārjuna's *Vigrahavyāvartanī*** ("Refutation of My Opponents," 2nd c. CE). Nāgārjuna defends his trademark thesis: nothing has **svabhāva** (independent, intrinsic reality); all things are dependent and relative — "empty."
- [00:05:24] In a move "as fundamental to Indian philosophy as to the medieval European universities," he raises objections to his own view and answers them. The opponent tries the oldest trick in the book — **self-refutation**: if *nothing* has independent reality, then the statement "nothing has independent reality" is itself empty, so it asserts nothing. And it can't be a purely negative denial either, since even a denial must "be something existent."
- [00:06:19] Nāgārjuna's reply: his statement is **not a thesis at all**, positive or negative. His analogy [00:06:30]: it's like one unreal magical being blocking the action of another unreal magical being. (Whether that response works is deferred to a later episode.)
- [00:06:41] The point: this is "indisputably philosophy of a broadly familiar kind" — a realist vs. a skeptic, charges of self-refutation, careful distinctions between speech-acts, creative analogies. More broadly [00:07:04], Indian thinkers tackle the whole menu: foundations of knowledge, reducing reality to a few fundamental entities (or, for Nāgārjuna, to *none*), atomism, time and space, logic, ethics, political legitimacy.

## Tempting parallels — and why we'll mostly resist them
- [00:07:47] The resonances with Greece are striking: Nāgārjuna recalls the equally radical **Sextus Empiricus** (also 2nd c. CE); the pleasure-loving materialist **Cārvāka** school is often compared to the **Epicureans**.
- [00:08:10] Such echoes may be no coincidence — Alexander reached India, and Greek skeptics like **Pyrrho** reputedly travelled east for wisdom. But influence is usually said to flow *westward* (India inspiring Pyrrho, later **Plotinus**, much later **Schopenhauer**).
- [00:08:50] Method decision: apart from one later episode on Indian influence in Europe, the series will study **Indian thought on its own terms**, with only occasional comparisons — and stay open to the idea that it may "challenge and expand our notion of what philosophy is, and could be."

## "Begin at the end": philosophy as a way of life
- [00:09:10] Back to the Upaniṣadic exhortation: reflect on the self *in order to know the world*. This implies philosophy isn't idle theory or "technical virtuosity for its own sake." Even Nāgārjuna's radical skepticism [00:09:49] served the core Buddhist goal of **avoiding suffering** — seeing things as unreal aids detachment.
- [00:10:22] That a **highest good** exists, that it is a *way of living*, and that doing philosophy is part of it — this is "not what students are typically told on the first day." Far from making India alien, this brings it *closer* to the roots of European philosophy.
- [00:10:29] Ancient philosophy "begins at the end" — with a conception of our **telos** (Aristotle's final goal of life). Adamson invokes **Pierre Hadot** and **Michel Foucault**: the Greeks saw philosophy as a way of life built around "spiritual exercises" — like an athlete's training or an invalid's medicine, but for the health of the soul.

## The highest good: taming the self
- [00:11:23] The concrete Indian example: the **Śānti Parvan** ("Book of Peace"), a vast philosophical stretch inside the even vaster epic **Mahābhārata**. It asks point-blank: *what is the highest good?* Answer [00:12:01]: **the taming of the self** — subduing the self's reaching-out toward things external to it (both physically outside and outside one's control).
- [00:12:24] The taming is a "drawing in," a restraint — echoed in the **Bhagavad-Gītā**'s image [00:12:30] of withdrawing the senses "as a tortoise withdraws its limbs." It means replacing fear, anger, and envy with **imperturbability** before pain or pleasure.
- [00:12:48] Reaching outward springs from greed — chasing things beyond our control — which breeds thwarted desire and anger. With greed checked, one lives "fearless in the face of death… delighting in no acquisition and pained by no loss." Such a life needs no further goal: "living well is its own reward."
- [00:13:25] Is this at odds with the Upaniṣads (seek knowledge of the self) vs. the Book of Peace (restrain the self)? Only apparently [00:13:39]: greed *is* a kind of ignorance — failure to see that nothing one grasps will last. Knowledge brings forbearance and tolerance.
- [00:14:11] The recommended state is **tapas** — which, Adamson jokes, "makes it sound as though enlightenment is available at any good Spanish restaurant," but means **spiritual austerity**. The tamed one is the **dānta**; their condition is perfect **praśama** (tranquility) [00:15:02]. The drawback — being seen by others as weak — is dissolved by the very tolerance self-knowledge brings.

## The charge of world-denial — and its rebuttal
- [00:15:11] The worry: doesn't "taming" threaten to *extinguish* the self altogether — "the only thing you need to know about the self is that there isn't one"? This feeds the old accusation that Indians located the highest good *outside* life — the "holy naysayers" **Nietzsche** mocked in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* [00:15:52], for whom philosophy is "an elaborate escape kit."
- [00:16:15] But things have changed since Nietzsche. Talk of "the distinctively Western" vs. "the distinctively Indian" is "the territory of invention rather than discovery" — contrasts like life-affirmers vs. world-deniers are "too tidy to have any basis in historical fact."
- [00:16:50] Yes, much is said about transcendent goals — **mokṣa, mukti, nirvāṇa** — but in practice these are for the chosen few; most people aspire to something nearer. And this is no different from China or Greece: the **Stoics** too made the **sage** a remote ideal [00:17:15]. Human beings don't actually try to *become* sages; the sage "is an ideal to steer by." Buddhists aren't really trying to become a Buddha — *thinking* of it that way is itself a spiritual exercise that helps them lead a good Buddhist life [00:17:45].
- [00:18:04] This explains why ideal states get described in negative, "unappealing" terms — free from pain, anger, desire, *and* often pleasure. Perhaps that very unappeal shows these aren't meant as literal descriptions of the human good [00:18:35], but as prompts: reflecting on a life devoid of pleasure and pain helps us **re-examine pleasure and pain themselves** [00:19:05]. Believing the ideal involves no pleasure lets one be "nourished by the pleasures I do have" without being driven to chase new ones — i.e., a life of **restraint and self-control**.

## Where we go next
- [00:19:29] The earliest answers to these questions are in **Vedic literature** — but before diving in, the next episode gives "a second introduction": an overview of how philosophy developed in India, "a whirlwind tour" to set the context. "Prepare to feel like Dorothy in the Wizard of Oz."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **ātman** — the self; reflecting on it is the route to knowing the whole world (Upaniṣadic claim).
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad** — "Great Forest" Upaniṣad; source of the opening exhortation to examine the self.
- **falsafa / Avicenna** — the Greek-descended philosophers of the Islamic world; Avicenna (from modern Afghanistan) shows "Western" is a poor label for the tradition.
- **Nāgārjuna** — 2nd-c. CE Buddhist; in the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* he defends that nothing has independent reality and answers the self-refutation objection by denying he holds any thesis.
- **svabhāva** — intrinsic / independent reality; Nāgārjuna says nothing has it ("emptiness").
- **Cārvāka** — Indian materialist, pleasure-friendly school, compared to the Epicureans.
- **telos** — Aristotle's "final end" of life; ancient philosophy "begins" with a conception of it.
- **spiritual exercises** — Hadot & Foucault's idea of philosophy-as-training-for-the-soul, paralleled in India.
- **Śānti Parvan ("Book of Peace")** — philosophical section of the *Mahābhārata*; says the highest good is "taming the self."
- **Bhagavad-Gītā** — part of the *Mahābhārata*; the "tortoise withdrawing its limbs" image of sense-restraint.
- **tapas** — spiritual austerity; the state of soul the Book of Peace recommends.
- **dānta / praśama** — the "tamed one" and the perfect "tranquility" that is their condition.
- **mokṣa / mukti / nirvāṇa** — transcendent goals of liberation; ideals "to steer by," in practice pursued fully by only a few.

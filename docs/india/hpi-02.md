## Not Greece, but all of Europe: the right scale for India
- [00:00:40] Episode 1 flagged the temptation of comparing Indian philosophy to classical Greek philosophy — a natural thought, even setting aside possible historical contact, since both are ancient civilizations long seen as founts of wisdom, and it's "hardly an insult" to be compared to the tradition of **Parmenides, Socrates, Plato and Aristotle**.
- [00:01:04] But given India's geographical size, its population, and the fact that Indian culture *outlived* that of the ancient Greeks and Romans, a different comparison is more legitimate: think of the history of philosophy in India as analogous to the **entire history of philosophy in Europe** — its bewildering range of periods, long-running debates, sudden paradigm shifts, dozens of major and countless minor (still poorly studied) figures.
- [00:01:40] We should set expectations to that scale. Doing the topic full justice "would require hundreds of podcasts" — nearly **250 episodes** were needed for Greece, Rome, the Islamic world and part of medieval Christendom. India won't be quite that detailed, but the promise holds: tell the story "without any gaps," not skipping from one big name to the next and leaving out whole swathes just because they don't fit our prior idea of what matters.
- [00:02:23] The aim is a balanced, ecumenical picture. As in Europe, the texts are in many languages — **Pali, Prakrit, Sanskrit, Malayalam, Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Persian, Arabic, Urdu, Bengali, Kannada, Punjabi, Hindi, Tibetan, Assamese** — and, from British colonial times, English. Indian philosophy spans religious divides (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Islam the most dominant) and many schools and factions; its themes include philosophy of law, medicine, mathematics and politics, even if the most prominent debates are in philosophy of mind, language, epistemology and metaphysics. Ideal coverage would include dissenters, heretics and sceptics, and reach beyond India's borders toward the Persianate world, Tibet, China and the lands of **Theravāda** Buddhism.

## How far we'll get: the first thousand years
- [00:03:46] How far the series will get is uncertain, but there are concrete plans to reach at least the revolutionary Buddhist thinker **Dignāga**, who died in the mid-**6th century CE**. Since the story begins with the **Upaniṣads** and other developments from the middle of the **first millennium BCE**, that covers "at least the first thousand years or so" of Indian philosophy.
- [00:04:11] This is no slight on the *second* millennium, equally packed with development — that later thought gets touched on in this very episode, which offers a general overview of the whole history.

## Against "classical": a home-grown periodization
- [00:04:26] A second temptation now looms: speaking of a **"classical period"** of Indian thought. That would reinforce the India–Greece parallel, but "classical" sits badly on a tradition marked by such exceptional **longevity and continuity** compared with other classical civilizations.
- [00:04:51] So — unlike Oscar Wilde, "who could resist everything except temptation" — we'll insist on a way of dividing India's past *not* borrowed from a history of Western civilization. The cue comes from Episode 1's theme of **philosophy as an art of living**.

## First period: philosophies of path and purpose (~8th c. BCE – 2nd c. CE)
- [00:05:14] The opening period strongly features philosophy as a way of life, a vehicle for **liberation or release from suffering** — texts and ideas from roughly the **8th century BCE to the 2nd century CE**, a time of what Adamson calls "philosophies of path and purpose."
- [00:05:36] The story begins with the ancient wisdom of the **Vedas**, set down well before even this earliest period — the beliefs of the people who called themselves the **Aryans**, offering prescriptions for ritual and counting as scripture or revelation, and seen as the ultimate source of the religion now called **Hinduism**.
- [00:06:04] Philosophical reflection on the Vedas began early with the **Upaniṣads**, "beautiful and majestic" works that comment on and extrapolate from the Vedic texts, articulating the unity of humanity, ritual and cosmos. Then came the grand Hindu epic, the **Mahābhārata** — some ten times the length of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* combined — which contains, as an inserted interlude, the famous "Song of the Lord," the **Bhagavad-Gītā**. The Gītā deserves its fame, but there is interesting philosophy elsewhere in the epic too.
- [00:06:46] Indian philosophical culture was never monolithic; it was marked by "disagreeability." The early period already shows this, as two schools arose to challenge the values and beliefs of the Vedas. One was **Buddhism**: the **Buddha** probably lived after the earliest Upaniṣads were written, and the phrase "path and purpose" is partly inspired by him — the last of his **four noble truths** is the *truth of the path*, the path leading from suffering to **nirvāṇa**, a state of health free from spiritual as well as physical pain.
- [00:07:29] That goal of liberation was shared by the **Jaina** school, famous for its ethic of absolute non-violence. Both schools, though present already in the early period, stay with us throughout, dividing into sub-traditions and producing ever more sophisticated and disputatious texts — rivalry both within and between schools.

## A warning about dates
- [00:07:54] Before moving on, a caution: this narrative has "more uncertain dates than a junior high school dance." Precise chronology for specific thinkers and texts is rarely available; the website offers a timeline, but for India the dates are highly speculative.
- [00:08:25] Usually the best one can hope for is a **firm relative chronology** — if one text cites another, it is later than the text it quotes — but often not even that is possible. Worse, attaching an author's *name* to a text is tricky: many works are compilations edited and re-edited over long periods (already true of the Upaniṣads, passed down orally for centuries before being written). Traditional authors may be mere literary fictions, or a famous name may be attached to a text to lend it clout.
- [00:09:07] Even the **Buddha's** date is controversial. Tradition says he died in **486 BCE** aged 80; modern scholarship pushes that forward, perhaps to around **400 BCE** or later — though a recent excavation of the Mahādevī temple at his birthplace, **Lumbinī**, has unearthed evidence that the traditional date may be right after all. His near-contemporary **Mahāvīra**, founder of Jainism, lived perhaps a little earlier and shared the Buddha's spirit of defiance against the social and intellectual status quo.

## Second period: the Age of the Sūtra (~100 BCE – 350 CE)
- [00:10:02] The attempts to meet that challenge define a second period, the **Age of the Sūtra**, dated (for the sake of having dates) from about **100 BCE to 350 CE**. For listeners keeping score: the first period (Upaniṣads, Mahābhārata, the rise of Buddhism and Jainism) is contemporaneous with the **Presocratics, Plato and Aristotle and the Hellenistic schools** / classical Greece and the Roman Republic; the Age of the Sūtra is roughly contemporary with the **revival of Platonism and Aristotelianism**, carrying through the lifetimes of **Plotinus and Augustine**.
- [00:10:57] *Sūtra* means **"thread"**: it is both a single philosophical aphorism and an entire collection of them. Such texts sound disparate — "more tangle than tapestry" — yet this was in fact a period of **system building**, seen less in the sūtras themselves than in the **commentaries** written on them.
- [00:11:29] These commentaries, called **bhāṣya**, set out explicitly to construct a systematic body of concepts, "weaving the threads into a single cloth." The first commentators — **Śabara, Vātsyāyana, Śaṅkara** among them — became the founders, and sometimes the namesakes, of long-lived traditions.

## The six systems — and why the map is biased
- [00:11:54] It's here that people speak of the **six systems of Indian philosophy** emerging from the sūtra literature: **Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta**. The last two take their names from the **Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra** and **Vedānta-Sūtra**, works that tried to explain the meaning of the earlier Vedic literature.
- [00:12:28] We'll attend to all six — but again dislike how the terrain is usually mapped. The "six systems" classification has a strong bias toward mainstream Hinduism's commitment to the **veracity of the Vedas**, ignoring less mainstream movements and all the dissenters, especially **Buddhists and Jains** — to say nothing of naturalistic materialists like **Bṛhaspati**, author of yet another sūtra that named a tradition, the **Cārvāka-Sūtra**.
- [00:13:00] All these divergent movements belong to a general trend: philosophers crystallizing their wisdom in systematic treatises. They did not abandon the idea that philosophy is a path to the highest good — but the new primary task was to **describe humankind, the world, and the human capacity to know it**. In the tradition's language, the earlier preoccupation with the **self** is now joined by an interest in **pramāṇas** (the sources of knowledge) and **prameyas** (the world as it is known).
- [00:13:36] The period also changed the very *language* of philosophy. Until the **2nd century CE**, Buddhist and Jaina works were typically composed in **Pali and Prakrit** — close to Sanskrit, but not destined to be the main vehicle of intellectual debate. When these schools switched to **Sanskrit**, the implications were far-reaching: the Vedic system-builders, now confronted on their own linguistic ground, had to defend their ideas as never before, doing so by adapting their antique texts while "shamelessly borrowing and appropriating" Buddhist and Jaina ideas.

## Third period: Buddhist analysis and Jaina synthesis (~150 – 550 CE)
- [00:14:18] The result was a third period, completing the first millennium, running from about **150 to 550 CE** — an era of **Buddhist analysis and Jaina synthesis**. Among the notable Buddhists were **Nāgārjuna** and his immediate disciple **Āryadeva**. Buddhism is no more monolithic than Indian philosophy as a whole: Nāgārjuna initiated one of the **four main schools**, the **Madhyamaka** ("middle way") school.
- [00:14:59] Another school, the **Sarvāstivāda**, was systematized in this period by **Vasubandhu**. Before him, the great **Buddhaghosa** had studied all the existing **Theravāda** commentaries in Sri Lanka and written a brilliant synopsis in Pali. The final school is **Yogācāra**, the school of "Buddhist epistemologists," whose inventor was **Dignāga**, who lived at the end of the period, dying about **540 CE**.
- [00:15:29] Dignāga studied and taught at the great Buddhist university of **Nālandā**, founded in the same century and destined to be a world-leading educational centre — **500 years before Bologna** was founded in **1088**. He owed much to a contemporary, the grammarian-philosopher **Bhartṛhari**; his disciple **Dharmakīrti** would reinvent Dignāga's system and adapt it to new Buddhist communities in ways Dignāga himself might not have approved.
- [00:16:24] The take-home message: around the time of the **fall of the Roman Empire**, Buddhism was growing in philosophical sophistication and proliferating into branches. The same happened in the **Jaina** tradition. Its most important thinkers here were **Kundakunda** and **Umāsvāti**, whose treatise, the "Sūtra on What There Is," became the common heritage of the two Jaina sub-traditions, the **Digambara** and the **Śvetāmbara** — a systematizing work in metaphysics, epistemology and philosophy of mind.
- [00:17:04] Key Jaina ideas develop further in **Siddhasena Divākara** (**5th century CE**), and in later figures: **Haribhadra Sūri** and **Akalaṅka** (both **8th century**), **Prabhācandra** (**980–1065**), and, next generation, **Hemacandra**. Their belief in tolerance and harmony led to a **pluralism** in metaphysics and ethics and a **relativism or perspectivism** in epistemology and semantics. This is likely as far as the coming episodes will go — "but it was certainly not the end of philosophy in India."

## Beyond the millennium: disquiet, early modernity, colonialism
- [00:17:45] The period just sketched was followed by intellectual turbulence, as Indian philosophers increasingly questioned the foundations of the systems they had built. A key development was the **political decline of Buddhism** within India, even as it thrived in neighbouring countries — Sri Lanka, Burma, Thailand, China, Korea, Japan.
- [00:18:18] Within India a new era of **uncertainty** began: without the Buddhists as a foil, the Hindu systems began to question themselves and interrogate each other. Adamson calls the period from the **9th to the 13th/14th century** an **"age of disquiet."** Many of its great figures came from **Kashmir**, including the **Śaiva** thinker **Abhinavagupta**, the **Nyāya** genius **Jayanta Bhaṭṭa**, and possibly the great sceptic **Śrīharṣa** and the materialist **Udbhaṭa**.
- [00:18:48] From the **14th century** a distinctive **early modernity** emerged. Driven perhaps by contact with the Islamic world, Indian philosophers self-consciously set out to **innovate** — to think *with* the ancient systems but no longer defer to them. A vast number of rich Sanskrit works from this period lie unedited, sometimes in a single manuscript copy — a paradox familiar from Islamic-world philosophy of the same era, also marked by huge numbers of barely-studied texts.
- [00:19:29] Then came **colonialism** and new priorities: ideas from the **European Enlightenment**, and the injustices of British rule, demanded a response. Indian philosophy entered its **current phase** with the end of colonialism and the **partition of India in 1947**. Thinkers from **Gandhi to Nehru to Tagore**, often writing in English, pushed **political and social philosophy** to centre stage — where earlier the central issues had been epistemology, metaphysics and philosophy of language.

## What "philosophy in India" really means: darśana vs. ānvīkṣikī
- [00:20:00] Having surveyed the terrain, we can return to the question of what it means to speak of "philosophy in India" — a Greek word with no equivalent in Sanskrit or the other ancient languages. Scholars often offer the Sanskrit **darśana** as a stand-in for a philosophical system. But this may mislead: derived from a verb meaning **"to see,"** it can mean a point of view, perspective or doctrine — yet *also* **vision**, which could wrongly suggest Indian systems rest on insight rather than argument.
- [00:20:42] Adamson prefers an earlier term, **ānvīkṣikī**, meaning something like **critical inquiry or investigation** — and, unlike *darśana*, a word the classical Indian philosophers actually used of themselves.
- [00:21:01] One of the earliest texts discussing **ānvīkṣikī** is the **Arthaśāstra** ("Treatise on Aims"), a work on government, politics and economics dating perhaps to the **4th century BCE**. Its author, **Kauṭilya**, a royal minister of the **Magadha Empire**, is said to have written it to train princes in the skills of successful rule.
- [00:21:23] Kauṭilya names **four branches of learning** for young princes: **ānvīkṣikī** (rational investigation); **trayī** (the religious canon, i.e. the three Vedas); **vārtā** (the science of material acquisition — trade, agriculture); and **daṇḍanīti** ("rule by stick," i.e. political administration and government).
- [00:21:50] He then explains ānvīkṣikī: distinguishing, with proper reasons, good from evil in Vedic religion, profit from loss in wealth, right from wrong policy in administration — it "renders help to people, keeps their minds steady in woe and weal, and produces a rightness of understanding, speech, and action." For Kauṭilya it is not one branch among many but the **study of the proper aims and methods of knowledge as such** — "a lamp for all branches of study, the means for all activities, the support for all religious and social duties."
- [00:22:44] Kauṭilya lists the kinds of investigation known to him: **Sāṃkhya, Yoga and Lokāyata**. These are also names of early systems, but he more likely means different **methods** of inquiry — by listing and enumeration, by dividing and reconnecting, and by empirical experimentation.
- [00:23:08] His agenda would be followed for centuries. His three methods would be supplemented by two more — **Nyāya** and **Mīmāṃsā** — which also became school names in the Age of the Sūtra. **Nyāya** (later the hallmark of the Nyāya system) refers to a **procedure of observation and deduction**, developed in the context of **medical diagnosis and prognosis**; **Mīmāṃsā** is a **technique for interpreting texts**. In the first instance, then, these are nothing more nor less than **techniques of reasoning**.
- [00:23:53] Such techniques would come to characterize philosophy in India "just as much as, or rather even more than, any body of doctrines." In the Age of the Sūtra, inquiry would come to be seen as an **end in itself** — but in the earliest periods these systematic concerns remain mostly implicit, as philosophers search above all for the **path to liberation**. That path is where the series begins in earnest next time, looking in more detail at the origins of philosophy in India.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **The four periods** — Adamson's home-grown scheme: (1) **Path & Purpose** (~**8th c. BCE–2nd c. CE**), (2) **Age of the Sūtra** (~**100 BCE–350 CE**), (3) **Buddhist analysis & Jaina synthesis** (~**150–550 CE**), then the later **age of disquiet** (~9th–14th c.), **early modernity** (14th c. on) and the colonial/post-1947 phase.
- **Vedas** — the Aryans' ritual scriptures; ultimate source of Hinduism, set down before even the first period.
- **Upaniṣads** — majestic works commenting on and extrapolating from the Vedas, articulating the unity of human, ritual and cosmos.
- **Mahābhārata / Bhagavad-Gītā** — the vast Hindu epic (ten times the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* combined) and its inserted "Song of the Lord."
- **Buddhism / Jainism** — the two early dissenting schools; Buddhism's "truth of the path" (last of the four noble truths) leads to **nirvāṇa**; Jainism is famous for absolute non-violence.
- **sūtra** — "thread"; both a single aphorism and a whole collection of them.
- **bhāṣya** — the systematizing commentaries on the sūtras (by Śabara, Vātsyāyana, Śaṅkara and others) that "wove the threads into a single cloth."
- **the six systems (darśanas)** — Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta; criticized here for a pro-Veda bias that omits Buddhists, Jains and materialists.
- **Cārvāka-Sūtra / Bṛhaspati** — the materialist tradition and its supposed author, left out of the six-systems map.
- **pramāṇa / prameya** — the sources of human knowledge, and the world as it is known; the new focus of the Age of the Sūtra.
- **Nāgārjuna, Āryadeva (Madhyamaka)** — initiator of the "middle way" school and his disciple.
- **Vasubandhu (Sarvāstivāda), Buddhaghosa (Theravāda), Dignāga (Yogācāra)** — systematizers of the other Buddhist schools; Dignāga (d. **c. 540 CE**), the Buddhist epistemologist, taught at **Nālandā** and marks the series' planned end-point.
- **Bhartṛhari / Dharmakīrti** — Dignāga's grammarian-philosopher contemporary, and the disciple who reinvented his system.
- **Kundakunda, Umāsvāti, Siddhasena Divākara** — leading Jaina synthesizers; their pluralism/perspectivism in metaphysics and epistemology.
- **darśana** — "vision/point of view" (from "to see"); a misleading stand-in for "philosophy," since it can imply insight over argument.
- **ānvīkṣikī** — "critical inquiry / investigation"; Adamson's preferred term, actually used by classical Indian philosophers.
- **Arthaśāstra / Kauṭilya** — the **4th-c. BCE** treatise on government (by a minister of **Magadha**) that names the four branches of princely learning — **ānvīkṣikī, trayī, vārtā, daṇḍanīti** — and frames ānvīkṣikī as the lamp of all study.
- **Nyāya & Mīmāṃsā (as methods)** — before they were schools: a procedure of observation/deduction (born in medical diagnosis) and a technique of textual interpretation — reasoning-techniques that define Indian philosophy more than any doctrine.

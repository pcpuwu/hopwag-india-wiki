## Setting up the no-self theory

- [00:00:32] Adamson introduces the guest: **Rupert Gethin**, Professor of Buddhist Studies at the **University of Bristol**, here for an interview on Buddhism. (The standard series credit names Jonardon Ganeri and Peter Adamson, but this is a solo-host interview episode.)
- [00:00:47] Adamson goes "straight to the most philosophically challenging and counterintuitive contribution of Buddhism" — for his money, the **no-self theory**: we don't have any selves. He flags that *how* counterintuitive this is depends on what we mean by "self," and asks Gethin to start with what the Buddhists thought they were denying, and why.
- [00:01:15] Gethin: the no-self doctrine is best read as a **reaction to a particular idea of the self** from early Indian thought — above all the **Upaniṣads** and their notion of the **ātman**. *Ātman* is just the ordinary Sanskrit word for "self," but in the Upaniṣads it acquires a special sense.
- [00:01:56] The argument the Upaniṣads are answering: at one level we are obviously a **flux** — a set of changing experiences, feelings, thoughts. So they ask: *what underlies all these? What ties them together? What is constant?* Their answer is the self. The **ātman is what is constant**, contrasted with the changing flux as that which does not change.
- [00:02:30] That, says Gethin, is the **starting point of the Buddhist critique**. Buddhism throws out a challenge: yes, there is a changing flow of experience; yes, we seem able to refer it back to a constant subject; but **when we introspect, what we actually find is never the self — only a specific experience, feeling, and so forth**.

## The Humean challenge: where is the self?

- [00:03:21] The Buddhist move is to challenge us to *locate* the unchanging constant that supposedly underlies our experiences — and to find that we can't. Gethin notes this is "a kind of precursor" to **David Hume**, who lays down a very similar challenge about the self.
- [00:03:51] Adamson spells out the upshot for the listener: reflect on your own experience right now and you find *the experiencing* — hearing our voices — but not some *further thing*, a subject who does the hearing. He adds that the Upaniṣads to some extent **concede this**: in places the self is admitted to be a hidden, underlying subject that is **hard to access**.

## The Vedic reply: unity at a time and over time

- [00:04:24] Adamson plays devil's advocate for the Upaniṣadic / Vedic side and presses Gethin two ways:
  - **Unity at a moment:** I can experience more than one thing at once — listening to the podcast *while* running through a park, seeing trees *and* hearing voices. What binds these into one experience?
  - **Identity over time:** I want to be the *same person* who hears the end of the episode and who heard the beginning. Don't Buddhists struggle to explain both the unity of experience at a moment and the persistence of one agent who starts and finishes an action?
- [00:05:26] Gethin's first reply: Buddhists **do not deny an experiencing subject** — there genuinely is that quality to experience, the sense of being a subject. What they deny is that this experiencing subject is a **constant thing persisting through time**.
- [00:05:58] The subject is "a simple quality of experience," and when you abstract it from the experiences it is "a kind of nothing." It is part of the *fact* of the flow of experience but **is not itself a thing**; it needs the objects of experience to be there. Without them it is blank, contentless.
- [00:06:31] So the Buddhist challenge to the Upaniṣadic thinkers: you are using this *sense* of subject but **can tell us nothing about it**. You project onto it that it is a constant, separate, independent thing persisting through time — but it is really just a particular quality of experiencing, not a continuing thing in itself.

## Aggregates and causal connection: the photograph analogy

- [00:07:11] Adamson pushes: if all we have is particular experiences, "aggregated" (as the Buddhists like to say), don't we still need to distinguish one aggregate from another? His example — *my* seeing of your face is bound up with *my* sensation of sitting in this chair, while *your* seeing of my face goes with a different chair-sensation. How do the Buddhists explain different aggregates of experience, at a time and over time, without selves?
- [00:07:54] Gethin: they appeal to a **theory of causality** — causal connection between phenomena. You and I are each a collection of **mental and physical events** occurring in a **causally connected cluster**. The word "I" refers to a particular aggregate / cluster / pattern of mental and physical events that is **arising and falling continuously from moment to moment** — a constant state of flux.
- [00:08:56] The pattern is **not random**: it follows certain **laws of causality**. It is the causal connection between the events that produces the *appearance* of unity when we experience things.
- [00:09:11] His analogy: a **colour photograph in a magazine**. From a distance you see people, a mountain, a scene; under a magnifying glass you see only **dots of colour** (four colours making the whole range). The picture is just dots that come together, and we project the image onto them. We are like that: when we think of ourselves and others we don't see the flux, the pattern of events — we see people as **solid and constant**, but that's "not really what is there."

## Did the Buddha actually teach no-self?

- [00:10:25] Adamson asks whether this is one of those cases where the Buddha "wasn't a Buddhist" (like Marx allegedly wasn't a Marxist): did the Buddha *himself* teach the no-self theory, and why associate it with him in particular?
- [00:10:57] Gethin: there is genuine discussion here. In the **earliest sources — the suttas of the Pāli canon** — there is **no passage where the Buddha categorically says "there is no self."** What he characteristically says is **"this is not the self, this is not the self"** — whatever you can point to, you can't take it as a self.
- [00:11:22] Some infer that the earliest tradition (even the Buddha) leaves it *open* whether there is some kind of self. Gethin thinks this **misunderstands** what is going on, and connects it to what he calls the **greater problem of the self**.

## Two problems of the self: the intellectual and the attachment problem

- [00:11:57] Buddhism approaches the self **two ways**:
  - the **intellectual problem** — examining the notion of self through logic and reason, asking whether it makes sense (this is what the conversation has covered so far);
  - the **bigger problem** — the self bound up with **attachment**. To say "I have a self / this is my self" is in effect to try to mark off a part of the universe and say **"this is mine and mine only."**
- [00:12:58] That, Gethin argues, is why the texts repeat **"this is not myself"**: there is **no single bit of the universe** you can point to and say "that's mine, I control it, it will be mine forever." This is again a response to the Upaniṣadic use of "self."
- [00:13:24] An aside: a modern Thai school of Buddhism is willing to call **nirvāṇa** "the self" in a sense. But for **mainstream Buddhism** there is almost a *critique of the very word "self"*: to call something that cannot be individuated, made personal, and owned a "self" is to **stretch the meaning of the word** beyond ordinary use. Buddhism wants to deny that "self" can have any **ultimate, meaningful referent** in the universe.

## Self and selfishness; letting go of both self and not-self

- [00:14:26] Adamson, half-joking: "you can't be selfish if you don't have a self." Gethin: "that is precisely it." To **have a self is inherently to be selfish**, so the goal is to **let go of self**.
- [00:14:40] Subtle point: sometimes you must let go of **both self *and* not-self**. Becoming too attached to the doctrine that there is *no* self is itself a form of attachment — you remain "preoccupied and caught up" in the question *do I have a self?*
- [00:15:13] Adamson recalls a Pāli passage where the Buddha says you can be attached to **continuing to survive** *or* attached to **being annihilated** — perhaps a dig at rival ascetic traditions seeking liberation by self-obliteration. Gethin agrees: both the wish to last forever and the wish to blot oneself out are premised on the same concept — **attachment to the self**.

## The ethical objection: moral responsibility over time

- [00:15:48] Adamson grants the no-self theory has clearly **ethical** payoff, then raises an *unwanted* ethical worry. Morality leans heavily on **moral responsibility**: if you wrong me, I may hold you responsible tomorrow or next week. But on the Buddhist picture, all that links the **skandhas** (experiences) constituting me now and those constituting me next week is a **causal relationship**. Is that enough? It seems you can only say "me" (in scare quotes) next week has *some relationship* to me now — but don't I need to be **genuinely the same agent** to be held responsible later, or in a **next life** after reincarnation (which Buddhists accept)?
- [00:17:21] Gethin concedes Buddhism "has a problem there." He frames **two models of personal continuity** — two kinds of "glue" holding a person together:
  - a **substance theory** — a constant, underlying, unchanging self that simply remains;
  - a **process / causal theory** — the **causal connections** that hold something together.
- [00:18:16] Buddhism is **committed to continuity** and must preserve some sense of it; the question is whether **causal connections are sufficient**. At one level Buddhism can only insist that yes, they are, and then articulate in detail *how* those causal connections work.
- [00:18:57] One way Buddhism develops is to **throw the challenge back at its critics**. Already in the Pāli canon people accuse the Buddha: "you teach the **destruction of the self**, you're a **nihilist**." The Buddha replies that this misunderstands him.
- [00:19:33] The reply uses the **middle way** between two extremes:
  - **eternalism** — *one and the same person* acts and experiences the result (one way to ground moral responsibility);
  - **annihilationism** — *one person* acts and a *different person* experiences the result.
- [00:20:29] Buddhism claims to be the **middle** between these, explained by reference to the causal connections that hold things together — traditionally **dependent origination** (the links between events). By that route Buddhism claims to avoid the annihilationist extreme: the **causal continuity is enough** to say it is, in some sense, the *same* person who experiences the results — **just not in the full-blooded sense the Upaniṣadic thinkers want**.

## Why would a no-self tradition meditate?

- [00:21:17] Adamson turns to **meditation**, something Gethin has worked on extensively, and notes an apparent paradox. Naïvely, meditation — e.g. closely attending to your breath — looks like **concentrating on yourself**, "getting in touch with yourself." People even say they meditate to "find myself." How can Buddhists be so invested in meditation while *denying* the self?
- [00:22:05] Gethin starts from the Buddhist starting point: **suffering — duḥkha**. We try to be at peace, to be comfortable in the world, yet this proves **elusive**; something is wrong. Why?
- [00:22:40] The basic premise: our problems arise because we **don't see the world as it is**. Not seeing what is really going on, we seek ways of being happy that are **inappropriate** — that don't fit how things actually work. So meditation is about **looking again** at what's going on, examining it.
- [00:23:20] This examining ties back to the **two aspects** of the no-self theory:
  - We can criticize the self **intellectually** — even modern psychologists say we *construct* our personal identities, become possessive of them, feel threatened. We are **emotionally attached** to a notion of ourselves.
  - The intellectual grasp is not enough. I can read a book on Buddhist no-self, think "yeah, that makes sense," put it down — and the moment someone criticizes me I feel **threatened and undermined**. The idea "hasn't really affected me or changed me in any deep way."
- [00:24:27] So meditation is **looking inside to deconstruct ourselves emotionally and psychologically**, not just intellectually. Yes, it can look like "navel-gazing," turning inward — but for Buddhism it is a necessary exercise, because we build our sense of self not over one lifetime but over **many, many lifetimes**; dismantling that requires work.

## Calm and insight meditation; mindfulness

- [00:25:22] Adamson asks whether meditation has been central throughout Buddhism's history, back to the Pāli canon. Gethin: it depends what you count — **contemplative techniques of various sorts** have always played a role, though not always literally sitting cross-legged on the floor; meditative technique is broader than that.
- [00:26:03] Adamson notes the contrast with familiar **European** traditions: you don't picture **David Hume meditating**, even though he reaches some of the same conclusions about the self. Gethin agrees — Hume comes to similar conclusions, then "shrugs his shoulders" and carries on living as before.
- [00:26:32] Gethin: these meditative techniques aren't peculiar to Buddhism — they are **common to the Indian tradition** generally, where philosophy is tied to the idea that you must **do and practise** something to *realize* the ideas. You search for **liberating knowledge**: you can get an idea of what it might be like, but that is **quite different from realizing it**, which requires practice.
- [00:27:23] Adamson reframes: there's a **distance between understanding the arguments and living out their conclusions**, and meditation is meant to **bridge that gap**.
- [00:27:34] Gethin lays out **two aspects of meditation**, traditionally **calm meditation** and **insight meditation**:
  - **Calm meditation** [00:28:09]: ordinarily our minds are scattered, jumbled, chaotic. As common sense says, when we're stirred up we don't see clearly ("calm down, tomorrow things will look better"). The technique is to take a **simple object** and focus on it to the exclusion of others, so the mind **gradually stills**. The image: **impurities / mud stirred up in water** gradually sink to the bottom and the water — the mind — becomes **clear** [00:29:25].
  - **Insight meditation** [00:29:44]: once the mind is clear you **examine** your physical and emotional experiences and try to **see them for what they are**.
- [00:30:08] **Mindfulness** is described as the **link** between the two — a clarity of mind that holds and looks at what's going on.

## The detrimental side of meditation, and secularized mindfulness

- [00:30:28] Gethin raises the less-discussed **detrimental effects** of meditation: people who walk in off the street for **ten-day intensive retreats** with no prior experience sometimes **come out feeling worse**, even **traumatized**.
- [00:31:04] What's interesting is that this is **what you'd expect**: intensive mindfulness is *precisely* about **deconstructing the self**, and deconstructing your sense of self is **bound to be challenging**.
- [00:31:33] Hence traditional Buddhism's heavy emphasis on an **ethical and religious framework**: the **symbols of the Buddha** and keeping the **ethical precepts** help **ground** the practitioner and give confidence they are heading somewhere desirable. When you **secularize mindfulness** and strip away that support system, it's "not surprising" the experience can become more **traumatic** than expected.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **ātman** — the Sanskrit word for "self"; in the Upaniṣads, the *constant* that underlies and ties together the changing flux of experience. The target of the Buddhist critique.
- **no-self / anātman theory** — Buddhism's claim that we have no self; on introspection we find only particular experiences, never an unchanging subject.
- **Upaniṣads** — early Indian texts that posit the constant ātman as what underlies the flux; Gethin notes some passages concede the self is a "hidden, hard-to-access" subject.
- **the Buddha** — the suttas never have him say flatly "there is no self," only "**this is not the self**"; for Gethin this reflects the self-as-attachment problem, not agnosticism.
- **suttas / Pāli canon** — the earliest Buddhist sources; site of the "this is not the self" formula and of early nihilism objections answered by the middle way.
- **skandhas (aggregates)** — the bundle of causally connected mental and physical events that "I" names; arising and falling moment to moment.
- **causal connection / process view** — the Buddhist alternative "glue" of personal continuity, opposed to a substance self; analogy of a photograph that is really just dots of colour.
- **David Hume** — Western precursor who reaches a similar no-self conclusion by the same introspective challenge, but then "shrugs" and carries on living.
- **duḥkha (suffering)** — the Buddhist starting point: our discomfort arises because we don't see the world as it is.
- **dependent origination** — the doctrine of causal links that grounds the middle way between eternalism and annihilationism, preserving continuity (and moral responsibility) without a permanent self.
- **eternalism vs annihilationism** — the two extremes on moral responsibility (same person acts and reaps / different person reaps); Buddhism claims the middle via causal continuity.
- **nirvāṇa** — liberation; a modern Thai school will even call it "the self," though mainstream Buddhism resists applying the word "self" to it.
- **calm meditation (śamatha)** — stilling the scattered mind by focusing on a simple object; the mud-settling-in-water image.
- **insight meditation (vipaśyanā)** — examining one's physical and emotional experiences clearly, to see them as they are.
- **mindfulness** — the clarity-of-mind link between calm and insight meditation; when secularized and stripped of its ethical/religious framework, can become destabilizing.

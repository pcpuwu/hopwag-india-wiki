## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Nāgārjuna** — his *Verses on the Middle Way*, chapter two on motion, plus his *Refutation of My Opponents* on the means of knowledge [00:01:46].
- **Zeno of Elea** — the Greek inventor of the paradoxes of motion, the recurring comparison case [00:01:22].
- **Patañjali (the grammarian)** — in his great commentary on Pāṇini, an earlier Indian denial of motion as objectively real [00:01:55].
- **Candrakīrti** — the Madhyamaka commentator whose elaboration of Nāgārjuna's compressed verses guides the reading [00:02:47].
- **Jan Westerhoff** — modern scholar cited for the view that the motion chapter is really an argumentative *template* [00:09:33].

## A race between a crocodile and a giraffe
- [00:00:36] Adamson and Ganeri agree about most things — that Indian philosophy is fascinating, that Buster Keaton's silent films are works of genius — but split over the relative merits of **giraffes and crocodiles**. To settle it they stage a race between **Gina** (a short-legged crocodile, given a head start) and **Hiawatha** (a giraffe).
- [00:01:15] Ganeri is confident that, despite Hiawatha's "fleetness of hoof," she cannot overtake Gina. Adamson assumes Ganeri has been reading **Zeno of Elea**, who invented paradoxes denying the possibility of motion: Gina's lead can never be overcome because Hiawatha must *first* reach the point Gina just occupied, by which time Gina has moved further on.
- [00:01:43] But Ganeri was not thinking of Zeno at all — he had been reading **Nāgārjuna**.

## An older Indian denial of motion
- [00:01:46] Nāgārjuna was not the first Indian thinker to deny motion. In his great commentary on **Pāṇini**, the grammarian **Patañjali** refers to a teaching that motion is **not an objectively real thing** — merely a *conceptual construct* used to explain that things arrive at a given place.
- [00:02:07] Nāgārjuna maintains the same idea in the **second chapter of his *Verses on the Middle Way***. He applies his **prasaṅga** (dialectical, reductio) method to two questions about motion, arguing that both are unanswerable: **where are movements located?** and **when do they begin?**
- [00:02:26] His aim, as always, is to show that our concept of motion is **empty**. And as always, scholars disagree about *why*: is Nāgārjuna saying human cognition simply cannot conceive the world correctly, so we should stop trying — or that our conventional idea of motion must be **replaced** with a more refined theory that avoids postulating intrinsic essences (**svabhāva**) in things?
- [00:02:47] Help comes from the Madhyamaka commentator **Candrakīrti**, who elaborates the discussion; the episode follows him, while noting his is only *one* possible reading of Nāgārjuna's verses.

## First question: where is a movement located?
- [00:03:12] Approach via the Sanskrit grammarians. Verbal roots refer to **actions or events**, which may be located either where the **agent** is or where the **object** of the verb is. "Enter" names an event located where the agent is (Ganeri entering the racetrack: the event is at the gate). "Hit" names an action located in the thing struck (the gong Peter strikes to start the race).
- [00:03:54] Presupposing this, Nāgārjuna says that if someone is travelling, we can say **the traveller travels the place that is being travelled**. "Travel" marks an event located in the spot on the road where the travelling happens. In fact there are **two things** going on: the place of travelling *is being travelled*, and the traveller *is travelling*. So far, common sense.
- [00:04:24] But Nāgārjuna uses common sense as a jumping-off point for a **prasaṅga refutation**. Take the path of a motion — Gina's slow progress along a 100-metre track — and divide it into three: the part **already traversed**, the part **yet to be traversed**, and the part **currently being traversed**. Which is the location of Gina's motion? Not the first or second, since the place being crossed *now* cannot be the place already crossed or the place not yet reached. So the motion must occur in the part **just now being moved across**.
- [00:05:01] But that too is impossible. **Candrakīrti**'s reasoning: every point on the path is *either* already traversed *or* yet to be traversed — there is **no portion left over** to accommodate the current motion. This recalls a sceptical argument about *time* seen before in **Gautama's Nyāya-Sūtra**: past and future do not currently exist, while the present has no duration, being merely the dividing line between them.

## A second argument: the redundancy of motion-talk
- [00:05:39] Another line. Consider "the place of travelling is being travelled" and "the place of movement is being traversed." Nāgārjuna points out these are **redundant** — like saying "I am where I am" (what Popeye would say if he were a GPS), or answering "where was the race held?" with "it happened where the race took place," or claiming to have solved a crime by saying it was committed by whoever perpetrated it. Such statements make **no substantive claim at all**.
- [00:06:14] The same applies to "the mover moves." This is a **tautology** — guaranteed true but uninformative, like "the bachelor is unmarried." It is useless to point at sprinting Hiawatha and say "the running Hiawatha is running." Yet if Hiawatha is *not* running, it is false to say she is running. So there seems to be **no way to truly *and* informatively** say she is running.
- [00:06:52] One escape: suppose there is a **further motion** — a second motion that *makes* Hiawatha be running. But if there are two motions, surely there should be two things moving, not one. Or perhaps not: can't a single giraffe be the subject of many actions at once — running, hearing the crowd, narrowing her eyes at Gina?

## Candrakīrti's reply: grammatical roles, and the axe
- [00:07:22] Candrakīrti's answer trades on a subtlety of Sanskrit grammatical theory. Of one and the same **axe** we can say many things: "the axe is made of metal" (agent), "he sees the axe" (direct object), "he cuts the tree with the axe" (instrument). The same object plays **different grammatical roles** relative to different verbs; each statement refers to the axe only *insofar as* it adopts that role.
- [00:08:05] Likewise the **running Hiawatha is not identical** to the hearing Hiawatha or the seeing Hiawatha. Only the *running* Hiawatha is Hiawatha-insofar-as-she-runs. And the *place* she runs over, insofar as she traverses it, is not just a patch of ground but a spot being travelled over.
- [00:08:29] This illuminates the compressed argument: the problem is not only that "the place travelled over is travelled over" and "the mover moves" are trivial — it is that **this uninformative description is the only way to describe what is happening**, for it is only the thing *insofar as it is moving* that moves.
- [00:08:53] The template generalizes. Mick Jagger sees a red door and wants it painted black: he sees the door insofar as it is *seen*, wants it painted insofar as it is the *object of his desire*.

## Dependent origination, and the argumentative template
- [00:09:11] In tackling motion near the start of the *Verses on the Middle Way*, Nāgārjuna gives a first taste of **dependent origination**: the motion of running is dependent on — cannot exist without — running Hiawatha, and vice versa. The same style later shows an object of vision **co-originated** with the act of seeing, an object of desire co-originated with the desire.
- [00:09:33] **Jan Westerhoff** has thus argued the movement chapter was *not* meant as a specific investigation of motion or the structure of time and place; rather it uses motion as an example to **illustrate an argumentative template** deployable in many contexts.

## Can the argument be escaped?
- [00:09:56] Is there a way out? Seemingly yes: just distinguish the various **roles** Hiawatha performs from **Hiawatha herself**. That she plays different grammatical functions in different sentences need not show — as Candrakīrti thinks — that we cannot form a notion of Hiawatha-in-herself; nor need we deny that one patch of ground could first be occupied by Gina and seconds later by Hiawatha.
- [00:10:37] But this reply **presupposes the very thing Madhyamaka attacks** — that there are things-in-themselves (substances, like those of **Vaiśeṣika**, or of Aristotle) which take on and lose properties. As seen last time, Nāgārjuna's challenge is that **any** description of a so-called substance must invoke some property with which the substance, as so described, is mutually interdependent.
- [00:11:10] And Sanskrit grammar agrees: if you cannot frame a sentence about Hiawatha or a patch of ground without tying it to a verb, you cannot refer to these things without treating them as **playing a role** marked by the grammar of the sentence.

## Second question: when does a motion begin?
- [00:11:28] A second line seems more specifically about motion itself, not just one instance of the general strategy. Nāgārjuna asks **when do motions begin?** — and means to show any answer is incoherent.
- [00:11:49] Take the start of the race, when Gina begins to run — well, waddle. Threefold division again: a time at which her waddling **has not yet** occurred, a time **while** it is taking place, and a time at which she has **already finished**. In which does her motion begin?
- [00:12:12] Not before she begins — she is still waiting for the starter's gong. Not after she has already moved. But neither *while* she is moving, since once underway she must **already have started**. The implication: she does not begin to move at all — which (Adamson quips) should give Hiawatha a good chance of catching her, if only Hiawatha could begin moving herself.

## Comparison with Zeno — and with Aristotle
- [00:12:40] Here we see why scholars compare Nāgārjuna with **Zeno**. Zeno's arrow can never reach its target, since at each moment it is, as Zeno says, "against something equal" — in a durational instant the arrow occupies a spatial extension identical to its own size, so cannot get from one region to the next; and if true at every instant, it cannot move at all. Hence, as **Tom Stoppard** observed, **St. Sebastian died of fright**.
- [00:13:19] Nāgārjuna's argument is indeed reminiscent — it would similarly license the conclusion that all the warriors slain by Arjuna's bow in the **Mahābhārata** died of fright, and it turns on the same problem of motion and an **unextended part of time**. Yet the point differs: Nāgārjuna asks about the **moment motion *begins***, not just any instant at which it occurs. A closer Greek parallel may be **Aristotle**'s worry about the **end of a change**: at the instant a change is completed, the change is no longer going on.
- [00:14:03] Stepping back over the whole battery of arguments, especially on Candrakīrti's reading, they are **unlike Zeno's** in being **inspired by grammar**. Nāgārjuna focuses not so much on whether motion is a coherent *concept* as on whether **language can consistently and informatively describe** it.
- [00:14:26] Sentences about motion have the same logical form as sentences about actions and events, so Nāgārjuna takes motion to be located at a place and a time. But motion is precisely a **displacement during a passage of time**, so fixing one location or moment for it is doomed. We could adopt an artificial idiom (physicists' coordinate systems), but that just **concedes his point** that natural languages lack the resources to describe motion.

## The same template against knowledge
- [00:15:07] Nāgārjuna uses the same style to unmask the emptiness of things beyond motion — notably **cognition**, the bone of contention between him and his **Nyāya** critics. Sanskrit grammar forces us to treat verbs of thinking and knowing as **acts that happen to an agent**, just like motion.
- [00:15:34] In his ***Refutation of My Opponents***, Nāgārjuna attacks the concept of knowledge by attacking the notion of **giving a proof**. Recall Gautama's **Nyāya** epistemology: we are justified in our beliefs by a **means of knowing** (**pramāṇa**) — sensation, reason. The thing known by such a legitimate means is the **prameya**. So we have the *instrument* and *direct object* of a verb of knowing.
- [00:16:06] Example: just as Ganeri restrains Gina with the instrument of a **leash** (so she won't bite Hiawatha after losing), so Ganeri knows Gina is dark green by the instrument of his **eyesight**. Ganeri is the knower, Gina the **prameya** (object known), his vision the **pramāṇa**.
- [00:16:29] Then Nāgārjuna strikes. Everything we know is known through some instrument — but **how do we know the instruments themselves are reliable?** If Ganeri knows Gina is green by his eyesight, he must also know his eyesight can be **trusted**. If he appeals to a further instrument to reassure himself, he can worry about *that* one too — an **infinite regress**, each pramāṇa needing confirmation by another.

## Nāgārjuna classifies and eliminates the options
- [00:17:18] As usual, Nāgārjuna classifies **all possible responses** to his question — how can a pramāṇa be established as reliable?
- [00:17:38] **Intrinsic validation** (without reference to things known), in three ways. **(1)** Validated by *another* source of knowledge — this is where the **infinite regress** bites. **(2)** Simply *insist* sources of knowledge need no validation (arguably the whole point of distinguishing prameya from pramāṇa: the object must be established, the means need not). Nāgārjuna rules this out on the starting assumption that whatever is known must be known **through some means** — if things can be known without a means, why need the pramāṇa at all? And what distinguishes the pramāṇa that needs no checking from the prameya that does, when any pramāṇa can *become* a prameya (an optician's chart tests whether your eyesight works)?
- [00:18:49] **(3)** Each means is **self-validating** — establishing itself along with the objects it makes known, like a **fire that illuminates both itself and other things**. Nāgārjuna retorts that it is **meaningless to say fire is illuminated**: fire is the *source* of light, whereas the illuminated is what *receives* light. To suppose a pramāṇa makes *itself* known the way it makes other things known is like saying **an axe cuts itself**. Grammatically: if the source plays the role of *instrument* for the action of knowing, it cannot also be the *object* of that action.
- [00:19:39] So much for intrinsic validation. **Extrinsic validation** (in relation to the objects), two options. **(1)** The source is validated by **its own objects** — vision is validated so long as it represents visible objects as they really are (corroborated by correspondence). But this **assumes the very thing at issue**: that the objects are already established — which was precisely the *job* of the pramāṇa. The opponent commits the fallacy of **siddha-sādhana**, "proving what is already proven."
- [00:20:35] **(2)** Source and objects are **mutually interdependent**. This sounds congenial to Madhyamaka, but is **hopeless as a theory of justification** for the opponent — it is plainly **circular**.

## The self-refutation worry — and the escape
- [00:20:58] The upshot: apparently **nothing can be known**. Oops — another case where Nāgārjuna seems to **refute himself**. How can he *know* knowledge is impossible? How can a complex set of arguments be given for the conclusion that no conclusion can ever be established by reason?
- [00:21:18] His reply: he is **using his opponents' own methods against them**. If the whole exercise is an **internal critique** of the justificatory system of philosophers like Gautama, it can establish that system's incoherence **without any commitment on Nāgārjuna's part**.
- [00:21:45] This is familiar: an **anti-democratic movement** seeking power through a democratic election; a **poem about the impossibility of writing poetry**; or saying "I cannot recommend Buster Keaton films highly enough" precisely *to* recommend them emphatically. So Nāgārjuna can **avoid being bitten by his own snake**.

## The signature form — and where we go next
- [00:22:12] The arguments about motion and knowledge alike always **lay out a set of options and eliminate them one by one** — entirely characteristic of Nāgārjuna. We have met **prasaṅga**, the destructive argument that reduces empty concepts to absurdity.
- [00:22:32] But not yet his most important contribution to logic: a negative proof with **four options, each refuted** — the form scholars call the **tetralemma**. With it, you should be able to prove anything — so long as what you want to prove is that **someone else is talking nonsense**. That logic is the subject of the next episode.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Nāgārjuna** — Madhyamaka Buddhist; in the *Verses on the Middle Way* (ch. 2) he argues motion has no coherent location or beginning, and in the *Refutation of My Opponents* that no "means of knowledge" can be validated — both showing such concepts are "empty."
- **prasaṅga** — Nāgārjuna's destructive/reductio method: lay out the options for a concept and eliminate each, reducing it to absurdity.
- **svabhāva** — intrinsic essence / thing-in-itself; what Nāgārjuna's analysis aims to deny things have.
- **dependent origination** — Nāgārjuna's positive lesson: e.g. the motion of running and the running Hiawatha mutually depend and "co-originate"; likewise object and act of seeing, object and desire.
- **Candrakīrti** — Madhyamaka commentator who elaborates Nāgārjuna via Sanskrit grammar (the axe playing agent/object/instrument roles); the episode's main interpretive guide.
- **Patañjali (the grammarian)** — in his commentary on Pāṇini reports an earlier teaching that motion is no objectively real thing, only a conceptual construct.
- **Pāṇini** — the foundational Sanskrit grammarian on whom Patañjali comments.
- **Vaiśeṣika** — the substance-recognizing school (with Aristotle) whose things-in-themselves taking on/losing properties Nāgārjuna's argument undermines.
- **pramāṇa** — a means/instrument of knowing (e.g. vision, reason); Nāgārjuna argues none can be established as reliable without regress, circularity or incoherence.
- **prameya** — the object known by a pramāṇa; Nāgārjuna notes any pramāṇa can itself become a prameya (e.g. an eye-test).
- **siddha-sādhana** — the fallacy of "proving what is already proven"; committed if one validates a pramāṇa by assuming its objects are already established.
- **tetralemma** — Nāgārjuna's four-option negative proof (each cornered option refuted); his key contribution to logic, deferred to the next episode.
- **Zeno of Elea** — Greek author of the motion paradoxes (the arrow "against something equal"); the recurring comparison, though Nāgārjuna's grammar-driven worry about *when* motion begins differs from Zeno's.
- **Aristotle** — invoked for the worry about the *end* of a change (at completion the change is no longer occurring), a closer parallel than Zeno to Nāgārjuna's "when does motion begin?"
- **Jan Westerhoff** — scholar cited for reading the motion chapter as an argumentative *template* rather than a study of motion as such.

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Cārvāka / Lokāyata** — the Indian materialists; their theory of mind anticipates modern emergentism [00:01:30].
- **Ajita Keśakambala** — pioneering materialist, contemporary of the Buddha; the person is a compound of four elements [00:03:33].
- **Pāyāsi** — a prince, "ancient Indian David Hume," who ran experiments to disprove a separable soul [00:03:58].
- **Bṛhaspati** — reputed author of the Cārvāka-Sūtra, who states the materialist account of mind [00:07:42].
- **Prabhācandra** (11th c.) & **Jayanta** (9th c.) — Jain and Nyāya opponents whose hostile reports preserve Cārvāka's view [00:12:40].

## Two ways to get philosophers interested in the past
- [00:00:36] There are basically two ways to get modern-day philosophers interested in the history of philosophy. **First**: point out that ideas from long-ago eras are excitingly *different* from our own. Adamson quotes **Peter Geach**: the usefulness of historical knowledge in philosophy is that "the prejudices of our own period may lose their grip on us if we imaginatively enter into another period when people's prejudices were different." Ancient Indian philosophy offers plenty of material for this — how many contemporary philosophers seriously engage with acting *without desire* (the central topic of the **Bhagavad-Gītā**), or with the consciousness-monism of **Advaita Vedānta**?
- [00:01:20] But there's also a **second, diametrically opposed strategy**: pointing out startling *anticipations* of modern ideas in the historical record. Today's episode is dedicated to just such a case — the **Cārvāka** theory of mind, which is remarkably similar to a position defended by many philosophers nowadays called **emergentism**.

## Emergentism: mind out of matter
- [00:01:43] As the name implies, emergentism holds that the mind *emerges from* the body. This means, on the one hand, that physical properties — like chemical processes in the brain — somehow give rise to mental life (philosophers speak of the mental **supervening** on the physical). Yet, on the other hand, mental life **cannot simply be reduced** to bodily processes: the mind has its own **causal powers**, and even the most sophisticated science couldn't eliminate talk of the mental and replace it with talk of things happening in the brain.
- [00:02:16] The nice thing about emergentism: consciousness and other features of the mind are *generated by* the body, but don't then just hang around as **impotent byproducts** with all the real causation happening at the physical level.
- [00:02:33] A metaphor found in discussions of Cārvāka makes the point beautifully: a **spark of flame** is produced by rubbing two sticks together, and the resulting fire can then be a genuine cause of heat and burning, with the help of other materials. Similarly, the body's composition gives rise to mental phenomena — thoughts, desires — which can then *cause further* thoughts and desires.

## The opposing schools
- [00:02:57] This view is powerfully opposed to what we've seen in other ancient Indian schools. The strongest contrast is with the **Vedānta** tendency to make **brahman** a *mental* principle, the cause of all physical phenomena — which may even be seen as unreal. But **Sāṃkhya** and **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** also insisted that humans have some **immaterial psychological principle**. More generally, there was widespread acceptance that the mind or self, if it exists at all, can **survive on its own without the body**.

## Early forerunner: Ajita Keśakambala
- [00:03:27] Widespread though this idea was, it came under attack very early in Indian history. Last time we met the pioneering materialist **Ajita Keśakambala**. In support of his rejection of Vedic ritual and its fruits — whether in this life or the next — Ajita claimed that the human person is **but a compound of the four great primary elements**. After death the person is **dissolved**, with earth returning to earth, water to water, and so on.

## Prince Pāyāsi, the "Indian David Hume"
- [00:03:58] Another forerunner of the Cārvāka view of mind was a prince called **Pāyāsi**. Like Ajita, he seems to have lived around the time of the **Buddha** himself, and we have a report of his ideas in the Buddhist **Nikāya**. It tells us how Pāyāsi, like an ancient Indian version of **David Hume**, undertook **experiments** to show there is no self separable from the body.
- [00:04:22] These experiments "would not pass muster with a modern-day scientific ethics committee." Pāyāsi suggested putting a person in a large **sealed vessel** and baking it until the person dies — then opening the jar and watching carefully to see if the soul escapes. (We see none.) Failing a jar that big, just **beat the life** out of the person and watch for a departing soul; or **weigh** someone before and after killing them to see if anything has escaped; or, "if you're feeling sadistic and curious in equal measure," **peel the person layer by layer** to see whether a soul is exposed at the core.

### Kāśyapa's reply: the silent trumpet
- [00:05:05] All of this is proposed in a dialogue with **Kāśyapa**, supposedly a companion of the Buddha. Kāśyapa can hardly insist the soul is *detectable* through Pāyāsi's gruesome experiments — but he **denies their relevance**, arguing that thrashing a person's body to find the soul is like **thrashing a trumpet to get at the music** it used to make. Just as a trumpet not being played by a musician is silent, the body without soul is inert: without life, heat and consciousness it is incapable of motion, sense-perception and thought.
- [00:05:35] As a Buddhist, Kāśyapa has no interest in defending a self as an enduring simple entity. His objection is rather **methodological**: whatever our doctrine of the self, we should not suppose that the mental can be investigated using the same approach we take in studying the natural world.

### Pāyāsi in the Jain version: Keśi's lamp
- [00:05:53] The figure of Pāyāsi is also known from **Jain** literature, which again makes him an interlocutor in a dialogue about the soul — this time with a man named **Keśi**. Pāyāsi puts it to Keśi that an ant and an elephant should have **souls of the same size** — so why are an elephant's activities so much more impressive than an ant's?
- [00:06:13] Keśi responds with a metaphor: imagine a windowless, doorless hall — no light can escape. A **lamp** is lit in the middle; it illuminates the entire hall but nothing outside. Cover the lamp with a **bushel**, and only the much smaller space inside the bushel is lit. In just the same way the soul is reborn into a body — small or large — as it is bound by the **karma** of its previous existence, and animates it accordingly. Therefore, Pāyāsi, the soul and the body are **different, not identical**.
- [00:06:55] The story of Pāyāsi shows that **naturalism was a strong undercurrent** in Indian philosophy from the very beginning. Like its rivals it was a philosophy of **path and purpose** — but one that recommended living for the **here and now** rather than in pursuit of transcendental ideals. Ajita and Pāyāsi dismissed the pretensions of Vedic ritual as a means to satisfying desire, and rejected the idea that **God exists and can intervene** on earth. Their skepticism about the soul was part and parcel of a more general philosophy with the potential to challenge the Vedic tradition — and also Buddhism and Jainism — once it was systematized into a proper school.

## The Cārvāka-Sūtra of Bṛhaspati
- [00:07:42] As so often, systematization meant producing a **canonical text in sūtra form** — hence the **Cārvāka-Sūtra**, ascribed to **Bṛhaspati**. We saw last time that Bṛhaspati, and Cārvāka more generally, carried on the early naturalist project with an **epistemology that privileged sense-perception** over inference and testimony. But at least as much emphasis was placed on the **naturalist account of mind** — indeed this topic seems to have featured at the very beginning of the Cārvāka-Sūtra.
- [00:08:18] The latest reconstruction has the text starting roughly: *"Earth, fire, air and water are the reals. Their combination is called the body, senses and objects. Consciousness is formed out of these elements, as the power to intoxicate is formed out of fermenting ingredients. A human being is a body qualified by consciousness. Thinking is from the body alone, because of its presence when there is a body."*
- [00:08:48] This is a stunningly forthright affirmation of **naturalism / physicalism**: everything that exists, states Bṛhaspati, is **identical to the elements** or some combination of them. In case we wonder how our complex surroundings could arise from such simple ingredients, he adds: *"The world is varied due to variations in origin, as the eye in the peacock's tail."* Modern philosophers would speak of the **causal history** of different things — things are complicated because they arose from a complex process of combination and interaction between simpler materials.

## The intoxication analogy and the question of causal power
- [00:09:29] Bṛhaspati's account applies also — even *especially* — to the human mind. Tellingly, he does **not** speak of *self* (**ātman**), but only of the generation of the human being or person (Sanskrit **puruṣa**). He doesn't do his opponents the favour of assuming there is any such thing as a self that could be independent of body; instead the **whole person**, including the power of thought, is made from the four elements.
- [00:10:00] Bṛhaspati compares the emergence of consciousness from matter to the way the **capacity to intoxicate** arises from mixing the ingredients of an alcoholic beverage. This analogy strongly suggests the mind that arises from matter would have its **own causal powers**, as emergentism requires: earth and water cannot think on their own, just as hops, barley and water cannot make you drunk on their own. The trick is all in the **combination**, from which arises a new power.

## The ablative ambiguity — three readings
- [00:10:36] Unfortunately the surviving Cārvāka-Sūtra leaves doubt about the exact relationship between mind and its material basis. The problem centres on the noun for "elements," here apparently in the **ablative case**. As in Latin, the Sanskrit ablative can mean several things: Bṛhaspati might be saying that mind is **made from** the elements, that thinking is **because of** the elements, or that thought is **produced out of** the elements. This — plus the typically compressed style of the aphorisms — left commentators room for several interpretations.

### The reductive reading: Huxley's steam whistle
- [00:11:10] One idea: mind is **only the manifestation** of processes at the physical level, with those physical processes carrying all the explanatory weight. On this reading Bṛhaspati was simply **identifying** the mind with a combination of bodily elements; our mental life would just be the way we *register* the physical processes caused by elemental combination.
- [00:11:38] This resonates with some modern ideas: in the late **19th century, T. H. Huxley** proposed that mental phenomena could be a mere **byproduct** of physical phenomena, comparing the mind to the **steam whistle of a locomotive** (no role in running the train) or the **bell of a clock** (no help in keeping time). On this reading Bṛhaspati's mind is causally **inert** — it doesn't do anything in its own right, just manifests the elemental combination.
- [00:12:09] Yet there are good reasons to doubt this reading. The **intoxicating-beverage** comparison seems designed to show the mind *does* do something distinctive of its own. Other Cārvākas duly held that the mind is a **distinct power** which **emerges** from the combination of material elements **without being identical** to them.

## The hostile witnesses: Prabhācandra and Jayanta
- [00:12:40] There is textual evidence of this disagreement among the Cārvākas. **Prabhācandra**, an **11th-century** Jain philosopher, discusses their views with unprecedented detail. He explains that a **"manifestation"** puts together well — refines and perfects — what is *already there*, rather than bringing into being something that wasn't there before; so the manifestation is **not a separate thing** over and above the four elements.
- [00:13:15] Prabhācandra lays out the **options** for explaining mind in terms of body. (1) The most **radical**: the mind or self simply **is** the body — an identity. (2) The physicalist need not go that far: she might say mind is one of the body's **qualities or states** — for which he uses the term **guṇa** (familiar from Sāṃkhya); this aligns with the train-whistle, mere-byproduct idea. (3) Finally — and, "for our money," the most plausible reading of the Cārvāka-Sūtra — mind is an **effect** of the body, well captured by the spark giving rise to flame. Again it is Prabhācandra who records this idea on behalf of his Cārvāka opponents.
- [00:14:11] Another insightful opponent was the **9th-century Nyāya** philosopher **Jayanta**, who explained the theory in order to refute it. He confirms Prabhācandra's observation that for Cārvāka the self is **not a separate entity**, and — befitting a Nyāya thinker — focuses on Cārvāka's **epistemological arguments** against an independent self.

### The self is not perceived, inferred, or testified
- [00:14:36] Since Cārvāka grounds all human knowledge in **sense-perception**, it makes sense for them to argue (as Jayanta reports) that the self is **not given to us in perception**: our external senses give no access to any self distinct from the body. "When was the last time you saw or heard a self?" Nor can **introspection** deliver it, since internal perception is always a perception of something like a particular pain or pleasure — not of a **subject** of consciousness.
- [00:15:16] This is more convincing than it sounds, given the views Cārvāka is criticizing: recall how **Nyāya** carefully distinguished the internal perception enjoyed by the interior mental organ from the role played by the self.
- [00:15:27] If the self can't be perceived directly, perhaps we could **infer** it. But Cārvāka doubted the possibility of inferences concerning things **beyond the senses**, and explicitly rejected arguments that inferred a separate self from its apparent **function** in our mental lives: we need posit **no self** to explain memory or the comparison of multiple sensory experiences — these are all things **the body can do**.
- [00:16:01] No better is establishing the self by **authoritative testimony** such as the ancient scriptures. Cārvāka gives arguments from authority no credence anyway — but they also point out (per Jayanta) that one can just as well use the scriptures to support a **naturalist** view of mind, citing a passage stating that the self is "a single mass of cognition which has risen up out of these elements and is dissolved into them."

## Not the same as Buddhist no-self
- [00:16:47] It might be tempting to associate the materialists with the **Buddhists**, whose famous **no-self** theory posed a similar challenge to the Brahminical tradition. But we should not conflate the two. Admittedly Bṛhaspati avoids the word *self* — but the Cārvākas are **not**, like the Buddhists, questioning that the human mind **persists over time**. They are fully committed to the reality of such an **enduring mind**; it's just that they believe it **emerges from and depends on the body**.
- [00:17:15] So Cārvāka could agree with the other sūtra-based traditions that we have **persisting minds**, while disagreeing by explaining those mental subjects in a **bottom-up** rather than **top-down** way. Cārvāka is like a **mirror image of Vedānta**: the mental arises out of the material world, rather than the material out of the mental.
- [00:17:38] That language is slightly misleading, though. If Bṛhaspati really is an early forerunner of **emergentism**, then in a sense he *does* make room for **top-down causation**: once the mind is produced out of material composition, it can exert its own causal influence — thoughts are not inert but play a role in explaining the world. The real point is that for Cārvāka the thinking subject is **not a fundamental explanatory principle** (as so many Vedic philosophers insisted), but **merely an effect of the body**.

## Conclusion and what's next
- [00:18:15] It's a real shame the Cārvāka theory of mind is so poorly preserved — the school's works are lost, known only indirectly through hostile reports. But Bṛhaspati proposed that human beings are **physical bodies which can be conscious**, that consciousness **emerges out of the organization of physical matter** and **disappears again** when that organization is destroyed. His position resonates powerfully with most modern philosophers of mind, who likewise see an intimate relationship between mind and body, the physical somehow giving rise to the mental.
- [00:18:48] Still, we shouldn't underestimate the **first** motivation for doing history of philosophy: the spectrum of Indian theories of mind is interesting precisely because it is **so different** from the spectrum current today. Before moving on to the **Buddhists** and their searching critique of the enduring self, the series will explore the ancient Indian psychological theories more deeply — in an upcoming conversation with **Monima Chadha**.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Cārvāka / Lokāyata** — the Indian materialists; mind and self emerge from the body and perish with it. Known only through hostile reports.
- **emergentism** — the modern view Cārvāka anticipates: mind is generated by the body (supervenes on the physical) yet has its own causal powers and can't be reduced to brain processes.
- **spark / fire analogy** — emergentism's image: fire produced by rubbing sticks then genuinely causes heat; likewise the body produces mind, which then causes further mental events.
- **Ajita Keśakambala** — early materialist, contemporary of the Buddha; the person is a compound of the four great elements and dissolves at death.
- **Pāyāsi** — a prince, "ancient Indian David Hume," who proposed grisly experiments (baking, weighing, peeling a body) to show no soul departs at death.
- **Kāśyapa** — Buddhist interlocutor who answers Pāyāsi: thrashing a body to find the soul is like thrashing a trumpet to get its music; a methodological, not a metaphysical, objection.
- **Keśi** — Jain interlocutor who answers Pāyāsi's "ant and elephant have equal souls" with the lamp-and-bushel image: one soul animates bodies large or small per the karma of past lives.
- **Bṛhaspati** — reputed author of the Cārvāka-Sūtra; states that the four elements are the reals, consciousness "is formed out of" them, and thinking is "from the body alone."
- **Cārvāka-Sūtra** — the school's systematizing canonical text; its opening aphorisms give the naturalist account of mind (and the peacock's-tail line on variety).
- **intoxication analogy** — consciousness arises from the elements as the power to intoxicate arises from fermenting ingredients; the **combination** yields a genuinely new power.
- **ablative ambiguity** — the elements-noun's case allows three readings: mind *made from* / *because of* / *produced out of* the elements — hence the interpretive disputes.
- **Huxley's steam whistle / clock bell** — T. H. Huxley's images for mind as a causally inert byproduct; the reductive reading of Bṛhaspati that the intoxication analogy argues against.
- **Prabhācandra** — 11th-c. Jain reporter who lays out the options: mind = body (identity), mind as a **guṇa**/quality (byproduct), or mind as an **effect** of the body (the emergentist reading).
- **Jayanta** — 9th-c. Nyāya reporter who details Cārvāka's epistemology: the self is given by neither perception, introspection, inference, nor scriptural testimony.
- **ātman / puruṣa** — self vs. person; Bṛhaspati pointedly speaks of generating the *person* (puruṣa), not a self (ātman) independent of body.
- **guṇa** — "quality/state" (term borrowed from Sāṃkhya); one option for relating mind to body.
- **top-down vs bottom-up** — Vedānta derives matter from mind (top-down); Cārvāka derives mind from matter (bottom-up) — yet emergent mind can still exert top-down causation.

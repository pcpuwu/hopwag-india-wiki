## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **The Vedas / Ṛg Veda** — the Aryans' revelatory scriptures; the Ṛg Veda (**c. 1200 BCE**) is the oldest, source of the horse-sacrifice and *Puruṣa* hymns [00:04:55].
- **The Upaniṣads** — later texts expounding the meaning of the Vedas; the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad supplies the horse–cosmos analogy and the king-versus-priest dialogue [00:08:21].
- **The Buddha and Mahāvīra** — **5th-c. BCE** founders of Buddhism and Jainism, both from the warrior class, who challenged Brahminical claims [00:11:54].
- **The Mauryan rulers** — Chandragupta, his advisor Kauṭilya (*Arthaśāstra*), and Aśoka, showing elites adopting the dissenters' teachings [00:14:50].

## Animals and the horse that spurred Indian philosophy
- [00:00:39] Despite being unable to talk, animals keep turning up in the history of philosophy: the Cynics named after dogs, the medievals' donkey starving between two equal bales of hay, the giraffes of a certain podcast. Turning to India you might expect cows — but the animal with the strongest claim to spurring Indian philosophy is **the horse**.
- [00:01:06] The horse is not indigenous to the subcontinent. It migrated in with a people who called themselves the **Aryans**, and with them came the Indo-European language that would become **Sanskrit** and the religious beliefs that fed into the **Vedas**, the scriptures to which so much of Indian philosophy responds.

## The land and the people before the Aryans
- [00:01:28] The Aryans were not the first inhabitants. Archaeology shows earlier civilizations with cities in the **Indus Valley** as early as the **3rd millennium BCE**.
- [00:01:47] Geography lesson: picture the subcontinent as a diamond — broad and blunt at the north, tapering to a point in the south where it juts into the Indian Ocean. The northern join with Asia is mountainous — the **Himalayas** and the **Hindu Kush** — and waters flow down into a plain dominated by two rivers, the **Indus** (northwest) and the **Ganges** (due north). Further mountains divide this **Indo-Gangetic plain** from the central plateau, the **Deccan** — whose name comes from the word for "south," since it lies south of the Indo-Gangetic region. Most of the story plays out in the northern plain and the Deccan.
- [00:02:46] The backstory begins along the Indus: the **Harappa** and **Mohenjo-daro** civilizations, before the Aryans. These were not primitive societies — well-laid-out cities, and the Harappans traded as far as **Mesopotamia**. Some writing survives but the **Harappan script** is undeciphered; we don't even know its language group, possibly **Dravidian** (which includes Tamil). Numerous pre-Aryan languages were spoken, and their influence can be detected in the Sanskrit of the Vedas.

## How the Aryans arrived — and the tell-tale absence of horses
- [00:03:30] The story of the Aryans' arrival has attracted "wild speculation, indeed ideologically inspired fantasy dressed up as sober scholarship." It used to be thought they swept in as conquerors on horse-drawn chariots, only to be "corrupted" by the "inferior races" of India. Scholars now reject such racist accounts and lean toward a picture of **gradual migration** rather than military conquest.
- [00:04:06] But the Aryans really did have horse-drawn chariots. The seals left by the Harappans feature many animals — even the hippopotamus ("river horse") — but never actual horses. This contrasts sharply with the Vedas, which mention horses constantly and put a horse at the centre of one of the most important rituals.

## The horse sacrifice and the shape of Vedic religion
- [00:04:32] In the famous **horse sacrifice**, a stallion was let roam free for a year, then killed and dismembered, its parts offered to the gods. Only a **king** could perform it, with the help of priests who were "not just skilled intermediaries to the gods, but also handy with a knife."
- [00:04:55] The oldest Veda, the **Ṛg Veda** (**c. 1200 BCE**), describes it, with instructions to the priest: "The axe cuts through the 34 ribs of the racehorse, who is the companion of the gods. Keep the limbs undamaged… cut them apart, calling out peace by peace."
- [00:05:21] The horse sacrifice was exalted, but it captures the social dynamic of Vedic practice generally: animals, dairy products, and most famously the hallucinogenic plant **soma** were offered into flames. The sacrifices were a collaboration between two elite classes — the priests (**brahmins**) and the aristocrat-warriors (**kṣatriyas**).
- [00:05:45] The **caste/class system** was completed by two more groups: the tradesmen and farmers of the commoner (**vaiśya**) class, and the serfs or labourers (**śūdra**). This last group was entirely excluded from Vedic ritual and would have had its own separate religious practices.

## Puruṣa: cosmology that justifies the class system
- [00:06:04] The Ṛg Veda refers to the four classes and explains them through a cosmology centred on the word **puruṣa** — which can mean "the self," but here is characterized as "all that hath yet been and all that is yet to be."
- [00:06:24] The gods are said to have sacrificed Puruṣa, dividing it just as a priest cuts up an animal. From Puruṣa came various species — goats, cattle, horses — as well as the four classes of humankind: "The priest was his mouth, of both his arms was the warrior made. His thighs became the commoner, from his feet the labourer was produced."
- [00:06:59] You might feel you're listening to a *history* podcast rather than a history-of-*philosophy* one — but this passage already carries far-reaching philosophical themes. Equating Puruṣa with all things, it anticipates the later **Upaniṣads**, which will argue that a knowledge of the self (**puruṣa** or **ātman**) is tantamount to a knowledge of the whole world.
- [00:07:36] Beyond the self–universe unity, the hymn suggests reality can be divided into **natural parts**, like a skilfully butchered sacrificial animal — and here that becomes a justification for the **class system**. To our eyes this looks self-serving, representing the interests of the very Brahmin and Kṣatriya classes who carried out and benefited from the sacrifices.

## Microcosm and macrocosm — the body as a map of the cosmos
- [00:08:04] But there is more than cynical rationalization here. Again and again the Vedas and Upaniṣads draw **systematic parallels** between the parts of bodies and the parts of the universe.
- [00:08:21] "At the risk of beating a dead horse," another example: the very opening of the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka** ("Great Forest") **Upaniṣad** identifies the parts of a sacrificial horse with parts of the cosmos — its head is the dawn, its body the year, its back the sky, its feet the days and nights, its bones the stars, its flesh the clouds. It concludes: "when it yawns, lightning flashes; when it shakes itself, it thunders; when it urinates, it rains. Its neighing is speech itself."
- [00:09:00] The lesson: understanding the parts and powers of horses and humans, we "travel a path from **microcosm to macrocosm**." Studying the structure of animals and humans is *the* way to uncover the hidden structure of all things. The priest is the paradigm case: the Great Forest Upaniṣad says he is "**brahman** personified, a whole world in himself."
- [00:09:28] This holistic understanding was the stock-in-trade of the Brahmin class. Only they had expertise in the fine points of ritual and insight into the gods — which is why the warrior **kṣatriyas** needed them. Thanks to the rituals, the kṣatriyas could hope for divine favour: insurance against calamity, large and healthy families, ever-increasing wealth; and, more ambitiously, an explanation of the origin of all things, as in the Ṛg Veda's question about "that one who established and fixed firm these worlds' six regions."

## Cows for wisdom — and the priest who lost the argument
- [00:10:16] What did the Brahmins get out of the deal? "It's here that the cows come in." In the Upaniṣads a sage is repeatedly promised **head of cattle** in return for wisdom — occasion for gentle comedy: a philosopher comes to court, the king asks whether he's after cows or subtle disquisitions, and the philosopher replies, "Both, your majesty."
- [00:10:40] But the Brahmins' knowledge-claims did not go unchallenged. The Great Forest Upaniṣad describes a dialogue between a king named **Ajātaśatru** and a priest named **Dṛpta-Bālāki**, who offers — for a thousand cows if convincing — to give an account of **brahman**, fundamental reality. He proposes brahman resides in the sun; the king rejects it (the sun may be greatest of beings, but is not brahman itself). The priest tries the moon, space, wind, water, sound, body — the king "slaps down every suggestion using almost the same words each time."
- [00:11:29] Finally Dṛpta-Bālāki admits defeat and asks to learn from the king instead. The king agrees, even though it is "a reversal of the norm" for a Brahmin to become the pupil of a kṣatriya — a heavy-handed hint that the priests might have something to learn from the warriors.

## The 5th-century challengers: the Buddha and Mahāvīra
- [00:11:49] That hint was as nothing compared to the challenges that emerged around the **5th century BCE** — the age of the **Buddha** and of **Mahāvīra**. The tradition Mahāvīra founded, **Jainism**, takes its name from his epithet **Jina**, "conqueror."
- [00:12:06] Tellingly, both came from the **kṣatriya** aristocracy, not the priestly Brahmin class, and both abandoned their wealth for lives of **ascetic restraint** — already an implicit criticism of the literal and spiritual economy at the heart of the Brahminical tradition.
- [00:12:30] Sometimes the criticism was explicit: Buddhists and Jains increasingly doubted the **efficacy of Vedic rituals** and the very possibility of the understanding the Brahmins claimed. No wonder Indian philosophy became obsessed early on with the **methods that lead to knowledge** — knowledge was the very commodity that earned the Brahmins their livelihood. In these debates, an entire elite way of life, not just epistemology, was at stake.

## Liberation and the cycle of rebirth
- [00:12:52] But Buddhism and Jainism offered more than criticism — they promised what no ritual could deliver: **liberation**. The classic Brahminical texts already allude to a belief most readily associated with India: the **cycle of reincarnation**. The **Bhagavad-Gītā**: "As a man casts off his worn-out clothes and takes on new ones, so does the self cast off its worn-out bodies and enter new ones."
- [00:13:26] Rather than being relieved at returning to bodily existence again and again, Indian thinkers often set as their goal an **escape** from the relentless cycle. Consistently with Vedic literature's exaltation of knowledge, a later Upaniṣad states: "When a man has understanding, is mindful and always pure, he reaches that final step from which he is not reborn again."
- [00:13:54] The Buddhists and Jains offered their own versions of liberation — the Buddhists going so far as to subject to radical critique the very idea of a **self or soul** that could pass from one body to another.

## From insurgents to establishment: Buddhism becomes a world religion
- [00:14:07] You might think these dissenting schools were pure insurgents against the elite. But Buddhism would itself become a major **world religion**, a process beginning in antiquity. The Buddhists, though dismissive of Vedic rituals, had their own observances — for instance different burial practices.
- [00:14:38] It did not take long for elites to adopt — if not co-opt — the new teachings. The powerful **Mauryan Empire** held much of the subcontinent around the **3rd century BCE**. It was founded in **321 BCE** by **Chandragupta Maurya**, who expanded by exploiting the power vacuum left when **Alexander the Great**'s armies pulled out of northwestern India.
- [00:15:08] Chandragupta had a Brahmin advisor, **Kauṭilya**, said to be author of a political treatise, the **Arthaśāstra** (to be covered in a future episode). The dynasty shows the dissenters' ideas could also appeal to the ruling class. Chandragupta himself is said to have **abdicated** after becoming an adherent of Jainism.

## Aśoka and the Greek–Indian interpenetration
- [00:15:38] More evidence survives for Chandragupta's grandson **Aśoka**. Appalled by the violence he witnessed in his campaign against **Kaliṅga**, Aśoka sought to wield royal power in line with **Buddhist** teachings: his armies would make war only in self-defence, and as ruler he would look to the welfare of all his subjects.
- [00:16:03] Aśoka had **inscriptions** erected across his empire in many languages — his edicts were even translated into **Greek and Aramaic** for the northwestern regions, one example of the **interpenetration of Greek and Indian culture** after Alexander.
- [00:16:17] In the **Hellenistic period** the Bactrian ruler **Demetrius** extended his rule into the Punjab and Indus Valley, and tradition records that another Greek king, **Menander**, converted to Buddhism — a supposed dialogue between him and a Buddhist teacher named **Nāgasena** survives as the *Questions of Menander* (also to return in a future episode).

## Where we go next
- [00:16:43] Before getting into the Buddhist and Jaina traditions and their critique of Brahminical culture, we need to understand better what they were criticizing — first and foremost the **Upaniṣads**, works that set out to expound the meaning of the Vedic texts and their rituals.
- [00:17:01] Devised over many generations, the Upaniṣads are "monuments of world literature" and foundational texts of the **Hindu** religion, full of philosophical material — sometimes suggestive and metaphorical, sometimes "frankly argumentative and disputatious." The next installment begins to tackle the Upaniṣads.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Aryans** — horse-borne, Sanskrit-speaking migrants whose religious beliefs entered the Vedas; arrived by gradual migration, not (as once claimed) violent conquest.
- **Indus Valley / Harappa & Mohenjo-daro** — sophisticated pre-Aryan cities (from the **3rd millennium BCE**) that traded with Mesopotamia; their script is still undeciphered, possibly Dravidian.
- **Vedas / Ṛg Veda** — the Aryans' revelatory scriptures; the Ṛg Veda (**c. 1200 BCE**) is the oldest, containing the horse-sacrifice instructions and the *Puruṣa* hymn.
- **horse sacrifice (aśvamedha)** — a king's stallion let roam a year, then dismembered and offered to the gods; emblem of Vedic ritual, performed by king plus priests.
- **soma** — a hallucinogenic plant offered into the sacrificial flames.
- **four classes (varṇa)** — brahmin (priest), kṣatriya (warrior/aristocrat), vaiśya (commoner/tradesman), śūdra (serf/labourer, excluded from Vedic ritual).
- **puruṣa** — cosmic self/person equated with "all that has been and will be"; sacrificed and divided by the gods, the four classes issuing from its body-parts.
- **ātman** — the self; the Upaniṣads claim that knowing it is tantamount to knowing the whole world.
- **brahman** — fundamental reality; the priest is said to be "brahman personified, a whole world in himself."
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad** — source of the horse–cosmos analogy and of the dialogue in which king **Ajātaśatru** out-argues the priest **Dṛpta-Bālāki** about brahman.
- **Upaniṣads** — later texts expounding the Vedas; foundational Hindu works, philosophically suggestive and disputatious.
- **the Buddha & Mahāvīra** — **5th-c. BCE** founders of Buddhism and Jainism (Jainism named for Mahāvīra's epithet **Jina**, "conqueror"); both kṣatriyas who renounced wealth and challenged Brahminical ritual and knowledge-claims.
- **reincarnation & liberation** — the cycle of rebirth (Bhagavad-Gītā's "worn-out clothes" image); escape from it is the goal, the Buddhists even denying a persisting self that could transmigrate.
- **Mauryan Empire** — founded **321 BCE** by **Chandragupta Maurya** (advised by **Kauṭilya**, author of the **Arthaśāstra**; said to have abdicated as a Jain) and continued by **Aśoka**, who governed by Buddhist principles after Kaliṅga and erected multilingual edicts.
- **Greek–Indian contact** — Aśoka's Greek/Aramaic edicts, Bactrian **Demetrius** in the Punjab, and **Menander**'s conversion to Buddhism (the *Questions of Menander*, with **Nāgasena**).

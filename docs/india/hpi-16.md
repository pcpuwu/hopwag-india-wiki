## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **The Laws of Manu** — Brahminical law-code; quoted both for stark misogyny and (later) for passages leaning the other way [00:01:06].
- **The Arthaśāstra of Kauṭilya** — the political treatise, whose third book regulates marriage, divorce, and sexual offences [00:02:50].
- **The Therīgāthā / Theragāthā** — paired anthologies in the Buddhist Pāli canon, of poems by nuns and by monks respectively [00:09:09].
- **Gārgī Vācaknavī & Maitreyī** — female interlocutors of the sage Yājñavalkya in the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad [00:11:16].
- **Sulabhā** — wandering female ascetic of the *Mahābhārata* who out-argues King Janaka; "the most astonishing example of a female philosopher in antique India" [00:14:55].

## Half the population, "not the better half"
- [00:00:36] A few episodes back we imagined ourselves in ancient India and weighed the social roles on offer — Brahmin, warrior, monk, farmer. But there was a roughly **50% chance** you'd have been a woman. As in other pre-modern literary traditions, Indian writings were composed mostly **by men and for men**; from the authors' point of view, the half of the population that was female was "not the better half."
- [00:01:06] A classic statement of misogyny is in the **Laws of Manu**: "A wife should do nothing independently even in her own house. Though her husband be uncouth and prone to pleasure, though he has no good points at all, the virtuous wife should ever worship her lord as a god."
- [00:01:24] Many other texts in the series so far share the attitude. Discussions of **reincarnation** took for granted that it is worse to be reborn a woman than a man; political treatises excluded women from leading the state; and **philosophical wisdom** was usually a male privilege. It was men who taught other men to win liberation through renunciation, knowledge, and self-discipline — even when the men in question were gods like **Indra** or **Kṛṣṇa**.
- [00:01:54] And yet the **life of renunciation** was open to women too. There were nuns in both the **Buddhist** and **Jaina** communities — though, as we'll see, this was controversial, and nuns never held the status of monks. Occasionally both Hindu and dissident literature concedes that women can reach **mokṣa**, liberation.
- [00:02:19] More remarkable still are the **female thinkers and sages** who appear in the Upaniṣads and the Mahābhārata, depicted holding their own with men — even defeating them in argument. Still, these texts rarely if ever challenge the assumption that women are generally inferior. The verdict: the female thinkers of Indian literature are "not separate, but neither are they equal."

## The Arthaśāstra: women under the ideal king
- [00:02:50] To gauge the general status of women, turn again to the **Arthaśāstra of Kauṭilya**, the political treatise that reveals so much about the society the ideal king would rule. Its third book has much to say about women — and it "makes for rather grim reading."
- [00:03:09] It lays out rules on the **beating of wives**, fines for women who flirt, and a scale of punishment for **rapists** — with those who rape slaves treated more leniently than those who assault free women. Kauṭilya even recognizes a kind of marriage that is effectively just **abduction** (though, "on the bright side," in that case the wife's property is not transferred to the new husband).
- [00:03:31] **Divorce** is mostly the husband's to initiate, though a wife can sometimes leave — e.g. if he goes abroad for a prolonged period. In general the text's interest in women is confined to **regulating sexual activity and the transfer of property** within families.

## A full half of oneself: the wife in Vedic ritual
- [00:03:54] What of the religious side of Brahminical culture? An important role was reserved for the **wife of a sacrificer**. Only men could sacrifice, but the ritual was effective only if the wife was **present**. One text: "A full half of oneself is one's wife. As long as one does not obtain a wife, one can never be reborn, for he then remains incomplete."
- [00:04:19] Even so, the wife is not the husband's better half — her role is **above all to enable the husband** to achieve his sacrificial purposes. When women appear in ritual symbolism, it is usually as comparisons between **ritual objects and her sexual organs**. Sex itself carries ritual overtones: the Upaniṣads say a man can use **knowledge** to acquire the merits of a woman through intercourse. Women can acquire men's merit too — "but without the part about knowledge."
- [00:04:54] The **virtue of women** is all-important — not because we should care about women being virtuous, but because a wife's virtue brings **karmic benefit to her husband**.

## Reproducing oneself: marriage, sons, and the gendered self
- [00:05:04] Along the same lines, the Upaniṣads present marriage's chief purpose as providing a man with children — or rather with **sons**. Through the wife the husband "literally reproduces himself," so that seeing his son is like seeing his own face in a mirror: "the father is the same as the son, and the son is the same as the father."
- [00:05:30] This finds its ultimate expression in the so-called **right of transfer** — a ritual in which a man and his son lie one atop the other to pass on **karma and vital functions** across the generations.
- [00:05:43] The Vedic tradition even seems to assume that the **ātman**, the self belonging to each of us, has a **gender** — "and you'll never guess which one." The self is associated with male gods like **Puruṣa** and **Prajāpati**; we hear of the ātman producing a **female body from his own** and then immediately having intercourse with her.
- [00:06:04] The Upaniṣads do recognize that women — and animals, perhaps even plants — have an inner self. So the message seems to be that at the core "we are all men," even those "unfortunate enough" to be living a current life in a female body.

## Why asceticism was so radical
- [00:06:21] Here, incidentally, is one reason **asceticism** was such a radical development. Renouncing the householder life, as **Buddhist and Jaina monks** did, meant renouncing **sex and procreation**. Their vow of chastity was not merely self-discipline but a **departure from deeply held ideas** about transferring karma — and even the self — from parent to child.
- [00:06:46] If chastity was a dramatic gesture for men, it was "downright shocking" when **women** renounced family life. A householding-class woman who became a nun was defying her **dharma twice over**, rejecting the duties of both **class and gender**.
- [00:07:04] The warrior **kṣatriya**'s task is to fight; the humble **śūdra**'s is to serve his betters; likewise woman has a special dharma — to be a **devoted wife and bear children**. Monasticism offered an **escape** from these expectations. Much as ancient Christian women turned to asceticism to avoid the constrictions of family life, so Buddhist and Jaina nuns may have taken vows to live a life that was, in a sense, **less cloistered** and held out greater prospects of **individual fulfilment**.

## Jain disagreement: can a woman reach liberation?
- [00:07:38] Unsurprisingly this was seen as problematic even within the Buddhist and Jaina movements. The **Digambara** branch of Jainism includes **nudity** among its severe ascetic practices. Since it was thought women could not decently or safely follow the lead of their "sky-clad" brethren, they were effectively **barred from the perfect renunciation** that leads to liberation.
- [00:08:04] The **Śvetāmbara** Jainas, by contrast, hold that **women can achieve this goal**. They have even claimed that one of the **ford-makers** — the line of ancient teachers up to **Mahāvīra** who taught the way to liberation — was a **woman**.

## The Buddha and his nuns
- [00:08:18] As for the **Buddha**, no stranger to radical ideas, even he reportedly struggled to accept that women should join his community of renouncers. **Nuns** were admitted to his **Saṅgha** only a few years after he began accepting men as monks, and when he relented — ordaining his own **aunt** — it was "with misgivings."
- [00:08:40] He is quoted as saying a monastic community that includes women is like a **field afflicted by mildew**, and that with this change in the rules the lifespan of his teaching would be **halved from 1,000 to 500 years**. Once admitted, nuns were made **subservient to the monks** and required to submit to their authority.
- [00:08:57] Even so this could seem preferable to staying home as a good wife. One Buddhist nun "rejoiced in her freedom from three things: **mortar, pestle, and my crooked husband**."

## The Therīgāthā: poems of the nuns
- [00:09:09] That quotation is from a work in the Buddhist **Pāli canon** devoted entirely to women — an anthology of poems called the **Therīgāthā**. Though its women have refused to live as men's consorts, the text is a **companion to a second work** about Buddhist monks, the **Theragāthā**; some passages appear in **both**, with small changes.
- [00:09:34] For example, both include a story of a **chaste hero** who rebuffs a seduction. In the female version, a nun approached by a sexual predator tries to dissuade him by **discoursing on the decaying nature of bodily things** — describing her own body as a corpse and a "painted puppet." When that fails, she **removes one of her eyes** and hands it to him, which does the trick. (A note to **Oedipus**: better to tear out your eyes *before* getting into an unwise sexual relationship.)
- [00:10:09] The passage is typical of the Therīgāthā, full of meditations on **decay, illness, and death** in aid of suppressing bodily desire — a theme even **more prominent** here than in the partner text for monks, perhaps suggesting the authors (who may themselves have been **male**) believed women more prone to the lure of bodily pleasure. Sexist assumptions notwithstanding, the Therīgāthā is remarkable for its assumption that **women can indeed achieve liberation**.

## Hindu literature: appalling attitudes, and the other direction
- [00:10:40] What about **Hindu** literature? The Upaniṣads and Laws of Manu can be mined for appalling attitudes — but that isn't the whole story. As so often, the **Laws of Manu** also contain passages leaning the other way, including one that allows **women to pursue the life of a wandering ascetic**.
- [00:11:09] And the Upaniṣads give women a role not only in ritual but in **philosophical debate**. Back in episode four we met, in the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka** ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad, a female sage named **Gārgī Vācaknavī**, who goes "toe to toe" with the philosophical hero **Yājñavalkya**.
- [00:11:29] Admittedly she is **silenced**: told she goes "too far" when she repeatedly asks what underlies each in a successive series of cosmic principles — "keep this up and your **head will explode**," Yājñavalkya warns. Still, Gārgī **outdoes the other male Brahmins** present: she acknowledges Yājñavalkya's pre-eminence when they do not, and — head-shattering threats aside — she is ready to **question him a second time** after his first answers leave her unsatisfied.

## Maitreyī: love of the self, and of a husband
- [00:11:57] Nor is that Yājñavalkya's only female interlocutor. Elsewhere in the Great Forest Upaniṣad he discusses the **self** with his wife **Maitreyī**. The scene begins with **Maitreyī asking how she can attain immortality** — note that she asks this **for herself**, not how to help her husband live forever.
- [00:12:31] Given the context this might seem presumptuous, but Yājñavalkya is "far from affronted." He **favours Maitreyī** over his second wife precisely for her **philosophical keenness**, contrasted with the other wife's interest in "nothing but womanly matters." He even seems to see the spousal relation in unusually **equal terms**: it's no surprise to hear "it is out of love for **oneself** that one holds a **wife** dear" — but very surprising to also hear "it is out of love for oneself that one holds a **husband** dear."

## The Mahābhārata's powerful women: Draupadī
- [00:13:06] True to its claim to contain all things, the **Mahābhārata** features several powerful female characters. The most obvious is **Draupadī**, wife of all five **Pāṇḍava** brothers and thus a major figure. Like **Arjuna** and other male heroes, she receives **portentous predictions at birth**: she will lead the kṣatriyas to their doom — and she does, urging warlike behaviour and **castigating** her husband **Yudhiṣṭhira** for failing to follow the warrior dharma.
- [00:13:43] She has good reason: she was **staked in a game of dice** and treated with great disrespect at the fateful gambling match that launches the family conflict.
- [00:13:53] Draupadī also counters the notion that these texts were aimed **solely at men**. Within the epic's framing devices, she and other women often serve as the **audience** for stories and lessons, making **claims to knowledge** and figuring as potential learners.
- [00:14:18] This fits a tradition about the whole **Mahābhārata**: that it was composed **for women and śūdras** — a "fifth Veda," a **Veda for everyone**, whereas the other Vedas are for the priestly and ruling classes. The idea sits awkwardly with the epic's obsessive interest in warrior dharma, but it echoes in the **Bhagavad-Gītā**, where **Kṛṣṇa** teaches that even women and śūdras can attain liberation through **devotion to him**.

## Sulabhā: the most astonishing female philosopher
- [00:14:55] Another section of the Mahābhārata offers "the most astonishing example of a female philosopher in antique India" — **Sulabhā**. Her story comes where **Bhīṣma**, on his deathbed, explains techniques of liberation to **Yudhiṣṭhira**. He tells of a king named **Janaka**, who claims to have achieved liberation **while still carrying out his royal duties**.
- [00:15:19] Sulabhā, who won liberation the **old-fashioned way** — rejecting the householder life and **wandering as a rootless ascetic** — finds it hard to believe Janaka has really attained freedom while living as a king. So she does "the obvious thing": uses her **yoga power** to transform into a stunningly beautiful woman, **teleport** to his court, and **implant her mind into his body**, so the two can hold an **internal dialogue** within Janaka's soul.
- [00:15:55] King Janaka speaks first, in "arrogant and disdainful" terms, boasting that he reached freedom **without asceticism**, through **knowledge** alone, which lets him resist all attachment — "for him it is the same whether someone anoints his right arm or cuts off his left." This **ethic of unattached action** is the very teaching **Kṛṣṇa** offers in the Bhagavad-Gītā, so we might expect his claims to be vindicated.
- [00:16:20] But Janaka grows less appealing as he **rails against Sulabhā** — who is this upstart young woman who dares enter his royal presence, and his very **body**?

## A perfect speech that exposes the king
- [00:16:39] Now Sulabhā responds. She first instructs Janaka on the **qualities of good speech** and promises to exemplify them: her remarks will be pleasing, **neither too long nor too short**, adapted to her audience, and **free of emotion**. (A characteristic feature of ancient Indian writing: the **form of speech is inextricably bound up with the truth it teaches**.)
- [00:17:03] Her perfect discourse then **exposes the king's hypocrisy**. Using ideas reminiscent of the **Sāṃkhya** tradition, she argues that the **true self** is more fundamental than, and underlies, all other features of a person. Janaka's very demand to know **who she is** — his interest in her **gender and class** — shows he does **not understand this basic truth**.
- [00:17:26] Furthermore, the **life of a king is incompatible with liberation**: he must constantly cope with the demands of other political actors and follow the kṣatriya dharma, rather than devoting himself **solely to spiritual concerns** as Sulabhā has.

## Undoing gender: the genderless true self
- [00:17:46] The narrator praises Sulabhā for her **appropriate and convincing words**. She does **better even than Gārgī**: she bests her rival philosophically and successfully challenges ideas **actually defended elsewhere** in the Mahābhārata. Along the way she **defies and undermines traditional ideas about gender** — for, according to her, the **true self is neither male nor female**, but the underlying **source** of this and all other differences.
- [00:18:15] And the very depiction of such a character is itself an **affront** to those traditional ideas. It was often believed women **could not attain liberation** — either through "fundamental inferiority," or because they could not undertake rigorous asceticism, being "too vulnerable without men to protect them." But **Sulabhā wanders free**: she has refused to act as a woman is expected to, and "knows exactly what she is not doing."

## Do these prove real female sages? Maybe the question is backwards
- [00:18:43] Do Sulabhā, and figures like **Gārgī** and **Maitreyī**, prove there really were female Hindu sages in antiquity? "It's hard to say." We've mostly been asking what the texts tell us about how **women were seen**, assuming they reflect historical reality. But "perhaps this is **backwards**" — we could instead ask what the inclusion of such women tells us about the **texts**.
- [00:19:14] Notably, the strong female philosophers of the Great Forest Upaniṣad are **both associated with the even stronger male philosopher**, **Yājñavalkya**. His willingness to debate women on a roughly equal footing is "unusual, if not unique" — so the point may be to mark **him** as an **innovative, idiosyncratic figure**, rather than to insist women can do philosophy, much less to record that real female philosophers existed.
- [00:19:41] It's also striking that figures like **Gārgī** and **Sulabhā** are presented in various ways as **masculine**: Gārgī refers to herself as a **warrior** (using a grammatically male-gendered word); Maitreyī is pointedly contrasted with Yājñavalkya's "more feminine" other wife; and Sulabhā delivers her speech **from within the body of a man**. Nothing could more powerfully symbolize "the fundamental fact" that when these ancient women — whether fictional or real — speak to us, their speech is **always filtered through the agency of men**.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Laws of Manu** — Brahminical law-code; quoted for stark misogyny ("the virtuous wife should ever worship her lord as a god") but also for passages allowing a woman to become a wandering ascetic.
- **Arthaśāstra (of Kauṭilya)** — political treatise whose third book regulates wife-beating, divorce, property transfer, and rape; its interest in women is largely confined to sex and property.
- **ātman** — the self; the Vedic tradition seems to assume it is **male** (associated with Puruṣa, Prajāpati), so that "at core we are all men."
- **right of transfer** — ritual in which father and son lie one atop the other to pass on karma and vital functions across generations.
- **dharma (of women)** — a woman's prescribed duty: to be a devoted wife and bear children; a nun defies the dharma of both class and gender.
- **mokṣa** — liberation; debated as to whether women can attain it.
- **Digambara vs Śvetāmbara** — Jain branches: the "sky-clad" Digambaras effectively bar women from perfect (naked) renunciation; the Śvetāmbaras hold women can reach liberation, even claiming a female ford-maker.
- **ford-makers** — the line of ancient Jaina teachers, up to Mahāvīra, who taught the way to liberation.
- **Saṅgha** — the Buddha's monastic community; he admitted nuns reluctantly ("a field afflicted by mildew"; the teaching's lifespan halved from 1,000 to 500 years), making them subservient to monks.
- **Therīgāthā / Theragāthā** — paired Pāli-canon anthologies of poems by nuns / by monks; the nuns' poems dwell heavily on bodily decay and assume women can achieve liberation.
- **Gārgī Vācaknavī** — female sage of the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* Upaniṣad who out-argues the male Brahmins and questions Yājñavalkya twice, until warned her "head will explode"; refers to herself as a warrior.
- **Maitreyī** — Yājñavalkya's philosophically keen wife, who asks how *she* may attain immortality; in their dialogue he says one holds a husband dear out of love for oneself.
- **Yājñavalkya** — the Upaniṣadic "philosophical hero" whose unusual willingness to debate women may mark him as an idiosyncratic individual rather than evidence of real female sages.
- **Draupadī** — wife of the five Pāṇḍava brothers; given a portentous birth-prophecy, staked at the fateful dice game, urges warrior dharma on Yudhiṣṭhira.
- **Mahābhārata as "fifth Veda"** — tradition that the epic was composed for women and śūdras — a Veda for everyone — echoed by the Gītā's claim that women and śūdras can reach liberation through devotion to Kṛṣṇa.
- **Sulabhā** — wandering female ascetic who uses yoga power to enter King Janaka's body and, in a "perfect speech" drawing on Sāṃkhya, exposes his hypocrisy and argues the true self is neither male nor female.
- **Janaka** — king who claims liberation through knowledge while still ruling; bested by Sulabhā, who shows the royal life is incompatible with liberation.

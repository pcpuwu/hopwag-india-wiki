## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Mahāvīra** — last of the Jain ford-makers, source of the conditional-yes answers and the omniscient standpoint [00:02:35].
- **Siddhasena** — Jain philosopher quoted on how a manifold thing is fully comprehended only by the omniscient [00:02:47].
- **Vādideva Sūri** — 11th-century Jain who lays out the seven-fold predicate theory with particular clarity; quoted at length [00:06:43].
- **Prabhācandra** — 10th-century Jain (born **980**), offers the "neutrality" reading of the inexpressible [00:13:08].
- **syāt / saptabhaṅgī** — the qualifying particle "in a sense" and the seven modes of predication built on it [00:04:27].

## The test a theory must survive: being applied to itself
- [00:00:31] When you evaluate a philosophical theory there are standard tests: Does it preserve our intuitions? If not, does it give sufficient reason to abandon our previous beliefs? Does it fall prey to counter-examples or leave exceptions unexplained? And one more that must never be omitted — **can the theory survive being applied to itself?**
- [00:00:54] We saw the Buddhist **Nāgārjuna** wrestle with exactly this in his *Refutation of My Opponents*: the objection that his arguments for the **emptiness** thesis can be dismissed, since Nāgārjuna himself must admit those very arguments are empty.
- [00:01:17] The Jain theory of **standpoints** (*naya*), examined last episode, looks just as vulnerable. The Jains say philosophical theories are true only from a certain perspective — but is *that* assertion itself only the expression of a certain standpoint?

## The Jain reply on universals: standpoints all the way up
- [00:01:35] The Jains can answer readily. Take their approach to the metaphysics of **universals**. Adopting the theory of *naya*s, they say there is a perspective that affirms things sharing a universal character, and another that emphasizes the reality of individual particulars.
- [00:01:52] From the first standpoint, a **realist** about universals argues that all pots share "potness" in common. From the second, a **skeptic** about universals says only each individual pot is real.
- [00:02:05] The Jain says **both standpoints are in a sense correct**, because each captures something about reality. In saying so, the Jain adopts a **third standpoint** — the one from which both the universal and the particular ways of looking have contributed something to our understanding.
- [00:02:22] That third standpoint may itself be partial. But there is also the prospect of a **most comprehensive, omniscient point of view** that incorporates all standpoints — the point of view of someone like **Mahāvīra**, the last of the Jain ford-makers. It is the only fully adequate standpoint, precisely because of the complexity of things in the world.
- [00:02:47] As the Jain philosopher **Siddhasena** puts it: "since a thing has a manifold character, it is fully comprehended only by the omniscient, but a thing becomes the subject matter of a *naya* when it is conceived from one particular standpoint." We are not omniscient, yet we can make progress by understanding how each philosophical theory contributes a *merely partial* truth. So the self-refutation charge fails: the Jain claim isn't one more dogmatic thesis, but a higher-order, partial-but-progressing standpoint.

## The harder test: do the Jains contradict themselves?
- [00:03:11] There is a simpler, more dangerous test: does the theory involve **self-contradiction**? This threat also loomed over **Nāgārjuna**, whose **tetralemma** has seemed to some to involve entertaining contradictory propositions. Adamson resisted that reading for Nāgārjuna and wants to resist it for the Jains too.
- [00:03:34] You can see why they get accused. As philosophical **pluralists**, the Jains answer paired questions like "Is the world infinite?" and "Is the world finite?" with a **qualified yes in both cases** — each expresses a partial truth.
- [00:03:52] Many commentators, past and present, have felt that the Jain attempt to reconcile **opposite** positions amounts to challenging the notion of contradiction — that it *tolerates inconsistency*. How else could they find something true in both the **Buddhist no-self** theory and the **Advaita** theory that all things are really a single self, namely **brahman**?
- [00:04:15] No less than Nāgārjuna, the Jains are alive to the threat, and seek to show how one can have **impartiality / non-one-sidedness** (*anekāntavāda*) without lapsing into self-contradiction.

## The little word that does the work: *syāt*
- [00:04:27] Their answer centers on a single Sanskrit particle, **syāt**. It is an optative form of the verb "to be," meaning "possibly" or "perhaps" — but in this context it is better rendered "**in a sense**," or "**conditionally**," or even "arguably."
- [00:04:48] *Syāt* captures the Jain **refusal to make categorical assertions**: instead they answer questions from one or another perspective, within the framework of a certain metaphysical stance. So rather than asserting flatly that the world is eternal, we say only, conditionally, "the world is infinite" — meaning *from a certain standpoint* the universe is infinite.
- [00:05:11] Compare the words modern logicians call **modal operators** — "possibly," "necessarily." They indicate the *way* in which a statement is true. "Possibly, the universe is infinite" flags that something *might* be true; "necessarily…" flags something that *must* be true. In neither case am I straightforwardly asserting the infinity of the universe. *Syāt* works the same way: it modulates the assertion rather than baldly making it.

## Seven modes of predication (*saptabhaṅgī*) — and where they come from
- [00:05:41] To the *syāt* qualifier the Jains add a list of **seven types of statement**, each prefaced by *syāt*. This is comparable to Nāgārjuna's tetralemma — except the Jains "go him three better," offering **seven** alternatives where he had only four.
- [00:05:54] Just as the tetralemma traces back to the **Buddha's refusal** to answer certain questions, the origins of this remarkable, unprecedented theory lie in reports about **Mahāvīra**. Confronted with "Is the world infinite?" and "Is the world finite?", Mahāvīra did not fall silent — he said **yes to both**, but a *conditional* yes, not an absolute yes.
- [00:06:18] He was even asked whether a question might have **no expressible answer at all**. Here the Buddha might finally have said yes — his silence was a rebuke to the question as posed. Mahāvīra likewise admits the right answer may be **inexpressible** — but even *that* admission is conditional, the expression of a certain standpoint.
- [00:06:43] The 11th-century Jain **Vādideva Sūri** explains the theory with particular clarity. The **sevenfold predicate theory** uses seven modes of assertion, each preceded by "conditionally" (*syāt*), all concerning a single object and its properties, composed of attributions and denials — either **simultaneously** or **successively** — and *without contradiction*:
  1. Conditionally, an object **exists** (an attribution).
  2. Conditionally, it **does not exist** (a denial).
  3. Conditionally it exists, conditionally it doesn't (**successive** attribution + denial).
  4. Conditionally, it is **non-assertable** (**simultaneous** attribution + denial).
  5. Conditionally it exists; conditionally it is non-assertable (attribution + the simultaneous pair).
  6. Conditionally it doesn't exist; conditionally it is non-assertable (denial + the simultaneous pair).
  7. Conditionally it exists, conditionally it doesn't, conditionally it is non-assertable (successive attribution + denial + the simultaneous pair).

## Getting our heads around the seven options
- [00:08:14] With the tetralemma we had to figure out how Nāgārjuna got away with **four** possibilities — yes, no, both, neither. The Jain scheme is harder still. One difference: for the Jains there are **three** basic answers, not just yes and no, but a third — the **non-assertable** or **inexpressible**.
- [00:08:42] Three simple modes get us started: (1) something *is* the case, (2) something is *not* the case, (3) something is *inexpressible*. Example: "there is a pot," "there is no pot," "it is inexpressible whether there is a pot." The theory extends beyond existence to any predicate — "the pot is blue," "the pot is not blue," "it is inexpressible whether the pot is blue."
- [00:09:13] Then the Jains **combine** these three into more complex options. "A pot exists" could be (4) both true and false, (5) true yet inexpressible, (6) false yet inexpressible, or (7) all three — true, false, and inexpressible. That is **seven** in all: three simple cases plus four compound ones.
- [00:09:37] And remember: *all seven* are prefaced by *syāt*, "conditionally." So even the "simple" option where something is said to be the case isn't simple. "Conditionally, there is a pot" / "conditionally, the universe is infinite" holds **just in case there is a standpoint** from which it is so — and that is compatible with the same thing being false from another point of view.

## Worked examples: standpoints that flip the verdict
- [00:09:59] We saw last time how this works. The universe has no **temporal** ending, so from that standpoint it is infinite — yet it is not infinite in **size**, so there is also a standpoint from which it is finite.
- [00:10:15] Similarly: from the standpoint that focuses on **stable, enduring substances**, "there is a pot"; yet from the perspective that focuses only on **momentary sensory experiences**, there is no pot — only ephemeral experiences of colour, shape, and hardness. (The same realist-vs-momentariness split that divides Vaiśeṣika from Buddhist metaphysics.)
- [00:10:32] A more banal version: if the pot is in **Munich** but not in **New York**, then from a Munich standpoint "there is a pot" is true, but not from a New York standpoint.

## The trickiest cases: "both true and false," and the inexpressible
- [00:10:47] Trouble starts with saying something is **both true and false** — dangerously close to a standpoint adopted by people happy to contradict themselves. Yet Vādideva Sūri prefaced the whole account by insisting the seven options involve **no self-contradiction**.
- [00:11:11] Why not? Because he says the mixed predicate "true and false" involves the **successive** attribution and denial of a property. So it is just the standpoint of someone who *first* notes a perspective from which a claim is true, and *then* adds another perspective from which it is false. Quite sensible — e.g. a Jain saying "our **Vaiśeṣika** colleagues believe the pot exists, but the Buddhists deny it." The seven-fold list explains how the theory of standpoints works; but it is the **theory of standpoints** that explains why the list involves no intentional inconsistency.
- [00:11:46] Not out of the woods yet: the most puzzling basic option is the **inexpressible**. Here Vādideva Sūri *does* seem to court contradiction, because he glosses "inexpressible" as the **simultaneous** attribution and denial of a property — a standpoint endorsing both a statement and its denial at once. So we seem back among those who contradict themselves. Yet the Jains promised no contradiction. What is going on?

## Two readings of the inexpressible
- [00:12:33] **Explanation one (the simple one):** we cannot say both yes *and* no to a question at the same time, because no one can assert two contradictory ideas at once. So the key Jain insight — that both answers are right, but from different points of view — is **inexpressible simply because of the limitations of normal language**. This fits Vādideva Sūri's tying the inexpressible to *simultaneous* attribution and denial.
- [00:13:03] **Explanation two:** comes from the 10th-century Jain **Prabhācandra** — met before as a source for **Cārvāka** thought, but a brilliant philosopher in his own right, born in **980**, the same year as **Avicenna**, and (incidentally) author of a thought experiment very like Avicenna's "**flying man**." More than any other Jain, Prabhācandra wrestles with what makes the seven alternatives genuinely **distinct** from one another while still **comprehensive** (no option left out).
- [00:13:42] When Prabhācandra explains "inexpressible," he does **not** say it involves simultaneous assertion and denial. He ties it instead to **neutrality**: from the standpoint one occupies, one can *neither endorse nor deny* a certain claim.
- [00:14:00] This could be very simple — neutrality from **lack of information**. In New York, not having been to Munich lately, I just shrug when asked whether there's a pot in Munich.
- [00:14:17] Or something more interesting. To assert from a standpoint is to adopt a perspective that **ratifies** some claims and **undermines** others. Take an example the Jains could never have contemplated — **modern physics**. From the physicist's standpoint, claims about **sub-atomic particles** are affirmed while claims about the ancient Greek **four elements** are denied. But about *some* propositions a standpoint is simply **neutral**: physics neither grounds nor undermines claims about **morality**. You can't work out whether stealing is wrong — or solve the moral dilemmas that perplex the protagonist of the **Mahābhārata** — by studying physics. Such propositions are *neither affirmed nor denied from inside that standpoint*. In **this** sense an answer may be inexpressible.

## Why this differs from the Buddha's silence
- [00:15:16] If Prabhācandra is right, the Jain "inexpressible" is **different** from the Buddhist refusal to answer. When the **Buddha** declined to respond, it was certainly **not** because he didn't know the answer.
- [00:15:26] A Buddhist would fiercely resist the idea that Buddhism is just *one more standpoint* that might lack the resources to answer a given question (the way physics lacks the resources for morality). The Buddhist standpoint claims to be "**the whole truth and nothing but the truth**." If it fails to answer a question, **the question is to blame** — it must be ill-formed or rest on false assumptions, like the reality of holes or of enduring selves.
- [00:15:51] This is why **Nāgārjuna's** strategy was to **expose the incoherence** in other views, whereas the **Jain** strategy is to expose their **dogmatic insistence** on being the *only* truth — when in fact there is more to truth than any one school's claim.
- [00:16:09] So the Jains hold there *are* ways to answer the questions the Buddha was asked, and point to scriptures where **Mahāvīra** did answer them. It is just that the right answer requires taking up a standpoint other than the Buddhist one — indeed several different **complementary** standpoints.

## Why coherence matters: philosophy in aid of liberation
- [00:16:26] Adamson has insisted on the **coherence** of Jain epistemology and on its commitment to attaining a *true* standpoint — not merely because it annoys him when people deny the principle of non-contradiction (it "both annoys us and doesn't").
- [00:16:43] It is also because **Jainism, like its rivals, is a philosophy in aid of a soteriology**. No less than other ancient Indian thinkers, the Jains hold out the prospect of **liberation**, and claim liberation is achieved through **knowledge**.
- [00:17:01] This is also why they are willing to say other schools have got things **wrong**: the partial perspective of a Buddhist or a Vedic thinker is **not liberating**, because it captures only a fragment of the **holistic, comprehensive** grasp of things that is nothing other than **omniscience**.
- [00:17:17] Adamson admits he hasn't *given* us that holistic grasp of Jainism — to do so would take many more episodes, "perhaps an infinity of them," which would take up too much storage on the feed.
- [00:17:37] So one more standpoint on Jain logic and epistemology is promised: next time's guest, **Marie-Hélène Gorisse (?)**, "certainly well qualified" to discuss the Jain theory of **conditional truth**.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **self-application test** — the rule that a theory must survive being applied to itself; the episode's organizing test, passed (the Jains argue) by both their standpoint theory and their seven-fold predication.
- **naya (standpoint)** — a perspective from which a claim is conceived; rival theories (realist vs. skeptic about universals, etc.) are each true from some *naya*.
- **syāt ("in a sense / conditionally")** — optative of "to be," used by the Jains as a qualifier (like a modal operator) so that assertions are made from a standpoint, never categorically.
- **anekāntavāda (non-one-sidedness)** — the Jain stance of impartiality that finds partial truth in opposed schools without (they claim) tolerating contradiction.
- **saptabhaṅgī (seven-fold predication)** — seven *syāt*-prefaced modes (exists / doesn't / both successively / non-assertable / and their combinations); the Jain answer to Nāgārjuna's four-cornered tetralemma.
- **the inexpressible / non-assertable** — the third basic answer beyond yes and no; read either as a limit of language (simultaneous attribution + denial, Vādideva Sūri) or as standpoint-neutrality (Prabhācandra).
- **Mahāvīra** — last Jain ford-maker; gave the conditional "yes" to opposed questions and embodies the omniscient, all-incorporating standpoint.
- **Siddhasena** — Jain philosopher quoted: a manifold thing is fully comprehended only by the omniscient; conceived from one standpoint it becomes the subject of a *naya*.
- **Vādideva Sūri** — 11th-c. Jain; quoted at length laying out the seven modes "without contradiction," glossing the inexpressible as simultaneous attribution and denial.
- **Prabhācandra** — 10th-c. Jain (born **980**, same year as Avicenna; has his own "flying man"-like thought experiment); reads the inexpressible as standpoint *neutrality*.
- **tetralemma (Nāgārjuna)** — the Buddhist four-cornered scheme (yes/no/both/neither) against which the seven-fold theory is contrasted; Nāgārjuna's strategy is to expose incoherence in rivals.
- **omniscience & soteriology** — Jain liberation is achieved through knowledge; only the comprehensive, all-standpoint (omniscient) grasp liberates, so partial Buddhist/Vedic perspectives fall short.

> **Speakers:** Peter Adamson with Jessica Frazier · **Runtime:** 00:34:41
> **In brief:** An interview on how the Hindu traditions do philosophy of religion: Brahman arises not as a worshipped person but as a *fundamental ontology* — the ground that lets being and non-being be at all — and the tradition's signature problem is not the problem of evil but how an unchanging divine can underlie a plural, changing world.

## The guest and the frame
- [00:00:17] Adamson introduces an interview "about Hinduism and philosophy" with **Jessica Frazier**, lecturer in religious studies at the **University of Kent** and a fellow at the **Oxford Centre for Hindu Studies**. The natural starting point: since Hinduism is a religion, what conception(s) of God or the divine show up in the classic texts?
- [00:01:01] Adamson's opening gambit: the obvious move is **Brahman** (found in the Upaniṣads) — isn't Brahman just "basically like the god of the Abrahamic religions," one all-powerful force pervading everything? Is that a good way in?

## Brahman as fundamental ontology, not a person
- [00:01:33] Frazier resists the parallel. Brahman is a "really unusual and distinctive" route to the divine because it **arises out of reasoning** — out of early philosophy around **500 BCE** trying to make sense of the world. Personal deities like Yahweh, and personalism generally, do appear in the earliest texts, but that's *not* where the distinctive concept of Brahman comes from. It comes from an attempt to form something like a **fundamental ontology**.
- [00:02:16] Her key example: the **Nāsadīya Sūkta**, a creation hymn in the **Ṛg Veda** — a very old text. Where you'd expect it to call on a god for help, it instead says that at the beginning "there was being and there was non-being" (notice it names *both*) — and then asks: what *framed* that? Where was it located? What gave the place for both being and non-being?
- [00:02:53] The hymn even says **the gods must know — but the gods can't know**, because they are merely beings; they cannot know what the framework for everything is. Frazier calls this a crucial philosophical moment: the search is not for a person who made stuff, not for any contingent particular being, but for "what is the frame for anything to exist or not exist at all."
- [00:03:16] Adamson sharpens it: a **metaphysical grounding of everything, even the things that don't exist**. Frazier agrees — what is the very *principle of the possibility of being*? She adds a polemical aside: the 20th-century complaint that "the West forgets the question of being" doesn't apply to India, which kept hold of ontology precisely because it was central to its concept of God.
- [00:03:53] This generates rich accounts of the divine *and* of reality. The Upaniṣads give perhaps the first philosophical treatment of divine attributes: **eternity / eternal infinity**, **simplicity** (God is undivided), **sovereignty or soleness** — *advaita*, "without a second" — and **self-existence**, *svayambhū*, self-existence or ipseity in the classical vocabulary.

## Turning the ontological argument on its head — and the puzzle of worship
- [00:04:28] Adamson presses a tension: the "ground of all being and non-being" sounds transcendent, but it's not clear it's something you'd *worship*. Yet Frazier just said it has the traits we associate with the Abrahamic God. Does that make it an object of religious worship as well as a ground of ontology, or is there pressure between the two?
- [00:05:14] Frazier's answer: the Indian approach **generates the concept of the divine out of what is most fundamental to reality** — "turning the ontological argument on its head." Rather than starting with a God and asking what makes him godly, it asks: what are the godly attributes — self-existent, eternal, infinite, immutable? *Whatever has those, that is what we'll call God.*
- [00:05:54] The twist: Hindus have not, historically, tended to **worship Brahman**. You don't offer it things; you don't talk to it — "you can't talk to being in that way." So the West's idea that *worship* is central to religion may need revising. For something more like "the **God of the philosophers**," the fountain of religious thought and experience is not worship but **awe or reflection**.

## Brahman and the many gods — the cosmological move
- [00:06:26] Adamson raises the obvious counter: most listeners picture Hinduism as all those images and statues of gods. How does the *ground of all things* relate to that **polytheistic** framework?
- [00:07:00] India has **both models**: personal beings descending from early Vedic deity-worship (animism is rife in early India — spirits and deities everywhere) *and* the philosophical concept of an ultimate ground. They ultimately **combine** — just as "Aristotle and the Bible finally get together" to generate scholastic thought.
- [00:07:31] So India does produce **proofs of the existence of God** (here a personal creator), but that's not the focus of the **Vedāntic** tradition. When a text praises **Kṛṣṇa**, **Śiva**, or **Durgā**, it typically jumps in to say *this is the deity who is the source of all*. The personal beings get aligned with the **cosmological argument**: look beyond the anthropomorphic face and you see the **material and efficient cause** of the universe — that from which everything comes.
- [00:08:30] For any religious tradition to become a philosophical culture, "the divine idea has to become philosophized." In India the gods come to signify not only the source but the very **material** of the universe — the divine is literally a kind of *stuff* out of which everything is made, with very concrete passages on this in the Vedic literature.

## Pantheism, and the stuff of the world: clay vs. consciousness
- [00:08:55] Adamson: doesn't that make everything — him, the microphone, Frazier, the listener, the device — literally *made of God*? That sounds like **pantheism**: one divine entity pervading everything. Is that the right reading, or is it more metaphorical?
- [00:09:28] Frazier: the West's idea of pantheism is "quite a crude and undeveloped idea." Going back to **Raphson**, who coined the word, *pantheism* (everything is God) was set against **panhylism** (everything is mere unconscious substance). The Indian tradition lives in the space *between* — asking whether there is something divine, something extraordinary, about the eternal **stratum** that is the basis of everything.
- [00:10:14] Crucially, India doesn't stop at "it's all made of God" — it gives the idea **incredible nuance**. The **Chāndogya Upaniṣad** says the divine material is like **clay**. But the **Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad** says the divine is the **consciousness** of which all perceptions are made — "an ocean of consciousness" taking different forms. And the **Kena Upaniṣad** arguably presents a **process ontology**: God is "that by which events happen," the constant impeller of phenomena.
- [00:10:59] The payoff: the **substratum of existence** — the *hyle*, the *theos*, the thing it's made of — is **not simply matter, not simply stuff, not substantialism**. The West, Frazier argues, "got stuck on the idea of substance," which doesn't answer the subtler questions about the fabric of being.
- [00:11:31] Adamson: if the stuff of everything is **consciousness**, then you're describing the universe as a **single self-aware mind** grasping itself — which changes what "pantheism" means. But how does that square with the *clay* idea? Is clay just a metaphor for consciousness?
- [00:12:11] Frazier: there's a whole tradition asking *what kinds of thing can be the material of something*. The Chāndogya offers a "raw materialism" — clay is insensate, unconscious stuff that takes on different **names and forms** (*nāma-rūpa*); you shape matter into the bookshelf, the book, you, me. But the Upaniṣads also take a **phenomenological turn**, perhaps inspired by the early **yoga** tradition of people examining their own thought: *is* the material of the world really matter? Isn't it all perception, concept, experience? From that the answer flips — it's **consciousness** we're all made of — and a whole separate tradition of thinking about "formedness" follows.

## The Upaniṣads as a plurality — questions, not treatises
- [00:13:34] Adamson notes this illustrates a recurring point: even *within* the Upaniṣads, and certainly within Hinduism, you find a **variety of views and stages of thought**.
- [00:13:48] Frazier's image: the Upaniṣads are "as if you got together all the **pre-Socratic philosophers**, took their writing, and turned it into one scripture" — mixing myth, cosmology, early science, even mathematics, and channeling it into *multiple* philosophical suggestions (written by different families of priests and intelligentsia), laid out for you to explore and combine.
- [00:14:21] And the Upaniṣads — like most later Indian texts — proceed **less by treatise than by question**: not telling you what's the case but asking what *might* be, then opening the field to a range of answers. A "proliferating, open tradition that welcomes multiple possible solutions."

## Proofs of God: the design and cosmological arguments
- [00:14:47] Adamson: we do nonetheless find **positive arguments** in the Hindu tradition. To what extent do they map onto modern philosophy of religion — proofs of God's existence, the nature of God, the afterlife, miracles? Do real *proofs of God's existence* appear in the texts?
- [00:15:23] A few schools do try to prove the divine, often a *personal* god: **Nyāya** (the school of logic) and **Vaiśeṣika** (the atomists), "going hand in hand" — e.g. the **Nyāyamañjarī of Jayanta**. Their argument resembles **Descartes**: look at everything the world is made of — what causes it to function together as a *unity*? Why is there coherence in the world? There must be a **unity-maker, a designer**. Just as Descartes says God makes mind and world align, they argue the divine gets atoms, concepts, universals, even the metaphysical categories to generate a coherent world together.
- [00:16:30] But the **cosmological argument** is the more common case. Throughout the Upaniṣads and the later Vedāntic tradition, thinkers barely *have* to argue there's a god — they simply say **that which is the source is the thing we're talking about**.
- [00:16:53] A deep contrast: the West is preoccupied with **creedal assent** (assent to a religious belief or a philosophical proposition). By saying "the thing that exists is the thing we mean," the Upaniṣadic tradition **bypasses** that problem altogether.

## The real Indian question: immutability vs. change
- [00:17:12] Frazier thinks a *different* set of questions interests India most. Where the West worries about **omnipotence vs. omnibenevolence** (the problem of evil), India is gripped by **immutability vs. plurality**: God is one unchanging identity, yet the world shows change and multiplicity — how can both hold? This is the debate that generates a whole spread of answers.
- [00:17:42] The menu of solutions:
  - Schools holding God changes only in **non-essential attributes** — an **aupādhika** change; only the contingent elements change.
  - Schools holding God **does intrinsically change**, but the underlying material stays the same.
  - Most interesting, schools seeking forms of **plural identity** — kinds of oneness that are *intrinsically plural*, so there's no loss of integrity. **Bhartṛhari** points to **meaning**: one meaning can be made of complex, subtle internal elements (a dialectical account). **Rāmānuja**, a medieval philosopher, points to **agency**: to act is to actualize multiple possibilities, which *is* to change — yet because that just *is* what agency is, in changing you remain an agent. Identity that incorporates an internal transformational element.
- [00:18:49] The shared aim: find something that allows **plurality within unity**.

## God as the ground of consciousness = the self
- [00:18:57] Adamson connects two threads: God as the **ground of consciousness** sounds exactly like the debates about the **self** — is the self one unchanging thing, a plurality, or even an illusion? Maybe it's the *same* debate in two vocabularies: if God is the ground of consciousness, then God may be the self — and indeed there's the idea that **Brahman is the self** (*ātman*).
- [00:19:48] Frazier: there's a real correlation, with a subtle difference. You could drop the word "God" — it's such a Western, person-presupposing idea — whereas **Brahman literally means the foundation, that which bears up**, and must be the foundation of *everything*. In both cases you must "account for a continuity that underlies change and a coherence that underlies plurality."

## The Buddhist straw man
- [00:20:34] In both debates (self and divine), "the silent voice, the straw man, is always the **Buddhists**." Present throughout India, they make the **ultimate critique**: there is nothing substantial, foundational, or permanent — **no essence**. If you define the divine (or what has intrinsic value) as *that which is permanent and essential*, the Buddhists have denied there's anything of intrinsic value at all. So Buddhists — constantly reminding everyone that change appears to destroy identity — are the opponents Hindus must answer about *both* the self and the divine. They want to go far enough to explain plurality, "but not so far that it becomes *just* plurality."
- [00:21:22] Frazier admits she once agonized — "are the Buddhists right or the Hindus right?" — but thinks the Hindus land a good point. The Buddhists say you must specify what stuff is made of, and answer that everything is made of **dharmas**: tiny points of existence with **no duration and no spatial extension**. She finds this incoherent — an extensionless, durationless point with no intrinsic existence doesn't explain what these existence-points are *themselves* made of.
- [00:22:16] So she returns to the **Nāsadīya Sūkta**: we still have to account for *that which allows both existence and non-existence to be* — and, extending it, that which allows change, phenomena, even insubstantial phenomena in consciousness, even illusions and misperceptions, to have an ontological basis. India keeps reminding us to go beyond **substantialism** toward a bigger **fundamental ontology**: "the being which grounds beings," ontology rather than ontic concepts.

## Does scripture fence in philosophy?
- [00:23:06] Adamson plays devil's advocate: this is a *religious* tradition built on scripture. Doesn't that limit the arguments and positions available, since you can't contradict the Vedic texts — a "fence around the possibility of philosophy"?
- [00:23:59] Frazier turns it around: that's a standard "we haven't been holding *Western* philosophy to." Most Western philosophy from the rise of Christianity into the 20th century was religious in some sense — **Kant, Descartes, Hegel**. And the Indian scriptures are *already quite philosophical* in character: "it's like taking **Plato** as your Bible," whereas the actual Bible is light on metaphysics. So Hindu thinkers work with material that is already reasoned.
- [00:24:53] More than that, **text is an enabler, not a restriction**. India commits early to the Vedic **Śruti** and then *expands the canon* again and again — to epics like the **Bhagavad-Gītā**, to philosophical texts like the **Brahma-Sūtra**, to diverse later texts like the **Purāṇas** — so the range of what counts as scripture keeps growing, facilitating creativity.
- [00:25:34] And **interpretation** is central to their reasoning. You don't, like Descartes, sit alone deriving first postulates; you work with suggestions already there. One of the **pramāṇas** (sources of good reason) is **anumāna**, inference — a well-developed account of using analogies and examples to build new models. Philosophizing **through commentary** means you constantly *develop* and even *change* the ideas you draw out. There's "plenty written on how someone uses a text that says one thing and cleverly makes it say the opposite" — so the text ends up serving reason.
- [00:26:23] Adamson, who's also covering medieval European philosophy, agrees: **scholastic philosophy** offers plenty of examples of taking a text that seems to say one thing and making it say the other.

## Ritual vs. philosophy — a false dichotomy
- [00:26:34] Adamson raises **ritual** as distinctive of the Vedāntic world — ritual act, ritual speech, manipulated objects. Doesn't ritual give an **alternative route** to your goal, maybe making philosophy unnecessary — *two paths to God* rather than one?
- [00:27:13] Frazier: in some traditions it works that way, but the two aren't necessarily aiming at the same goal. **Ritual is method** — the *means* to certain ends. In Hindu culture rituals do many things: transubstantiational rituals, interactive rituals contacting spirits or deities, and many that **celebrate natural law** (Vedic rituals meant to "concretize" the laws of nature and society that already exist). They're modes, means, tools.
- [00:27:57] But **reason never loses its place** as the thing by which you *explain* the world — "ritual can't explain anything." The Upaniṣads repeatedly show the **son coming to the father**, the king to the Brahmin, the priest to the king, even a god to another god: *explain it to me, be my teacher.* Whatever your place in the hierarchy, you want to know the **meaning** and, crucially, the **purpose**. Ritual is the method but doesn't tell you the goal of the method.
- [00:28:43] So it's a **false dichotomy** to set ritual ("lighting fires and burning stuff") against philosophy ("debates at court"): philosophy is partly there to help you understand *what the ritual is for*, and the ritual is there to *carry out the philosophical insight*.

## Seers, and reason's independent life
- [00:29:02] Adamson agrees, and Frazier deepens it: India's original intelligentsia were the **Brahmins**, the priests — not a sideline but their very nature. The authors of the scriptures, the **ṛṣis**, are **seers**: not prophets reporting what God said, but seers who *see the nature of reality*.
- [00:29:33] So the dichotomy "religion gets you magical salvation, reason is some extraneous activity" is wrong. Frazier notes that **Jonardon Ganeri** has reminded us reason often functioned *independently* of religion and could even **challenge** it — India's sciences were rich and widely respected. Reason and philosophy have their own life, "but it was never necessarily in contrast to religion." And the project of religion isn't just "some juicy reward": it is **metaphysical** — understanding, not merely a happy end.

## The caste critique — and Sulabhā's lesson
- [00:30:11] Adamson presses a social critique: the **Brahmins** make unique claims to knowledge *and* power — the ability to get the gods to deliver health, wealth, political success. Isn't the whole system of ritual-and-philosophy built to explain why they should be a caste above the others, with even kings prostrating at their feet?
- [00:30:59] Frazier grants much of it. Brahmins are an intelligentsia, and intelligentsias are often class-based; the hard part in India is that it's **explicitly endogamous, by birth** — what was an informal class division in Europe becomes a **formal caste division**. So the *de facto* truth of the West (the philosophical elite is always an elite) was *de jure* true in India.
- [00:31:38] But she argues that can only last so long. Her example: the **Mahābhārata**, a huge epic that gives voice to marginal characters — **women, lower-caste figures** — who suddenly **speak out in philosophical language**, calling everyone to witness the quality of their reasoning and defending themselves against higher castes.
- [00:32:13] The story of **Sulabhā**: an independent woman yogi, a renouncer/ascetic. A king thinks it unreasonable that a woman should wander alone seeking knowledge. Having both philosophical *and* supernatural powers, she **possesses him — enters his mind** — and from within delivers a lecture whose key point is: *look how reasonable I am, look at my well-grounded premises and the systematic formulation of my arguments; my rationality grounds me as your equal.* Having shamed him and shown herself his equal "at the rational level," she finds his mind "terribly empty," declines to stay, and leaves.
- [00:33:35] The moral Frazier draws: reason **overcomes inequality**. What reason does when it persuades is enter your mind and show you, *from within*, that something is true — so you are *changed*, made into someone who must respect the mind of the other. What began as something elite (philosophical practice) becomes the underpinning by which people throughout Indian history **assert equality in the face of inequality**.
- [00:34:04] Adamson: "a beautiful example" of how philosophy and reason create a **level playing field** across religions, classes, and genders.

## Closing
- [00:34:20] Adamson thanks Jessica Frazier, and previews the next big section of the series — the **Age of the Sūtras** — to be taken up again with Jonardon Ganeri.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Brahman** — the foundation, "that which bears up"; reached through reasoning as the *ground* of being (and non-being), not a worshipped person; close to the self (*ātman*). Classical attributes: eternity, infinity, simplicity, *advaita* ("without a second"), *svayambhū* (self-existence).
- **Nāsadīya Sūkta** — creation hymn in the Ṛg Veda; asks what *frames* both being and non-being — the gods can't know, being mere beings; Frazier's paradigm of fundamental ontology.
- **Upaniṣads** — first philosophical treatments of the divine and reality; "like the pre-Socratics turned into one scripture"; proceed by *question*, not treatise. The **Chāndogya** makes the divine material **clay** (shaped into *nāma-rūpa*, name-and-form); the **Māṇḍūkya** makes it **consciousness** ("an ocean of consciousness"); the **Kena** gives a **process ontology** (God as "that by which events happen").
- **Nyāya & Vaiśeṣika** — the logic school and the atomist school; offer a Descartes-like **design argument** for a personal god as the "unity-maker" of a coherent world (e.g. Jayanta's **Nyāyamañjarī**).
- **cosmological argument** — the more common Indian proof: "that which is the source is the thing we're talking about," bypassing creedal assent.
- **aupādhika** — change in non-essential / contingent attributes only; one proposed solution to immutability-vs-change.
- **Bhartṛhari** — locates plural-in-one identity in **meaning** (one meaning made of subtle internal elements); a dialectical account.
- **Rāmānuja** — medieval philosopher; locates plural-in-one identity in **agency** (to act is to change, yet remain an agent).
- **dharmas (Buddhist)** — tiny points of existence with no duration or spatial extension; the Buddhist anti-substantialist answer Frazier finds incoherent — the straw man behind both the self and the divine debates.
- **Śruti** — the Vedic canon Hinduism commits to, then expands (to the Bhagavad-Gītā, Brahma-Sūtra, Purāṇas) — text as enabler, not fence.
- **pramāṇa / anumāna** — sources of valid knowledge / inference; the well-developed Indian art of reasoning by analogy and example, often via commentary.
- **ṛṣis** — the "seers" who authored the scriptures; not prophets reporting God's words but seers of the nature of reality.
- **Mahābhārata / Sulabhā** — epic giving philosophical voice to marginal figures; Sulabhā is the woman yogi who possesses a dismissive king and proves, from within his mind, her rational equality — Frazier's emblem of reason overcoming inequality.

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Dharmakīrti** — Dignāga's brilliant disciple, whose work became the canon later philosophy argued with for centuries, even as Dignāga's own texts faded [00:06:05].
- **Śrīharṣa** — **12th-century** skeptic whose *Khaṇḍana-khaṇḍa-khādya* ("morsels of refutation") demolished definition-based philosophy [00:07:25].
- **Gaṅgeśa** — **14th-century** author of a single book, who founded **Navya-Nyāya** (the New Nyāya), the second great revolution after Dignāga [00:09:31].
- **Raghunātha Śiromaṇi** — **16th-century** figure of Navadvīpa in Bengal who reignited a self-conscious, exploratory phase of philosophy [00:10:51].
- **The colonial-era thinkers** — Gandhi, Nehru, Ambedkar, Tagore, who made political and social philosophy central [00:14:18].

## Recap: what the series covered — from before the pre-Socratics to Dignāga
- [00:00:31] The series tried to follow the "Without Any Gaps" approach, but India's tradition is so vast that many "barely known figures and movements" had to be skipped, and even those included could not be done full justice. It began with texts older than the pre-Socratics of ancient Greece and worked forward as far as **Dignāga** and his contemporaries, around **600 AD**.
- [00:01:00] In the earliest phase, philosophy appeared above all as a **reflection on how one lives** — a path to a desirable final state, whether **mokṣa** (ultimate freedom) or **nirvāṇa** (release from all human suffering). These philosophies of path and purpose included the ancient ritual wisdom of the **Vedas**, brought (it seems) by Aryan settlers, and the **Upaniṣads**, poetic visions of the unity of humanity, ritual and cosmos. Under this heading came the original teachings of the **Buddha** and **Mahāvīra**, and the vast Hindu epic the **Mahābhārata** — which contains within it the **Bhagavad-Gītā** — as another thread woven into "the rope of thought through time" that was early Indian philosophy.
- [00:01:49] Keeping the weaving metaphor, the series then turned to the **Age of the Sūtra** (*sūtra* literally means "thread"). These texts were so compressed and aphoristic that they were "extremely hard to figure out" without an interpretive gloss — so the series also met the **great commentators**, who really brought *systematicity* into Indian debate and drove the evolution of the schools: **Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, Cārvāka, and Vedānta**.
- [00:02:22] Buddhist and Jain philosophers were not left out. Their switch to **Sanskrit** fundamentally transformed Indian philosophy until the end of the first millennium. Among the keenest Buddhists was **Nāgārjuna**, whose assault on the metaphysical notion of **svabhāva** (self-nature) inspired the **Madhyamaka**, the "Middle Way" philosophy. Another was **Vasubandhu**, who both standardized **Abhidharma** in Sanskrit and laid the foundations for **Yogācāra**. The series also looked at the early Jains, including **Umāsvāti**, and their *perspectival* approach to epistemology.

## Dignāga: a genius, and a new way of writing philosophy
- [00:03:08] After "meandering through all this early history," the series finally reached **Dignāga** — a "philosophical genius of the highest order," a figure who would make it onto even the most conservative "top of the pops" list of greatest philosophers. His work was decisive in shaping the next period: a cosmopolitan **Age of Dialogue in Sanskrit** that lasted until the collapse of Buddhism in India — a collapse "usually, but not entirely uncontentiously, blamed on invasion from the Islamic world."
- [00:03:39] Adamson and Ganeri are not alone in seeing Dignāga as inaugurating an entirely new epoch, though scholars differ on *why* he was so influential. The podcast emphasized his **theoretical innovations** — new ways of defining terms, new formalizations of sound argumentation — but others stress that he invented a new *way of writing* philosophy, a "transformation of discursive practice."
- [00:04:13] **Lawrence McCrea** is quoted: Dignāga "initiated a sudden, widespread, and radical transformation in the reading, citational, and discursive practices of Sanskrit philosophers" — perhaps even more dramatic in effect than his specifically philosophical contributions. He made the **systematic investigation of, and response to, the texts of rival traditions** a basic organizing principle of his own work. These new ways of arguing and writing were immediately taken up and "rapidly became the hallmark of philosophical activity in a broad **Sanskrit cosmopolis**" that endured for centuries and spread well beyond the subcontinent.

## End of the survey — and a promise of the briefest of hints
- [00:05:00] That brings the series to its end. It occasionally glimpsed further ahead (e.g. **Abhinavagupta**, **11th century**), later figures came up via interview guests, and special themes got attention — non-violence, the role of women, animals, karma, time, even tantra. In effect, the series covered "the first millennium and a half of Indian philosophy" — "not too bad in 60 episodes."
- [00:05:26] But things "really picked up speed in the next millennium and a half, between Dignāga and the present day." A huge amount of great philosophy happened in that time; perhaps a future series will treat it properly. For now, Adamson offers "the briefest of hints" at the history of philosophy in India from Dignāga onward.

## Dharmakīrti eclipses his own master
- [00:05:52] It is an irony that, despite his enormous impact, Dignāga's Sanskrit works were lost for a long time — because he was "blessed, or possibly cursed," with an extremely brilliant disciple, **Dharmakīrti**. It was Dharmakīrti's work that provided the **canon** for later authors: widely disseminated, commented on, and attacked, even as Dignāga's own writings faded into the background.
- [00:06:21] For at least three centuries, most serious Indian philosophy took Dharmakīrti as its principal point of reference. As all the schools the series reviewed developed, they took Dharmakīrti **as their foil** — and in doing so achieved great progress, articulating their core beliefs and supplying arguments "of a higher caliber than had ever been necessary before."

## Kashmir, and the persistence of skepticism
- [00:06:50] Many important figures came from **Kashmir**, including Śaiva thinkers like **Utpaladeva** and the aforementioned **Abhinavagupta**, as well as the **Nyāya** genius **Jayanta Bhaṭṭa**.
- [00:07:01] Skepticism, which had played a major role in Indian thought from an early period (and may even have helped trigger ancient Greek skepticism), continued in later times. In the **9th century**, **Jayarāśi** described himself as "a lion who had come to upturn every philosophical cart."
- [00:07:19] Matters came to a head with **Śrīharṣa**'s revolutionary critique of the fundamentals of **pramāṇa** epistemology. His **12th-century** classic — "amassed morsels of refutation" (the *Khaṇḍana-khaṇḍa-khādya*) — is a brilliant *destructive* analysis of the "definition-mongering" activity of past thinkers. Śrīharṣa argued that a philosophical method based on the **search for definitions is misguided, indeed incoherent**, and developed a rival **method of refutation** to expose as vacuous the very way of doing philosophy that Dignāga had made so influential. Remarkably, his method required him first to **reconstruct the best possible version of any definition** — not merely the best anyone had actually formulated — articulating opponents' positions "with greater insight, accuracy, and acuity than their own proponents."

## Two more lines of critique: Mīmāṃsā on inquiry, Advaita on common sense
- [00:08:21] Other critiques gathered momentum. One came from new developments in **Mīmāṃsā** theory about the nature of *inquiry*, still within its traditional framework of defending the legitimacy and authority of **Vedic** knowledge. To suppose the Vedas authoritative is to accept their teachings as unquestionable: if the Veda teaches something, there is "no need to engage in verification." This pushed Brahmanical thinkers to reflect on methodology and the nature of knowledge, contesting the notion that *truth must be the outcome of discovery and confirmation*.
- [00:08:59] Another critique was a growing challenge to the **metaphysics of common sense**: increasingly sophisticated **Advaita** philosophers sought to undermine the principle that appearance is trustworthy — in particular, that there is a world of everyday objects known to a *plurality of distinct cognizers*.

## Gaṅgeśa and the second great revolution: Navya-Nyāya
- [00:09:14] In the **14th century**, in response to all these pressures, a philosopher emerged who transformed the landscape as brilliantly and decisively as Dignāga had in the 6th — "a second great revolution." His name was **Gaṅgeśa**.
- [00:09:33] He wrote only **one book**, one that "styles itself as the jewel that fulfils the wish for truth" (the *Tattvacintāmaṇi*). His new conceptual methodologies — his answer to Śrīharṣa's general skepticism and to the specific challenges from Mīmāṃsā and Advaita — rapidly gained currency across the Sanskrit world.
- [00:09:51] His new method was called **Navya-Nyāya**, the "New Nyāya." It spread to all intellectual disciplines and proved especially influential in **law and jurisprudence**. Its heart was a "meticulous technique for the disambiguation of terms and assertions," so that an opponent's thesis could be carefully separated into a variety of possible readings, each then individually refuted.

## The early-modern flowering: Raghunātha, Navadvīpa, and Varanasi
- [00:10:21] The **16th and 17th centuries** were "an extraordinary period for philosophy in India" — partly, but only partly, the result of new encounters with Islamic culture. Exposure to new paradigms led Sanskrit philosophers to innovate *self-consciously*: "to think with the old structures, but not defer to them." An "astonishingly vast" number of Sanskrit works survive from this time.
- [00:10:51] In the writings of those who followed **Raghunātha Śiromaṇi** — from about the middle of the **16th century** until the middle of the **18th** — there is a metamorphosis in epistemology, metaphysics, semantics, and philosophical logic. Many of these philosophers lived in Raghunātha's hometown of **Navadvīpa** in Bengal, and their works are full of phrases of "a newly open and exploratory attitude" — "this should be considered further," "this needs to be reflected on." What drove the work was not fresh *exegesis* of ancient texts but **inquiry into the problems themselves**, with a sense of an ongoing project.
- [00:11:25] A second group, based in **Varanasi** and again profoundly influenced by Raghunātha, used his work to **reinterpret ancient metaphysics**, sometimes with the support of the Islamic rulers of **Mughal** India.
- [00:11:43] At the same time — in *opposition* to Raghunātha's "new reasoners," while co-opting his methods — thinkers like **Madhusūdana Sarasvatī**, **Appaya Dīkṣita**, and **Nīlakaṇṭha Caturdhara** brought a distinctive renewal to **Vedānta**, amid "complex negotiations with Mughal patronage and power, and with their own pasts."

## Islamic philosophy in India, in parallel
- [00:12:08] Alongside these Sanskrit developments, Muslim philosophers were producing important and innovative work in parallel centers of Islamic learning. Three Islamic trends emerged in the **17th and 18th centuries**.
- [00:12:24] First, the **bilingual project** of **Dārā Shikoh** and others, who translated philosophy from **Sanskrit into Persian**. Second, the **Sufi** philosophy of **Muḥibbullāh Ilāhābādī**, a prolific author in Persian and Arabic and a defender of the Andalusian mystic **Ibn ʿArabī**. Third, a debate between **Avicennan** thinkers — such as the influential **Maḥmūd Jaunpūrī** — and **Illuminationists**.
- [00:12:51] Meanwhile, Muḥibb Allāh al-Bihārī's **Sullam al-ʿUlūm** is "a milestone 17th-century Indian textbook in Arab-Islamic logic." Some of this was discussed in the series on philosophy in the Islamic world (Episode 189). But, Adamson concedes, "we still have only the most rudimentary understanding" of the intersections between Sanskrit, Persian, and Arabic scholarship in early-modern India, and little insight into philosophy in Indian **vernacular** languages of the period.

## Colonial occupation and the turn to political philosophy
- [00:13:27] The series next "moved to the era of British colonial occupation" — which, Adamson stresses, "was not quite the total disaster for indigenous philosophy" that it was in many other colonized parts of the world. One effect it did have was to **foreground new philosophical priorities**, especially the need to respond to an incompatibility at the heart of the colonial project: European claims about liberty, tolerance, equality, and secularism on one hand, and the "manifest illiberalities, intolerances, and inequalities of colonial rule" on the other.
- [00:14:00] That rule began with the **East India Company**'s exploitative governance from the **Battle of Plassey (1757)** until the failed war of independence in **1857**, then continued under direct British crown rule until **independence in 1947**.
- [00:14:18] Brilliant thinkers like **Gandhi, Nehru, Ambedkar, and Tagore** — now often writing in **English** — made **political and social philosophy** the center of Indian philosophical activity, where earlier it had been mind, language, epistemology, and metaphysics. Others reflected deeply on the **subject** — its freedom, agency, and identity — in an effort to formulate the grounds of an **intellectual decolonization**. In the struggle for freedom, "the whole of India's philosophical past became an immense resource," its perceived spirit of **negotiated pluralism and non-coercive cosmopolitanism** made central to the design of a post-independence nation.

## From the 1950s to the present
- [00:15:05] Philosophy has continued to progress from the **1950s** to today — though sometimes struggling to find a voice inside "Victorian-era universities" and understandably taking a backseat to the urgent task of rebuilding an economy ravaged by extractive colonial governance. There are positive signs that, as India enters a period of economic prosperity, philosophy will reassume "its traditional place at the very heart of Indian society and culture."
- [00:15:35] Thanks both to new perspectives from the **South Asian diaspora** and to a new generation of Western philosophers with a more global and cosmopolitan view, the study of Indian philosophy in universities worldwide is enjoying "a period of unprecedented growth." Adamson hopes the podcast will help push insights from Indian thought into philosophy as taught and researched outside India.

## Where Indian thought looks most promising today
- [00:16:13] Adamson closes by naming areas where the Indian contribution seems "distinctive and promising" from a contemporary point of view.
- **Ethics — non-violence** [00:16:24]: returning to **Amber Carpenter**'s interview on the Indian ethic of non-violence toward animals. The Indian rationale was *very different* from today's. **Peter Singer** uses **utilitarian** principles (animals can suffer and get something positive from life); other ethicists take a **rights**-based approach (animals have as much claim to liberty and welfare as we do, potentially justifying total animal liberation). By contrast, ancient Indian **ahiṃsā** was "a holistic way of life," with avoidance of animal suffering only one of its many aspects. To live by ahiṃsā is to take a certain attitude toward *all* living things, including plants, toward oneself, and even toward inanimate objects — "kicking a stone in anger could be a violation," though a stone has no rights and cannot suffer. As the Mahābhārata puts it, "ahiṃsā is the **dharma** … the highest truth from which all dharma proceeds." It would be interesting to see a modern philosopher *revive* this — putting avoidance of violence at the center of ethics rather than extracting non-violence from some other notion like utility or duty. The recent revival of **Aristotelian** virtue ethics, with its similar holism, gives "some cause for optimism."
- **Metaphysics — Nāgārjuna** [00:18:27]: Sāṃkhya's category theory or Vedānta monism seem unlikely to appeal to the average philosopher today, but **Nāgārjuna** might be a more plausible source of inspiration. As heard in the **Graham Priest** interview, Nāgārjuna's reasoning can be compared to **non-classical logic**, and his ideas about **emptiness** and **dependent origination** are worth revisiting. If "to say things are empty is to claim they have no fixed intrinsic essence" — that the being of things is "purely relational" — this could excite a modern metaphysician: rather than a world of discrete objects bearing relations, **what if relations were the fundamental building blocks?** Some philosophers already admit relations are *ineliminable*, and some think a relational view fits modern physics better than one based on intrinsic natures. Nāgārjuna "could be a formidable ally."
- **Epistemology & philosophy of language** [00:20:08]: a long-standing concern of the ancient Indians, with both constructive work (the theory of **pramāṇa**s) and rich skeptical traditions. **Śrīharṣa**'s critique is attracting contemporary epistemologists, as is Sanskrit work in **philosophy of language**, especially its highly developed accounts of **metaphor** and literary significance.
- **Philosophy of mind** [00:20:39]: perhaps the area with most cross-cultural activity. Classical Indian theories of mind have fed into **cognitive science** and new understandings of consciousness, attention, and self-representation. The Oxford philosopher **Michael Dummett** thought philosophy of mind was the area that would "profit most from philosophers working in divergent traditions coming together" — and there does seem to be truth in that.

## Conclusion, and what comes next
- [00:21:12] The series ends "on an emphatic note": ancient Indian thought "has everything a historian of philosophy could want" — rich with interpretive challenges, full of subtle texts that respond to one another and are intriguing in literary form. It was historically influential (somewhat on European ideas, but "above all in cultures across Asia"), it addressed core questions in *every* department of philosophy, and it advanced novel ideas — some ripe for contemporary uptake. No philosopher is *obliged* to study Indian intellectual history (there is too much other great philosophy elsewhere for that), but "any philosopher could benefit greatly from doing so."
- [00:22:18] So the podcast will keep exploring non-European traditions. These will eventually include **China** and other East Asian cultures (a chance to learn about the influence of Indian thought) — but the *next stop is Africa*. In a further series of comparable length, **Chike Jeffers** will join Adamson to explore philosophy in **Africana culture**, from ancient Egypt down to the diaspora of the late 20th century, appearing on the same feed.
- [00:23:06] Before that, there will be "one last look at Indian culture" — a finale with "a very special secret interview guest." Since this was the last *scripted* episode, Adamson finishes by thanking **Jonardon Ganeri** for lending his expertise and helping tell "the history of philosophy in India."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Dignāga** — c. **600 AD** genius whose theoretical innovations *and* new "discursive practice" (systematic engagement with rivals' texts, per Lawrence McCrea) inaugurated the Sanskrit "Age of Dialogue."
- **Dharmakīrti** — Dignāga's disciple whose work became the canon; for three centuries the chief foil against which every school sharpened its arguments, eclipsing Dignāga's own (lost) texts.
- **Jayarāśi** — **9th-century** skeptic, "a lion come to upturn every philosophical cart."
- **Śrīharṣa** — **12th-century** skeptic; his "morsels of refutation" (*Khaṇḍana-khaṇḍa-khādya*) argued that definition-based philosophy is incoherent, replacing it with a method of refutation.
- **pramāṇa** — means/instrument of knowledge; the epistemology Śrīharṣa attacked and the Nyāya tradition defended.
- **Gaṅgeśa** — **14th-century** author of one book ("the jewel that fulfils the wish for truth," the *Tattvacintāmaṇi*); founder of the second great revolution.
- **Navya-Nyāya** — Gaṅgeśa's "New Nyāya"; a meticulous technique for disambiguating terms so a thesis can be split into readings and each refuted; spread to all disciplines, especially law.
- **Mīmāṃsā** — school defending the unquestionable authority of Vedic knowledge; its later theory of *inquiry* contested that truth must come from discovery and confirmation.
- **Advaita** — non-dualist Vedānta; its philosophers challenged common-sense metaphysics and the trustworthiness of appearance.
- **Raghunātha Śiromaṇi** — **16th-century** philosopher of **Navadvīpa** (Bengal); sparked a self-conscious, problem-driven phase of "new reasoners."
- **Madhusūdana Sarasvatī / Appaya Dīkṣita / Nīlakaṇṭha Caturdhara** — early-modern thinkers who renewed **Vedānta** while negotiating Mughal patronage.
- **Dārā Shikoh** — Mughal prince behind the bilingual project translating Sanskrit philosophy into Persian.
- **Muḥibbullāh Ilāhābādī** — Sufi philosopher in Persian and Arabic, defender of **Ibn ʿArabī**; **Maḥmūd Jaunpūrī** — Avicennan thinker; **Sullam al-ʿUlūm** (Muḥibb Allāh al-Bihārī) — milestone 17th-c. Indian textbook of Arab-Islamic logic.
- **ahiṃsā** — non-violence as a *holistic way of life* (toward all beings, plants, oneself, even objects); "the highest truth from which all dharma proceeds" (Mahābhārata).
- **emptiness / dependent origination** — Nāgārjuna's view that things have no fixed intrinsic nature but are "purely relational" — proposed as inspiration for a relational metaphysics.
- **Chike Jeffers** — scholar who will co-present the next series, on Africana philosophy.

## Nyāya among the six orthodox schools

- [00:01:04] **V.N. Jha** sets the frame: Nyāya is one of the **six orthodox schools** of Indian philosophy. The Sanskrit for an orthodox school is **āstika** (as against **nāstika**), and the word for philosophy itself is **darśana**.
- [00:01:30] A caution about that word: *darśana* comes from the root **dṛś**, "to see." It implies that philosophy is meant to make us *visualize, discover, realize* — to actually see the reality, not merely theorize about it. That sense of "seeing the real" is how the whole enterprise should be understood.
- [00:02:08] **Gautama** is the systematizer of Nyāya; he wrote the **Nyāya-Sūtra**, and from it flows "a history of a thousand years of dialogue through commentaries" — the Sūtra commented on by **Vātsyāyana**, then **Uddyotakara**, then **Vācaspati Miśra**, then **Udayana** ("Udayanācārya," **10th century CE**).
- [00:02:45] Why so many commentaries? Because each is a move in a debate: when Gautama puts forward a thesis, it is not acceptable "to one and all," so objections are raised, and commentaries are written to answer them. The result is a *recorded* history — "fortunately" preserved — of a thousand years of intense philosophical dialogue.
- [00:03:32] Over that time Nyāya developed its characteristic **tools**, all in the service of communication: an **epistemology**, a **logic**, and "a universal model of inquiry." Out of this came "very deep epistemological results."

## Why a logic-school is really after the end of suffering

- [00:04:00] Adamson presses the puzzle: Nyāya is famous for epistemology and logic, yet it shares the Vedic schools' goal of ultimate happiness (like Vedānta or Yoga). How do the technical project and the soteriological one connect?
- [00:04:30] **V.N. Jha:** philosophizing must have a *purpose* — the Indian tradition "did not believe that philosophy should be simply a mental exercise." Thought has to be put into practice; therefore philosophy must address human purpose.
- [00:05:00] Adamson reframes it: so I have beliefs about how to live, and what Nyāya really cares about is whether such belief counts as *knowledge*. Jha points to **Gautama's first sūtra**, which lays out a "roadmap" for a person to arrive at reality and identify who he is — answering the old Upaniṣadic question, *who am I?*
- [00:05:41] The first sūtra says that by the *true cognition* of **16 entities (padārthas)** — beginning with **pramāṇa** (the process / means of knowing) and **prameya** (the knowable, the object) — one attains the ultimate goal of human life. Nyāya accepts **four** means of knowing.
- [00:06:13] A careful point about *prameya* and the word **padārtha**: the **Vaiśeṣikas** also tried to understand the whole universe through language, and for them *padārtha* — "the referent of language," whatever language refers to — *is* the universe, a categorial term. Gautama borrows the same word but uses it differently: his **16 padārthas** are not the whole universe but a *chosen few* entities, the ones "required for engaging yourself in arguments in order to understand who you are."
- [00:07:07] The logic of the selection: to discover myself I must identify myself, and I am one entity among all. So I need to grasp "me and non-me" — *unless I know what I am not, how can I identify what I am?* Hence the inquiry must reach out into the whole universe (which is why the Vaiśeṣikas catalogue everything), but Gautama keeps only the entities useful for distinguishing the self from the not-self.
- [00:08:17] The link to liberation is in **sūtra 1.1.2**, which gives the whole **causal chain** of bondage. We live in a "conditioned state" and therefore suffer. To be conditioned means precisely *to have taken X to be Y* — if the reality is X but we cognize it as Y. This is **mithyā-jñāna**, wrong cognition / misconception.
- [00:09:02] From that misconception an *attitude* immediately arises; the attitude prompts *behavior*; behavior yields results, good and bad, which accumulate and ultimately drive the **cycle of birth and rebirth**. So the wheel of rebirth is produced, at root, by mithyā-jñāna.
- [00:09:46] The cure: since the root cause has been *diagnosed*, it can be removed — only by **tattva-jñāna** (also **yathārtha-jñāna**), correct cognition, knowing X as X. Right knowledge dissolves the misconception; the corrected attitude corrects behavior, which then "leaves no residue" to fuel further suffering and rebirth. *That* is what knowledge has to do with the wider aims of Nyāya — already present in Gautama, and shared (Adamson adds) with schools Vedic and non-Vedic alike: the Buddhists too make knowledge the road out of suffering.

## The five-member syllogism

- [00:11:05] Adamson turns to **Ujjwala Jha** for the other face of early Nyāya — *logic*. If you care about knowledge you care about arguments, and early Nyāya develops a **theory of the syllogism** that can look "simplistic or ad hoc." Is there a defense of it?
- [00:11:39] **Ujjwala Jha:** one should *not* call it ad hoc. The **sūtra-kāra** (Gautama) defined the syllogism with **five members**, each well-defined and then explained by the **bhāṣya-kāra** (Vātsyāyana). Had it been ad hoc, it would have been dropped once its purpose was served — but the same five-member form is defended right through the tradition: up to **Viśvanātha's Nyāya-Siddhānta-Muktāvalī** in the **17th century**, and by **Annaṃbhaṭṭa** in his *Tarka-saṃgraha*. Vātsyāyana even weighed a rival view that the syllogism should have **ten** members, and rejected it: only five.
- [00:13:16] The stock example, member by member:
  1. **Pratijñā** (the proposition): *parvato vahnimān* — "the mountain has fire."
  2. **Hetu** (the reason / probans): *dhūmavat* — "because it has smoke."
  3. **Udāharaṇa** (the example, which is also the statement of the invariable relation): *yatra yatra dhūmaḥ tatra tatra vahniḥ, yathā mahānasa* — "wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in the kitchen."
  4. **Upanaya** (the confirmatory recognition): *tathā ca ayam* — "and the same [smoke, invariably tied to fire] is on this mountain."
  5. **Nigamana** (the conclusion): *tasmāt tathā* — "therefore [the mountain has fire]."
- [00:13:52] The engine is the **invariable relationship** between smoke and fire: when one knows that X and Y are invariably related, then seeing X, "he invariably remembers Y."
- [00:14:39] The Mīmāṃsakas keep objecting: *why five? three are enough*, because two of the steps seem to be mere repetition. Nyāya refutes this; but even the sūtra-kāra was *aware* of the apparent repetition.
- [00:15:17] Which two steps repeat? The **first** member (*parvato vahnimān* — "there is fire on the mountain") and the **conclusion** ("therefore there is fire on the mountain") — i.e. I first state what I aim to prove, then conclude that it is proven. Nyāya answers this in its very *definition* of the conclusion: the term for conclusion is **nigamana**, defined as **punar-vacana**, the *restatement* of the proposition. (The opening proposition itself is **pratijñā**; the reason, **hetu**, glossed as "probans," the thing that proves.)
- [00:16:26] Adamson and Jha tighten the logic of the third member: the example must assert an *invariable relation* between the thing to be proved and the basis for proving it — and the direction matters. "Wherever there is smoke, there is fire," **not** the reverse — *because there can be fire without smoke.* A lit candle, or (a traditional Nyāya case) "a hot iron ball," has fire but no smoke.
- [00:17:48] Hence the terminology of **pervader and pervaded**: fire covers a *larger* area than smoke, so smoke (the *pervaded*) can be the basis for inferring fire (the *pervader*), but never the other way around.
- [00:18:05] How *general* is the syllogism meant to be? Adamson asks whether it can prove any questionable conclusion at all. **Exactly so** — because, as Jha says, Nyāya is essentially developing "the methodology of **dialectics**," and in a dialectic you must be able to prove your own point. This five-step form is **anumāna-prayoga**, "the form of inference."
- [00:18:56] A closing reminder on the fourth member, the **confirmatory recognition**: after recalling the invariable relation, you *confirm* that "the same type of smoke" — the kind invariably tied to fire — is present on the mountain, which is what licenses the conclusion. And **all five are necessary**; anything less does not complete the process. The five sentences together form one whole, the **mahāvākya**.

## Nyāya's "utter realism" and the status of perception

- [00:18:42] Adamson raises a tension: the proof is described *linguistically*, yet one of Nyāya's **pramāṇas** is **perception (pratyakṣa)**. When I perceive smoke, do I already see it *as* smoke (the relation coming "for free" with perception), or do I get raw data that I then interpret with a concept?
- [00:20:50] **V.N. Jha** redirects: that question belongs to another debate. The crucial thing is that Nyāya is "a system of **utter realism**." For Nyāya, *the real is that which is knowable and namable.*
- [00:21:07] The realist argument from causation: knowing is a process, so there is a **causal relationship** between what I know and the knowledge that emerges. A cause must precede its effect — the world I know is the **kāraṇa** (cause), the perception of it the **kārya** (effect). Since the cause precedes the effect, the world must precede, and so be *independent of*, my mind. Therefore "the entire world is independent of mind."
- [00:21:57] A second front: the **idealists** held that *reality cannot be captured through language* — a position that began in the Upaniṣadic period and was "concretized" by the **Buddhist logicians**. Against them, Nyāya consistently held that **language too can refer to reality**.
- [00:22:29] On these two issues — establishing the self, and establishing God — **Udayana** (Udayanācārya of Mithilā, **10th century**) wrote two texts: the **Ātma-tattva-viveka** (to establish the self) and the **Nyāyakusumāñjali** (to establish God / **Īśvara**).
- [00:22:45] The pay-off of the realist criterion: be it a **paramāṇu** (an atom — so subtle our gross eyes cannot perceive it) or God himself, *anything that exists is knowable and namable.* By these two criteria alone Nyāya makes its claim about what is real — "a very optimistic epistemology."

## Answering the skeptic's regress

- [00:23:17] Adamson: an optimistic epistemology is "under an obligation" to face skeptical attack, which duly comes from the Buddhists. He poses one form — the **regress of justification**: whatever **pramāṇa** I rely on, I seem to need a *further* reason guaranteeing its reliability; that reason will be another pramāṇa, and so on forever.
- [00:24:25] **Ujjwala Jha:** the accusation's own premise is faulty — namely that *a pramāṇa must already be known before it can operate toward its object.* Nyāya accepts **two types** of source of knowledge: one that is known first and then operates, and one that operates **even without itself being known**. (In Sanskrit: *jñātaṃ sat pramāṇaṃ bhavati* — something is a means of knowledge when it is itself already known — versus *ajñātaṃ sat pramāṇaṃ bhavati* — a means of knowledge that is not itself known.)
- [00:25:23] The basic case is **pratyakṣa (perception)**, whose pramāṇa is just the **sense organ**. For perceptual cognition you need *not* know your own sense organ first: the eye sees a color without the knower first "knowing the eye." So the regress never gets started for perception.
- [00:26:03] But the *other* means — **anumāna** (inference), **upamāna** (analogy / comparison), **śabda** (verbal testimony) — *do* require prior knowledge. Inference requires knowing the invariable relation between X and Y (itself grasped by perception — "perception is the basis of all pramāṇas"). **Upamāna** rests on knowing a *resemblance*. **Śabda** requires knowing the language — the **padārtha-sambandha-jñāna**, the relation between word and meaning, must be known by the hearer prior to communication.
- [00:27:49] Adamson maps this onto the modern **externalist / internalist** distinction in epistemology: externalists deny the knower must have *access* to the fact that they know (it is enough that their eyes work, the lighting is good, etc.); internalists insist you must somehow *know that you know*. Nyāya, he observes, looks **externalist about perception but internalist about the other sources** — for language you can know that your linguistic knowledge is accurate, a demand they do *not* make of perception. Jha confirms: "Yes."

## Yoga, transformation, and the road to the "new logic"

- [00:29:23] Adamson asks about Nyāya's links to the other Vedic schools — not just **Vaiśeṣika** (already discussed) but **Yoga**, with its practical, ethical orientation. Can such technical logic be brought together with yoga?
- [00:29:52] **V.N. Jha:** Yoga is itself "a plan of a journey — an *inner* journey — from a conditioned state of man to a deconditioned state." He invokes **Jayanta Bhaṭṭa**, the Kashmiri logician ("a very fascinating man"): one can travel only so far by *thought*; "unless thought is put into action, transformation is not going to be that easy." So the methods **Patañjali** sets out in the **Yoga-Sūtra** need to be practiced even by Naiyāyikas, because Nyāya too is ultimately a system *for transformation* — and transformation must happen *inside*. Patañjali's roadmap "should be made available to one in all systems," and the Naiyāyikas "very happily accepted" it.
- [00:31:09] Adamson: this combination of highly technical philosophy *and* ethical self-shaping in the same thinkers recalls ancient Greek philosophy. But how technical does it actually get — is there anything like **formal logic**?
- [00:31:59] **Ujjwala Jha:** not formal logic in the contemporary sense, but something arrived at over centuries. From the start Nyāya pursued **dialectics**, and the dialectics kept failing on the **ambiguity of ordinary language**: what Vātsyāyana (the bhāṣya-kāra) meant was misread by the Buddhist logicians, who raised objections; Uddyotakara answered them, but *his* meaning too was misunderstood — a thousand years of dialogue in which "one term is taken to mean something else by the other."
- [00:33:08] So the moment came when the *medium of communication itself* had to be reformed — a new, **unambiguous language** of discourse. The turn begins after **Praśastapāda** (**6th century CE**), who wrote a commentary on the very old **Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra** (roughly contemporary with the Nyāya-Sūtra, perhaps **100 BCE**); his *bhāṣya* notably brings in **Īśvara**, so the two schools begin to merge. **Udayana**, commenting on Praśastapāda, warns that the same terminology should *not* be reused loosely, and starts coining precise terms so that "if you want to say X, the hearer will understand only X and nothing else."
- [00:34:49] That terminology was developed further by **Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya** in the **13th century**, by way of **Śeṣadhara** (whose *Nyāya-Siddhānta-Dīpa* was "a little compact," later expanded fully by Gaṅgeśa). Gaṅgeśa's **Tattvacintāmaṇi** became "the magnum opus of **Navya-Nyāya**" — the point at which "the things are formalized," all ambiguity driven out for the sake of precise communication. The resulting **Navya-Nyāya language** ("new logic") is, she stresses, best understood as the *proper name of the language itself*.

## A living tradition

- [00:36:34] Adamson asks whether Nyāya carried on through early modernity — through the coming of Islam in India and even to the present — a *living* tradition, with its five-step syllogism still defended in the 17th century "giving Aristotle a run for his money."
- [00:37:11] **V.N. Jha:** yes. See Nyāya in **two phases** — up to the **10th century**, then the new form (**Navya-Nyāya**) afterward — the hinge being **Udayana**, who "could have an overlook" of why the same questions kept recurring and located the problem in the *medium of communication*. Language thus became central. Just as the West had its **linguistic turn** only very late, "after Chomsky," India had Udayana making the same point about the importance of language in the **10th century**: we can accept or reject only what we first *understand*, and natural language, the more we use it, "opens the gate for ambiguity."
- [00:38:42] So a new language of discourse was needed — and once **Vaiśeṣika and Nyāya had become one** (sharing the same metaphysics), the *other* systems too — **Mīmāṃsā, Yoga, Sāṃkhya, Dharmaśāstra, the eight varieties of Vedānta** — all began conducting their philosophical discourse through the medium of **Navya-Nyāya language**. After the 13th century, anyone doing Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā, grammar or poetics adopted it.
- [00:40:00] "To tell you the truth," Jha adds, in every traditional Sanskrit institution *even today* debate takes place only in Navya-Nyāya language — whatever school the debater belongs to, that is the shared medium of discourse. Adamson thanks both professors, notes the milestone of the show's first married-couple interview, and previews the next stretch: the tradition so often paired with Nyāya, **Vaiśeṣika**.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms

- **Nyāya** — one of the six **āstika** (orthodox) schools; a *darśana* (philosophy, from *dṛś* "to see") aimed at logic, epistemology and a universal method of inquiry.
- **Gautama** — systematizer of Nyāya; author of the **Nyāya-Sūtra**, whose first sūtra lists 16 entities and whose sūtra 1.1.2 gives the causal chain of bondage and release.
- **Vātsyāyana / Uddyotakara / Vācaspati Miśra / Udayana** — the chain of classical commentators that makes Nyāya a thousand-year recorded dialogue.
- **pramāṇa / prameya** — the means of knowing (Nyāya accepts four) and the knowable object; the first two of Gautama's 16 *padārthas*.
- **padārtha** — "referent of language"; for Vaiśeṣika it is the whole categorized universe, for Gautama a chosen few entities useful for telling self from not-self.
- **mithyā-jñāna** — wrong cognition, "taking X to be Y"; the root cause of attitude, action, accumulation, and the cycle of rebirth.
- **tattva-jñāna / yathārtha-jñāna** — correct cognition, "knowing X as X"; the cure that dissolves misconception and ends suffering.
- **The five-member syllogism (anumāna-prayoga)** — pratijñā (proposition), hetu (reason/probans), udāharaṇa (example + invariable relation), upanaya (confirmation), nigamana (conclusion); the five sentences form one **mahāvākya**.
- **parvato vahnimān, dhūmavat** — the stock inference: "the mountain has fire, because it has smoke."
- **invariable relation; pervader & pervaded** — smoke (pervaded) invariably accompanies fire (pervader), so smoke proves fire but not vice versa (a candle / hot iron ball has fire without smoke).
- **pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, śabda** — perception, inference, analogy/comparison, verbal testimony: Nyāya's four means of knowledge; perception is the basis of the rest.
- **jñātaṃ / ajñātaṃ sat pramāṇaṃ** — the two kinds of source: one known before it operates, one that operates without being known; perception is the latter, which blocks the skeptic's regress.
- **utter realism** — the real is what is **knowable and namable** (from atom, *paramāṇu*, to God, *Īśvara*); defended via cause (kāraṇa) preceding effect (kārya), so the world is mind-independent.
- **Udayana (10th c.)** — author of the **Ātma-tattva-viveka** (to establish the self) and **Nyāyakusumāñjali** (to establish God); the hinge to the "new logic," who diagnosed language as the source of endless dispute.
- **Praśastapāda (6th c.)** — commentator on the **Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra** whose bhāṣya brings in Īśvara, beginning the merger of Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika.
- **Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya (13th c.) & the Tattvacintāmaṇi** — the magnum opus of **Navya-Nyāya** ("new logic"), where Nyāya discourse is formalized to remove ambiguity; built on **Śeṣadhara**'s compact *Nyāya-Siddhānta-Dīpa*.
- **Navya-Nyāya language** — the precise technical idiom adopted across all later Indian schools (Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta, grammar, poetics) and still the medium of traditional Sanskrit debate today.
- **Jayanta Bhaṭṭa** — Kashmiri logician cited for the claim that thought must be put into *action*; hence even Naiyāyikas should practice **Patañjali's Yoga-Sūtra**, since Nyāya too aims at inner transformation.

## Dating: when and by whom was the Yoga-Śāstra written?
- [00:00:29] The episode is an interview about **Patañjali's *Yoga-Śāstra***, with **Philipp Maas**, assistant professor at the Department of South Asian, Tibetan, and Buddhist Studies at the **University of Vienna**. Adamson frames the text as "the founding document of yoga as a philosophical movement."
- [00:01:20] Maas: questions of authorship in pre-modern Indian philosophy are always very hard. We know **nothing** of Patañjali's biography; everything has to be reconstructed from the text itself.
- [00:01:43] His reconstruction: Patañjali was a **learned Brahmin** living roughly in the **4th or 5th century CE**, who set out to compose a work of yoga that would establish — *within a Brahmanical context* — a tradition holding that spiritual freedom and liberation from suffering can be gained by means of **meditation**.
- [00:02:14] The dating evidence is internal. Patañjali explicitly refers to the **Vijñānavāda** ("consciousness-only") Buddhist theory of **Vasubandhu**, who lived approximately **320–400 CE** — so Patañjali must come *after* him.
- [00:02:47] At the other end, the work was quickly influential: it was already cited in the **5th century** by **Bhartṛhari** in his commentary on his own *Vākyapadīya* (the philosophy of grammar). Bracketed by Vasubandhu below and Bhartṛhari above, the text lands in the **4th or 5th century**. Adamson notes this is "a lot better" than the dating we can manage for many texts in the series.

## One work or two? The sūtra–bhāṣya question
- [00:03:18] The text comes in two layers: short remarks (the **sūtras**) and a **bhāṣya** (commentary). Tradition says these had two authors — the sūtras by **Patañjali**, the bhāṣya by **Vyāsa** (Veda-Vyāsa). Maas questions this.
- [00:03:50] His first reason is text-internal evidence that the *Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra* was composed as a **unified whole**. The colophons to its four chapters call the work the *Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra-Sāṃkhya-pravacana* — "the authoritative exposition of yoga, the definitive explanation of Sāṃkhya philosophy" — with **no mention of Vyāsa** as the author of any separate commentary.
- [00:04:22] More decisively, the sūtras are **tightly integrated** into the bhāṣya: these brief verbal/nominal expressions are frequently *parts of longer sentences* in the commentary. The bhāṣya makes no sense without the sūtras — and, more strikingly, the **sūtras make no sense without the bhāṣya** (there are pronouns in the sūtras that refer back to bhāṣya passages). So the sūtras were never meant to stand alone.
- [00:05:04] Adamson presses: does this mean the author was trying to **deceive** readers — passing off a single work as an authoritative sūtra-text with an independent commentary — or was this simply how people wrote at the time? Maas: the **second**. Self-commentary was an established genre.
- [00:05:39] His parallels: **Vasubandhu's *Abhidharma-kośa*** is exactly this kind of enterprise — a very condensed metrical work plus the author's own explanation — and **Bhartṛhari's *Vākyapadīya*** is a very difficult verse composition with the author's own auto-commentary. So composing in this sūtra-plus-bhāṣya form is "not as unusual as it may seem."
- [00:06:15] Adamson likes this: he is always trying to convince people that **commentaries can be philosophically original and interesting** — and what proves it better than an author who writes an original work in the form of a self-commentary on his own words?

## Yoga and Sāṃkhya: two texts, one tradition?
- [00:06:31] The philosophical content is obviously close to the **Sāṃkhya** tradition, whose founding document is **Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā*** (covered earlier in the series). Adamson asks: is Patañjali simply being *influenced by* Īśvarakṛṣṇa? Or are Sāṃkhya and yoga a long-running "double tradition" — Sāṃkhya the theory, yoga the practice — emanating from a single source?
- [00:07:26] Maas: Patañjali shows **no awareness** of Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā*, and the *Sāṃkhya-kārikā* shows no awareness of the *Yoga-Śāstra*. So the direct relationship cannot be settled. (It is only the *later commentators* on the *Sāṃkhya-kārikā* who treat the *Yoga-Śāstra* as one of their authoritative sources.)
- [00:07:54] The deeper problem: much of the Sāṃkhya and yoga literature that once existed has been **lost** in transmission. We are left with "pieces of a jigsaw puzzle" and the difficult task of reconstructing the past from them.
- [00:08:22] What *is* clear from the pre-classical literature: in ancient and pre-classical South Asia there existed **many, partly rival, schools of Sāṃkhya**, sharing a basic **ontological dualism** of mind and matter. Most left no trace. Two survived to the present — Īśvarakṛṣṇa's and Patañjali's. So the picture is **two independent works drawing on similar (dualist) ideas**, not one copying the other.

## The dualism: consciousness vs. matter — and where the "mental organ" falls
- [00:09:07] On that dualist framework: Sāṃkhya distinguishes **Puruṣa** (consciousness / the subject) from **Prakṛti** (nature / matter). What might surprise a newcomer is that the **mental organ** — the thing you actually use in everyday cognition — is **not** identified with consciousness. Does Patañjali argue for separating them?
- [00:09:58] Maas refines the terms: for *pre-classical* Sāṃkhya "mind vs. matter" was fine, but for *classical* Sāṃkhya and the *Yoga-Śāstra*, "**consciousness**" is the more accurate word. The world consists of **two realms**: the realm of **matter** — active, comprising the outside world *and also all the information processed in the mind* — and, occupying "a very, very tiny space," **consciousness**.
- [00:10:44] Consciousness is held to be caused by an **unchanging entity, the Puruṣa** (the subject), which remains eternally identical with itself and is not really involved with matter. But because of the **proximity** of the mental organ to consciousness, consciousness **wrongly identifies itself with matter** — it takes the content displayed to it (as in a **mirror**) as affecting itself.
- [00:11:22] This fundamental **ontological error** is what causes pain and suffering. The yogi's task is to **disidentify** from the displayed content, by experiencing in meditation the **ontological difference** between mind and matter.
- [00:11:47] Adamson works the example: a **listener** hearing and understanding this interview cannot be an act of pure consciousness / Puruṣa, *because* pure consciousness is unchanging. So the listener processing word after word, glancing around, etc., is all on the **matter** side, not the mind side — a very different mind–matter distinction from the one familiar in contemporary English-speaking philosophy of mind.
- [00:12:35] Maas confirms: every event of consciousness has **two aspects** — a **content aspect** and a **consciousness aspect**. What stays identical across all experiences (dreaming, listening, pleasure, pain) is the consciousness element; everything else is content. This contradicts our experience that mental events are **unified** — but that felt unity *is itself* the ontological error to be removed. The error has a name: **avidyā**, the misconception of taking content for consciousness.

## Why meditate? Understanding is not enough
- [00:13:31] Adamson's worry: what Maas described sounds like a **straightforward philosophical claim**. If good arguments persuaded me it were true, I could simply *know* it — so why must a yogi also meditate, control the breath, and so on?
- [00:14:11] Maas: if you genuinely understood and were convinced, that would make you **start a spiritual career** — leave home and family, become an ascetic — *in order to make the experience* of the mind–matter difference in meditation. A rational, intellectual grasp is **not enough**; the difference must be **experienced** in what could be called a **mystical experience**, one believed to change your whole attitude to the world and to disentangle your Puruṣa from it.
- [00:15:08] The payoff: such a one lives a while longer in this world, but after physical death the **eternal subject withdraws** from the realm of matter and stays forever in isolation — **Kaivalya**, liberation. Hence the **praxis** of yoga is necessary to open the way into that direct experience.

## Stages on the path: samādhi and the kinds of meditation
- [00:15:42] Adamson's follow-up: surely I'm either making the consciousness-vs-content mistake or I'm not — maybe I make it only *some* of the time (getting distracted, caught up in content). Is there an account of genuine **stages** toward the goal, rather than just flickering in and out of correct identification?
- [00:16:32] Maas: the *Yoga-Śāstra* opens by **defining** yoga — *yoga samādhi*, "yoga is concentration" — and then enumerates **stages** of the mind: a distracted state, a dull state, a state where concentration is impossible at all, and then **one-pointedness** (concentration on a single point). One-pointedness is the **first** content of consciousness that counts as **yoga proper**. The final aim is to make **all content of consciousness cease**, leaving the **self-perception of the Puruṣa** — consciousness perceiving itself as pure consciousness.
- [00:17:31] Yoga teaches **several kinds of meditation** toward this aim:
  - **On the subject itself** — meditation that seeks an ever-clearer view of what the subject is, reducing the content part of consciousness until only self-perception remains.
  - **Theological meditation** — **Īśvara** (God) is taken as a *model of a liberated self*; the yogi concentrates on Īśvara, strips away the mythological content, and the picture of his own subject and the idea of Īśvara **mingle** until they too yield self-perception of consciousness.
  - **On objects of the outside world** — a thing is chosen and **reduced** down the Sāṃkhya **emanation scheme**: the material world resolves into the elements, the elements into subtler contents, then into a stage of **pure being**, and finally into **Prakṛti**, primordial matter "beyond any specification by language." Once that happens, only consciousness is left to shine upon and perceive itself.
- [00:19:21] Mastering these structures **takes a long time** — which is why **training** is needed. All the practical apparatus — **breath control, postures, ascetic rules of life** — exists to **facilitate** this meditation.

## Can a mystical insight be put in a text — or even taught?
- [00:19:47] Adamson's next worry: if most of the work is done by meditative practice, then the *teaching* a text like Patañjali's can offer seems very limited. The yogi reaches an elevated, "mystical" standpoint — how could that privileged perception be communicated in a text, or even in a **teacher–student relationship**? Isn't Patañjali ultimately just telling us to try it and spend 20 years meditating?
- [00:20:46] Maas: the **final mystical experience cannot be communicated in words** — the *Yoga-Śāstra*'s own attempts to do so collapse into **kōan-like, contradictory formulations**. But meditation still needs **structure**: the yogi must interpret his own experience against a **preconception**, and Sāṃkhya-yoga supplies exactly that structure. The teacher's instruction helps him **interpret** his experience and not get "lost on the way."
- [00:21:32] So — Adamson summarizing, Maas agreeing — philosophy provides the **initial motivation and explanation** of *why* to do this, **and** the framework that tells the practitioner *what* he is experiencing, *why*, and *where* it sits on the path of progress.
- [00:21:53] The upshot corrects a common view: it is **misleading** to call yoga merely "the practical dimension of Sāṃkhya." The whole point is that yoga is **both** theory **and** practice, the two inextricably bound together.

## Yoga between Brahmanism and Buddhism: an eclectic mix, or a deliberate synthesis?
- [00:22:09] Beyond Sāṃkhya, the text shows resonances with other traditions. Two examples: Patañjali says the yogi must be a member of the **Brahman class** (implying Vedic education — and it's unclear why that should be required); and the instruction to **give up desire** to achieve liberation strongly recalls **Buddhism**. Is Patañjali, then, just assembling an **eclectic mix** — Vedic, Sāṃkhya, earlier yogic, and Buddhist ideas?
- [00:23:14] Maas: **not** an eclectic mix, but a **conscious attempt to construct a new philosophy and a new religion** — one that opens, *within a Brahmanical context*, a route to escape from suffering and liberation from the cycle of rebirth that had not existed there before.
- [00:23:45] The historical backdrop is **two cultures**. On one side, **Brahmanical religion**, focused on **ritualism**, with its own worldview and idea of ritual causation. On the other, a different culture in the **eastern Ganges Basin**, where **Buddhism, Jainism, Ājīvikism** and others arose from the **5th century BCE**.
- [00:24:28] These so-called **śramaṇa** religions shared a worldview **foreign to the Brahmanical tradition**: they see the **world as suffering**, believe in a **cycle of rebirth**, and believe in **karma**.
- [00:24:41] By political circumstance, these eastern religions — what **Johannes Bronkhorst** calls the religions of "**Greater Magadha**" — gained predominance and founded the first pan-Indian empire, the **Mauryan** Empire, which propagated śramaṇa ideals. Adamson: "So this is King **Aśoka**, this is the dynasty." Maas: absolutely.
- [00:25:11] Later, Brahmanical religion regained political dominance — visible in the **2nd century** and more clearly in the **3rd–4th**, when the **Gupta** Empire founded the second pan-Indian empire. The Guptas were devoted to a Brahmanical religion that had by then been exposed to the śramaṇa movements for a very long time, and the Brahmins **had to react**.
- [00:25:52] One option was to **accept the śramaṇa worldview and integrate it into a Brahmanical frame** — and *this* is what Patañjali did. That is precisely why the *Yoga-Śāstra* shows such close similarity to Buddhism: the meditation theories Patañjali had to draw on **were Buddhist**, so much of his terminology is "clearly of a Buddhist coinage." Adamson: by this period Buddhist ideas and vocabulary had simply **spread throughout intellectual culture**. Maas: absolutely.

## Ancient yoga vs. the yoga of the gym
- [00:26:28] Adamson's closing (admittedly hard) question: what could any of this advanced, historically embedded philosophy have to do with **yoga as most people picture it now** — going to a gym class with a mat to stretch and exercise (plus perhaps some meditation)?
- [00:27:26] Maas: it **has** something to do with it — as the **final result of a long historical development**. The history of yoga is a **history of transformation**. Yoga was practised in a **Buddhist** context, transferred into a **Brahmanical** one, and then transformed further: adopted into **Advaita Vedānta**, into **tantric** circles, and later developed into **Haṭha-yoga**. Ascetic movements took over yogic postures and breath control. All the elements found in the *Yoga-Śāstra* were **eclectically absorbed** into different systems.
- [00:28:17] What persisted through it all is that the **Pātañjala yoga is identified as the beginning of the whole thing**: people say all of it was "originally taught by Patañjali." This continues today — go to a yoga class and the teacher will tell you "this comes from Patañjali," **unaware** of the many intervening transformations.
- [00:28:41] So it is **definitely wrong** to claim modern yoga is unchanged from what Patañjali taught — but Patañjali still functions as the **founding father** of the entire tradition.
- [00:28:58] Adamson closes: a fine example of how **continuity** works in the history of philosophy — and in human culture generally — **transformation and stability coming together**. Thanks to Philipp Maas; Jonardon Ganeri and Adamson return next time.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Philipp Maas** — the episode's guest; assistant professor at the Dept. of South Asian, Tibetan, and Buddhist Studies, University of Vienna; works on the composition of the *Yoga-Śāstra*.
- **Patañjali** — a learned Brahmin of the **4th–5th c. CE**; author/compiler of the *Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra*; the "founding father" figure of the yoga tradition.
- **Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra** — the founding document of yoga; a unified work of **sūtras + bhāṣya** (the colophons call it the *Sāṃkhya-pravacana*, the definitive exposition of Sāṃkhya yoga).
- **sūtra / bhāṣya** — terse remarks and their commentary; Maas argues both layers were written by **one** author as a self-commentary, since neither makes sense without the other.
- **Vyāsa (Veda-Vyāsa)** — traditionally credited with the bhāṣya as a separate author; Maas rejects this attribution.
- **Vasubandhu** — Buddhist thinker (c. **320–400 CE**); his **Vijñānavāda** ("consciousness-only") theory is referred to by Patañjali, fixing the text's earliest date. Author of the self-commentarial *Abhidharma-kośa*.
- **Bhartṛhari** — 5th-c. philosopher of grammar; cites the *Yoga-Śāstra* in his auto-commentary on the *Vākyapadīya*, fixing the text's latest date.
- **Īśvarakṛṣṇa / Sāṃkhya-kārikā** — author and founding text of classical Sāṃkhya; mutually unaware of Patañjali, suggesting two independent survivors of a once-larger Sāṃkhya literature.
- **Sāṃkhya** — dualist tradition (mind/matter, theory) paired with yoga (practice); existed in many rival pre-classical schools.
- **Puruṣa** — the unchanging eternal subject; the cause of consciousness, not really involved with matter.
- **Prakṛti** — primordial matter / nature; the active realm, including the mental organ and all cognitive content; "beyond specification by language."
- **avidyā** — the fundamental ontological error: taking the *content* of consciousness for consciousness itself; the root of suffering.
- **samādhi** — concentration; the *Yoga-Śāstra* defines yoga as samādhi, progressing through distracted/dull states to one-pointedness and finally to the cessation of all content.
- **Īśvara** — God; used as a *model of a liberated self* and an object of theological meditation.
- **Kaivalya** — "isolation," liberation; the eternal subject's final withdrawal from matter after death.
- **śramaṇa religions / "Greater Magadha"** — Buddhism, Jainism, Ājīvikism and others from the eastern Ganges Basin (5th c. BCE); held world-as-suffering, rebirth, and karma — ideas foreign to Brahmanism. Term from Johannes Bronkhorst.
- **Haṭha-yoga** — a later transformation of yoga (via Advaita Vedānta and tantra) lying behind much of the postural practice in modern yoga.

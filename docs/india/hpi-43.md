## Thinkers & texts introduced

- **The Jain founders** — Kundakunda, Umāsvāti, and Siddhasena Divākara, the syncretist response [00:17:08].
- **Vasubandhu** — **c. 400 CE**; wrote the *Abhidharmakośa*, then converted to Mahāyāna [00:08:14].
- **The Abhidharma & the rival vādas** — Pudgalavāda, Sarvāstivāda, Theravāda; systematic accounts of Buddhist doctrine [00:03:01].
- **Dignāga & Dharmakīrti** — the Yogācāra logicians (**c. 500** and **c. 600 CE**) who set the agenda for all later Buddhist philosophy [00:14:37].

## The problem of philosophical diversity — and two ways to answer it
- [00:00:35] We've now seen a variety of philosophical systems arise in ancient India, each built around a core text of aphoristic verses, or **sūtras**. This great plurality of doctrinal opinion is India's strength — but it is also a philosophical problem in its own right. For even though these systems emerged out of the same **Vedic** culture, they adopted contradictory positions on essentially every key issue: self, mind, and world; human aspiration and human ability.
- [00:01:09] The two traditions of this chapter — Buddhists and Jains — each offer a response to this problem of diversity, i.e. to the bare availability of opposing standpoints. One response is the **skeptic's**: our chronic inability to agree on philosophical questions shows that *no* one answer is justified. The other is the **syncretist's**: every position contains at least a grain of truth, so we should accept all of them — but only *conditionally*.
- [00:01:43] In India the skeptical road was taken by a school of Buddhism, most notably by **Nāgārjuna**; the syncretist road was the Jains' preferred option. Both ways of rejecting dogmatism drove innovations in **epistemology and logic**, as each tradition strained to avoid the trap of **self-refutation**.

## Setting the scene: Sanskrit, the śramaṇa movements, and intellectual context
- [00:02:01] Nāgārjuna lived in the **2nd century CE**, a critical moment for both Buddhism and Jainism. In this period thinkers of both traditions began writing in **Sanskrit**, having previously used **Pāli** (the Buddhists) and **Prākrit** (the Jains). Adopting Sanskrit let the **śramaṇa** (renouncer) movements meet their Vedic opponents on the same linguistic ground — and at the same time argue with one another.
- [00:02:32] Buddhist philosophy is often told as if it emerged in an intellectual vacuum. But just as the Brahminical schools refined their ideas in response to Buddhist critique, we must factor in the broader intellectual context to understand Buddhist or Jain thought. Having already surveyed the orthodox systems, we are well placed to do this.

## The branching of Buddhism: the vādas and the Abhidharma
- [00:02:55] We pick up the story after the death of **Aśoka**, the emperor who promoted Buddhist thought; he reigned in the **3rd century BCE**, roughly when Buddhism underwent the first of several splits into rival intellectual schools called **vādas**. These set out systematic accounts of what they took to be Buddhist doctrine in seven canonical texts, each called an **Abhidharma**.
- [00:03:25] Take the central Buddhist tenet that there is **no eternal, unchanging self or soul**. We readily associate "no self" with Buddhism — yet mental reality is in some sense *affirmed*, which is why Buddhist texts react critically against the frank materialism of the Cārvāka forerunner **Pāyāsi**. The truth about the self lies *somewhere in between*: we are neither identical to our bodies (as Cārvāka would have it), nor are we souls cut off from everything else (a view close to the **Jain** theory of the self).
- [00:04:00] So the Buddha was at least as concerned to position himself relative to other dissident groups as to critique Brahminical hegemony. To convey his own view he used similes: a person is *not* like the single thread running through a necklace of pearls, but like the flowing of a river or the flickering of a candle flame.
- [00:04:24] Pressing that last image: liberation from bodily existence is **nirvāṇa**, a "blowing out" of the flame. And there is a fuel that keeps the flame burning — in a human being that fuel is **craving, attachment, and false desire**.

## "Neither the same nor another": Nāgasena and King Menander
- [00:04:46] The Buddha's ideas about the self were avidly explored in later generations, as in one of the earliest works of philosophical Buddhism — a Pāli text describing the encounter between the Buddhist monk **Nāgasena** and the king **Menander**.
- [00:05:03] Menander asks: he who is reborn, is he the same person or another? Nāgasena replies: **"neither the same nor another."** Asked for an illustration, he offers the milk that turns to curds, then butter, then ghee: it would be wrong to call the ghee, butter, and curds the *same* as the milk — yet they came from it, so neither is it right to call them something *else*.
- [00:05:34] The rival vādas tried to work out the theory behind such metaphors. Back in episode nine we met the **Pudgalavāda**, distinguished by their willingness to admit there is in *some* sense an enduring person or self that changes over time. Another group, the **Sarvāstivāda**, rejected this — yet were themselves reluctant to say that *nothing* is real apart from the fleeting, momentary **dharmas** that make up an aggregate thing. Partly to explain how there can be **karmic** effects of past deeds, the Sarvāstivāda proposed that **past and future things are real or existent** ("Sarvāstivāda" = "the doctrine that everything exists").

## How the Buddhists wrote: sūtra vs. Abhidharma, and "skillful means"
- [00:06:14] With the Abhidharma writings we see the Buddhists adopting writing strategies different from the Brahminical schools. The Buddhists did **not** write sūtra texts — the compressed, difficult works needing explanatory commentary. Confusingly, the early Buddhists *do* use the term sūtra (Pāli **sutta**), but with a different meaning: the recorded sayings of the Buddha himself, set down in the **Nikāyas**.
- [00:06:50] The schools tried to get beneath these explicit teachings to reveal the deep unity of philosophical truth within. One standard exegetical move: distinguish those utterances of the Buddha that *directly* express the deep truth from those whose meaning needs drawing out.
- [00:07:11] This becomes a running theme in later Buddhism. The **Mahāyāna** especially deployed the notion of **skillful means**: as an ideal teacher, the Buddha often said things that are *not strictly true* in order to help listeners advance along the path to liberation.

## Theravāda and Sarvāstivāda: the Abhidharma's afterlives
- [00:07:29] The Abhidharma teachings had varying afterlives. **Sarvāstivāda** became dominant in northern India and Central Asia; **Theravāda** developed on the island of Ceylon (Sri Lanka).
- [00:07:43] Theravāda's most remarkable ancient representative lived in the **5th century CE**: **Buddhaghosa**, author of a definitive unified commentary on both the Nikāyas and the canonical Abhidharma treatises. His work, the **Visuddhimagga** ("Path to Purity"), still exercises great influence in Sri Lanka, Burma, and Thailand, where Theravāda is alive and well.
- [00:08:11] The Sarvāstivāda branch had even wider influence. Its key philosopher was **Vasubandhu** (**c. 400 CE**), who wrote a pivotal digest of Abhidharma theory, the **Abhidharmakośa**, plus an important analysis of the **five factors** that, according to the Buddha, constitute the psychophysical "river" that is a human being.

## Vasubandhu's conversion and the rise of Mahāyāna
- [00:08:34] Vasubandhu also belongs to the *next* chapter: the spread of **Mahāyāna** ("Great Vehicle") Buddhism — so called because its proponents wished to distinguish it from what they considered less complete accounts of the Buddha's teaching, condescendingly tagging rivals (including the Theravāda) **Hīnayāna**, "Lesser Vehicle."
- [00:08:56] Having begun as a Sarvāstivāda proponent, Vasubandhu was apparently persuaded by his brother **Asaṅga** to switch to Mahāyāna. He went on to write some of its fundamental texts — and even a *self-commentary* on his own *Abhidharmakośa*, now from the Mahāyāna perspective. That was enough to annoy another Abhidharma scholar, **Saṃghabhadra**, into writing a rival commentary defending Vasubandhu's *old* view against his new one. Among Vasubandhu's new Mahāyāna works, his **Twenty Verses** and **Thirty Verses** are works of immense philosophical accomplishment.
- [00:09:37] Mahāyāna had in fact already emerged well before Vasubandhu's time — sometime around the **last century BCE or first century CE**. Its claimed superiority turned on pursuing a **higher ethical goal**. Where others were content to become enlightened and reach the status of an **arhat**, the Mahāyāna emphasized the more exalted role of the **bodhisattva** (their school was originally also called the **Bodhisattvayāna**).
- [00:10:10] A bodhisattva is *more* than an arhat or even a Buddha, because he goes beyond his own perfect liberation and wisdom by seeking to bring *others* to that same state. So the Mahāyāna placed **other-regarding ethics** at the center of Buddhism — recognizing that great sacrifice and patience are required, and speaking of needing **millions of world-cycles** to reach the bodhisattva level.

## Mahāyāna's expanded canon and its many Buddhas
- [00:10:34] Mahāyāna is also distinguished by the texts it accepts as authoritative. It treats a number of new **sūtras** as genuine records of the Buddha's sayings, despite their becoming available only centuries after his death — they had been hidden in the meantime, or posthumously divulged by the Buddha in dreams.
- [00:10:56] More generally, Mahāyāna thinks of the Buddha *less* as an individual person and *more* as a **functional role**: the Buddha teaches at all times and adopts many guises, and there has been more than one Buddha — indeed as many as the grains of sand in the Ganges.

## Two Mahāyāna sub-schools: Madhyamaka and Yogācāra
- [00:11:14] So far Buddhist history is less a single flowing river than a set of branching tributaries — and this continues with Mahāyāna, which itself split into two main sub-schools. One is **Madhyamaka**, the "philosophy of the Middle Way," founded by **Nāgārjuna**. Nāgārjuna's philosophy went on to great things in Tibet, China, and Japan, and is the ultimate source of **Zen** Buddhism.
- [00:11:46] It is tempting to think of Buddhism as having a first Indian phase and a later phase where it gradually disappeared from India even as its ideas spread across Asia — but the waters are muddier than that. Sri Lanka hosted Theravāda from early on, and some of our earliest sources for various strands of Buddhism (including Madhyamaka) are in **Chinese**. We owe valuable information about Indian Buddhism to two Chinese travelers, **Fa Xian** in the **5th century** and **Xuanzang** in the **7th** — and there are even cases where Buddhist works were written in Chinese and translated *into* Sanskrit.
- [00:12:21] Nāgārjuna lived around **150–200 CE**, making him a contemporary of **Gautama**, author/compiler of the **Nyāya-Sūtra**. His most famous works are the *Verses on the Middle Way* and the *Refutation of My Opponents*, in which he tries to undermine the Nyāya theory of knowledge and answers Nyāya criticisms of his own work.
- [00:12:47] His central thesis — though, as we'll see, even calling it a "thesis" is problematic — is that **all human conceptual schemes and all philosophical systems are empty, or null**. Hence his teaching is the **philosophy of emptiness**. He is a severe critic of the **pramāṇa** method of rational inquiry, urging instead the use of reason to expose **inconsistencies within the fabric of one's own conceptions**, never mind those of rivals.
- [00:13:24] Why call this radical brand "Middle Way"? Because it neither embraces the **permanence** of things nor denies that anything is real. Just as Nāgasena told Menander that things are neither the same nor different over time, Nāgārjuna says things are neither simply independently existent nor non-existent — rather they are real **insofar as they depend on other things**. To be is to be relative. (If that sounds mysterious, a Buddhist would advise you not to let it worry you.)
- [00:14:06] The other Mahāyāna sub-school is **Yogācāra**, first set out by the brothers **Asaṅga** and **Vasubandhu**. It is not so much opposed to Madhyamaka as **complementary**, focusing on psychological and spiritual issues. It is not to be confused with the **Yoga** branch of Brahminical thought; it is "Yogācāra" because it emphasizes spiritual practices, **yoga**, like meditation.
- [00:14:30] Around **500 CE**, about a century after Vasubandhu, Yogācāra found its most brilliant exponent in **Dignāga**, whose ideas in logic and epistemology — and their criticism and revision at the hands of **Dharmakīrti** (about **600 CE**) — we'll examine later. That's about as far as this series goes, but these figures **set the agenda** for all subsequent Buddhist philosophy: later thinkers tend to write either commentaries on Dharmakīrti or manuals presenting his thought in more manageable form.

## Scholasticism and its institutions: stūpas, monasteries, and Nālandā
- [00:15:13] This bewildering multiplication of schools and textual traditions may recall the **scholasticism** of later Islamic or Latin medieval thought — all the more so since the social and institutional context was not dissimilar. Just as the Latin Church and secular princes fostered the European universities, and Muslim rulers and viziers built the **madrasa** system, so Buddhism received intermittent but often substantial support from various Indian rulers.
- [00:15:42] Generous **laypersons** did more than put food in monks' begging bowls. Their lavish gifts made it possible to build spectacular **stūpas** (Buddhist shrines), to carve and decorate whole complexes of caves for monastic study, and to found such centers of learning as **Nālandā**, a huge monastery often described as playing the role of a university.
- [00:16:11] You might have wondered how Buddhist scholars could spend decades studying there, given the relative simplicity of the Buddha's original teaching. But considering the mass of writings produced by all these groups — and the fact that a competent Buddhist philosopher needed a detailed understanding of the **Brahminical** schools too, in order to refute them — you begin to believe that "one or two reincarnations might be needed" to explain a truly accomplished thinker like Vasubandhu or Dharmakīrti.

## The Jains: inclusivism and the doctrine of non-one-sidedness
- [00:16:43] Happily, things are more straightforward with the **Jains**. As mentioned in episode 15, Jainism split into two branches — the **Śvetāmbara** and **Digambara** ("white-clad" and "sky-clad") — but we can ignore this, because the philosophers discussed would be read and accepted by *both* sides. The three founding figures of Jain philosophy are **Kundakunda**, **Umāsvāti**, and **Siddhasena Divākara**, who may have lived in the **3rd, 4th, and 5th centuries** respectively, though their dates are uncertain.
- [00:17:20] No less than the Buddhists, the Jains were deeply knowledgeable about other systems, and their descriptions tend to be **faithful** — which makes them a useful source for otherwise-lost ideas. We could not say nearly so much about **Cārvāka** without the later Jain philosopher **Prabhācandra**. They were also sophisticated **doxographers**: the **8th-century Haribhadra Sūri** composed a *Compendium of the Six Systems* — which here means Buddhism and Jainism *plus four Hindu systems*, not the six Brahminical schools. The only thing Haribhadra regards as beyond the pale is **Cārvāka** naturalism.
- [00:18:01] There's a good reason for the Jains' interest in others' ideas, and it is not merely "knowing one's enemy" — actually, Jains don't really have enemies. Their philosophy is justly celebrated for its **inclusivism**, codified in a theory of the **non-one-sidedness** of reality, and in the idea that truth is always approached from a **specific point of view**.
- [00:18:27] **Kundakunda** sums up Jain pluralism in a dense remark: all substances are non-different from the *substantial* viewpoint, but again different from the *modificational* viewpoint, because of the individual modification pervading each for the time being; "according to some modification or other, it is stated that a substance exists, does not exist, is indescribable, is both, or otherwise." (The elements of this — especially the different metaphysical standpoints — get unpacked in later episodes.)
- [00:19:04] Whereas Kundakunda wrote exclusively in **Prākrit**, **Umāsvāti** was the first Jain philosopher to compose in **Sanskrit** — calling his great treatise a *sūtra*, "the sūtra on what there is." Almost every significant later Jain philosopher would write a commentary on this book, using the chance to defend their own views and attack their foes'. So maybe Jains *do* have enemies after all — certainly they were willing to defend their **perspectivalism** against critics.
- [00:19:40] We should bear this in mind in considering how Jain epistemology relates to the rest of their tradition. It's often assumed this epistemology is an intellectual version of **ahiṃsā**: just as the Jain observes strict non-violence in practical matters, so in theory the Jain embraces tolerance and harmony. That reading may not be wholly wrong, but it seems too simple — for the Jains were every bit as ready as the Brahminical thinkers and the Buddhists to engage in that quintessential ancient Indian practice, **debate**.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **the problem of diversity** — that India's many systems contradict one another on every key issue; the Buddhists answer it as skeptics ("no answer is justified"), the Jains as syncretists ("every view holds a conditional grain of truth").
- **vāda** — an intellectual school/sect of Buddhism; the post-Aśoka splits produced rival vādas with their own canonical accounts of doctrine.
- **Abhidharma** — the seven canonical texts giving systematic accounts of Buddhist doctrine; Buddhists wrote these instead of cryptic sūtras.
- **dharmas** — the fleeting, momentary constituents that make up an aggregate thing (e.g. a person).
- **Pudgalavāda / Sarvāstivāda** — rival vādas: the former admits an enduring "person/self"; the latter denies it but holds that past and future things are real, to ground karmic effects.
- **nirvāṇa** — the "blowing out" of the flame of bodily existence; its fuel is craving, attachment, and false desire.
- **Nāgasena & Menander** — Pāli dialogue: the reborn person is "neither the same nor another," illustrated by milk → curds → butter → ghee.
- **Theravāda / Buddhaghosa / Visuddhimagga** — the Ceylon (Sri Lanka) school; Buddhaghosa (**5th c. CE**) wrote the *Visuddhimagga* ("Path to Purity"), still influential in Sri Lanka, Burma, Thailand.
- **Vasubandhu** — **c. 400 CE**; wrote the *Abhidharmakośa* as a Sarvāstivādin, then converted to Mahāyāna (urged by his brother Asaṅga) and wrote the *Twenty Verses* and *Thirty Verses*.
- **Mahāyāna ("Great Vehicle")** — emerged **c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE**; centers other-regarding ethics on the **bodhisattva** (who exceeds the **arhat** by bringing others to liberation); dismisses rivals as **Hīnayāna**; accepts a wider sūtra canon and treats the Buddha as a recurring functional role.
- **skillful means** — the Mahāyāna idea that the Buddha, an ideal teacher, sometimes says things not strictly true to move listeners toward liberation.
- **Madhyamaka / emptiness** — Nāgārjuna's "Middle Way": all conceptual schemes are empty/null; things are neither independently existent nor non-existent but real *insofar as they depend on others* — "to be is to be relative." Source of Zen.
- **Yogācāra** — Mahāyāna sub-school of Asaṅga & Vasubandhu, complementary to Madhyamaka, stressing meditation (yoga); its logicians **Dignāga** (**c. 500 CE**) and **Dharmakīrti** (**c. 600 CE**) set the agenda for later Buddhist philosophy.
- **pramāṇa** — means/method of rational inquiry; Nāgārjuna is its severe critic, preferring to expose internal inconsistencies.
- **Nālandā / stūpa** — the great monastic "university" funded by rulers and lay donors; stūpas are Buddhist shrines.
- **Jain non-one-sidedness (perspectivalism)** — reality has many aspects, truth is always approached from a viewpoint; expressed by Kundakunda's "substantial vs. modificational" standpoints.
- **Jain founders** — Kundakunda (Prākrit), Umāsvāti (first to use Sanskrit; his *sūtra* drew commentary from nearly every later Jain), Siddhasena Divākara.
- **ahiṃsā** — non-violence; often read as the practical analogue of Jain epistemic tolerance — though the Jains debated as fiercely as anyone.
- **Cārvāka doxography** — Jain sources (e.g. Prabhācandra, and Haribhadra Sūri's *Compendium of the Six Systems*) preserve much of the otherwise-lost Cārvāka materialism they reject.

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Ahiṃsā** — "non-violence" or "non-injury"; the episode's central concept, broader than vegetarianism [00:03:01].
- **Mahāvīra and the ford-makers** — last of the 24 *tīrthaṅkaras* ("ford-makers"); Jainism is named for his honorific **Jina** ("conqueror") [00:07:30].
- **The Laws of Manu** — Hindu text that both laments meat-eating and licenses ritual killing as non-violence [00:03:54, 00:19:40].
- **The Mahābhārata / Bhagavad-Gītā** — Hindu epic that contains exhortations to non-violence yet stages a dialogue urging Arjuna into war [00:04:00].
- **Padmanabh Jaini** — modern scholar of Jainism quoted on food as the root cause of all *hiṃsā* [00:11:46].

## Philosophy is inevitable — and the steak on your plate
- [00:00:40] Of all the university disciplines, philosophy is the hardest to avoid *in real life*. You can skip literature, dodge physics and geology, even (if you play your cards right) get away without much maths — "though not if you actually play cards." Philosophy, by contrast, is inevitable: we all wrestle with philosophical problems whether or not we call them that, and never more so than with ethical dilemmas.
- [00:01:13] The most obvious example: is it morally defensible to **eat animals**? Rich or poor, anyone can live as a vegetarian, so the choice faces everyone. If you eat meat it's either because you think there's nothing wrong with it, or because its wrongness "doesn't weigh heavily enough" to keep you from a juicy steak now and again.
- [00:01:41] Yet despite being a textbook case of everyday philosophy, the ethics of killing animals only *recently* became much-debated by philosophers. Today there are figures like the utilitarian **Peter Singer**, famous for arguing for vegetarianism. This contrasts sharply with European antiquity and the Middle Ages: apart from a few exceptions — notably the late ancient thinkers **Plutarch** and **Porphyry** — philosophers there rarely argued at length about meat at all.
- [00:02:15] In ancient India it was utterly different. **Non-violence was one of the key themes** of religious and philosophical culture. Buddhists, and especially Jainas, defined themselves by their reluctance or refusal to eat meat. Animal sacrifice was central to the Vedic culture they criticized — yet even texts growing out of *that* culture often urge us to avoid harming animals. And this is no museum piece: one of the most famous features of Indian society today is widespread vegetarianism. So the topic, Adamson notes, gives us "something to sink our teeth into."

## The real topic is ahiṃsā, not vegetarianism
- [00:02:55] First clarification: the subject is **not vegetarianism** as such, but what the traditions call **ahiṃsā** — "non-violence" or "non-injury."
- [00:03:10] Avoiding violence may *seem* to imply vegetarianism, but some Vedic texts denied this, claiming the sacrifice of animals could be **consistent with ahiṃsā**. Conversely, those who *did* think non-violence required a vegetarian diet never thought it meant only that. Ahiṃsā obviously also covers avoiding violence toward humans — and, as the Jainas hold, one can even inflict *hiṃsā* on plants, or commit violence by lighting a fire or drinking water straight from a well.
- [00:03:43] The breadth of ahiṃsā's *implications* is matched by the breadth of its *acceptance*. It is most prominent in the dissident traditions of **Buddhism and Jainism**, but exhortations against violence also appear in Hindu texts like the **Laws of Manu** and even the **Mahābhārata** — despite the fact that its most famous section, the **Bhagavad-Gītā**, is a dialogue urging participation in war.

## Where did ahiṃsā come from? Ritual anxiety, not first a critique
- [00:04:11] Strikingly, ahiṃsā has more often been studied by historians of *religion* than of philosophy. It's controversial how and why ancient Indians began thinking in these terms, but scholars usually trace it to **religious practice**: its origins may lie in the **fear that ritualized violence could bring retribution**.
- [00:04:37] That fear could have produced taboos and devices designed to **deflect blame** from the sacrificer — reciting a formula to secure expiation for slaying an animal, or laying a blade of dry grass between the axe and a tree being felled for a sacrificial post. (Note the second case: it's a *plant*, not an animal, treated as the potentially injured party.)
- [00:05:06] The upshot for the history of the idea: ahiṃsā did **not** first emerge as Buddhists and Jainas accusing the priests of violence from the outside. Those external accusations were *preceded* by an **anxiety or embarrassment about sacrificial violence from within the Vedic tradition itself**.

## Is non-violence moral, or merely self-interested?
- [00:05:27] A related controversy bears on whether ahiṃsā is even *philosophically* interesting: was non-violence pursued for **moral** reasons, or only religious ones? If the worry is purely about avoiding retribution, then the sacrificer is anxious about **himself**, not the interests of the animal he kills. The **Laws of Manu** sounds this self-interested note "with what sounds like a tone of wistful regret": we can't eat meat without injuring the living, and that injury blocks a blessed afterlife — so we must do without meat.
- [00:06:01] But a fear of *vengeance* already implies that the animal (or plant) **has interests of its own** — the very interests the sacrificer risks violating. And ahiṃsā wasn't justified only by self-interest. One Buddhist work offers a reflection "strikingly reminiscent of the biblical golden rule": *since I want to live, I would find it disagreeable for someone to take my life; another, too, wants to live and would find it disagreeable for me to take his; what is unpleasant to me is unpleasant to him — so how could I inflict on another what I find unpleasant myself?* Similar ideas appear in Hindu texts, including the Mahābhārata.

## Jainism, the religion of non-violence
- [00:06:55] More than Buddhism or Hinduism, it was **Jainism** that was marked by an "unswerving devotion" to ahiṃsā — it has been called *a religion of non-violence*. If you know one thing about Jaina practice, it's probably the extreme measures: monks wearing **masks to avoid inhaling insects** and gently **sweeping the ground** before them as they walk, lest they tread on unseen life.
- [00:07:30] Jainism takes its name from the honorific **Jina** ("conqueror"), bestowed on **Mahāvīra**, a contemporary of the Buddha. He is often called the founder of Jainism, but — by the Jainas' own belief — that's wrong: he was the **last of 24 "ford-makers,"** whose title refers to providing a *ford*, a way out, of the unending flow of reincarnation. The Jainas regard Mahāvīra and his predecessors as divine saviours, all teaching the same lesson about escaping the cycle of rebirth.
- [00:08:08] As seen in episode six, the Jainas held that escape comes only through the **total elimination of karma**. Even *good* karma ties you to rebirth (though it at least secures a more favourable one). This presupposes a **dualist** view of the person: the soul is an immaterial entity that enters into relation with the physical world and a series of individual bodies.

## Karma as a sticky substance — and what makes it stick
- [00:08:43] Karma itself is described as if it were a **physical substance**, like **dust** that clings to the soul more easily when we give in to passion or bad intention. The Jaina texts capture this nicely by calling passion **kaṣāya**, which literally means **glue or resin**.
- [00:09:02] And it isn't only barbecue ribs that make you sticky: consuming **any** meat builds bad karma, since it inevitably involves the death of a being that **desires to live**.
- [00:09:15] But Jaina asceticism is "more than observing ahiṃsā, and observing ahiṃsā is more than abstaining from meat." Jainism is the **most ascetic** of the world's organized religions, perhaps in all history. Renouncers and laity alike look to the ford-makers, and especially Mahāvīra, whose self-restraint "reached superhuman levels" — he and the others are said to have gone weeks, even a year, **without eating anything at all**.
- [00:09:49] Through extreme practice the ascetic can shed all karma and become a **kevalin** — "someone who is alone," i.e. without the accretion of karma. The notorious practice of **sallekhanā**, in which a renouncer adopts total immobility until death by thirst, exposure and hunger, has been criticized as **religiously inspired suicide**. The Jainas insist those who take this path do **not desire death** (legend includes **Chandragupta**, founder of the Mauryan dynasty and grandfather of Aśoka); rather, they are avoiding the acquisition of *new* karma that might doom them to another rebirth.

## The elephant and the rabbit
- [00:10:39] For ahiṃsā, vegetarianism is only the beginning. A "delightful story" brings home what's expected of the perfectly non-violent renouncer. A **forest fire** breaks out, and all the animals crowd around a lake to escape, packed shoulder to shoulder. An **elephant lifts its foot** briefly — and a **rabbit darts into the spot** beneath it. Rather than crush the rabbit, the elephant holds its foot aloft **for three days**, before finally collapsing and dying of fatigue. The happy ending: the elephant is reborn as a human and later reincarnated as an associate of Mahāvīra himself.

## "You don't have a leg to stand on" — and the lay community's help
- [00:11:25] You might object that, in pursuing absolute non-violence, the Jainas "likewise don't have a leg to stand on": you cannot get through life without harming a living thing, because we need to eat, and **plants are alive too**. As the aptly named scholar **Padmanabh Jaini** put it, *the desire for food is the prime cause of all hiṃsā, since food cannot be consumed without destroying another life form.*
- [00:11:57] Far from denying this, the Jainas **avoid many fruits and vegetables** they believe to be aggregates of large numbers of life forms, and even restrict **water**, which a Jaina ascetic should drink only when **strained or boiled** to remove tiny organisms.
- [00:12:21] But even so, avoiding all violence is impossible for the renouncer **without assistance** — and help is at hand in the **Jaina layperson**. As in Buddhism, the monastic life is supported by lay followers who live by **less rigorous precepts**. A renouncer forbidden to **handle fire** (which would kill creatures caught in the flames) instead takes water **already boiled** by a layperson; monks likewise subsist on **alms** of donated food.

## The rich-uncle robbery — and why leftovers are the answer
- [00:13:21] Is this a *coherent* way to handle the dilemma? Consider an analogy: I need money fast, and the only way is to rob my rich uncle — but my moral scruples forbid it. So I get my **less fastidious friend** to rob the house and split the cash. I didn't commit the robbery, but surely I'm **morally culpable**. By the same logic, the Jaina renouncer would seem to **shoulder the hiṃsā** committed in preparing his food, even if he didn't do the preparing.
- [00:13:57] But there's more to the story. The renouncer may only take food and drink **prepared without him in mind**, and he **verifies** this before accepting it: he should live on **leftovers**, things that just happened to be available. Back to the analogy: this is more like accepting a **charitable donation from a thief** after confirming the money was *not stolen for your sake*.

## Intention is the heart of it — the innocent bystander
- [00:14:32] A critic still won't be satisfied: doesn't *knowingly benefiting* from a dubious act make your situation dubious? The deeper Jaina response invokes the **centrality of intention**. It is passion, desire, or intention that makes karma stick. By avoiding violence himself and eating only food **not intended for him**, the renouncer **neither intends violence nor benefits from an intention** to do violence on his behalf — he is "quite literally an **innocent bystander**."
- [00:15:11] The same goes for **unintentionally** stepping on an insect or plant: the renouncer's conscience stays clean **so long as he took every precaution**. Hence the further precautions — **not travelling in the rainy season**, when more life is underfoot, and **not eating after dark**, since before electricity eating after sundown risked swallowing a bug. ("Maybe no one should eat after dark.")

## King Yaśodhara and the flour rooster
- [00:15:53] Intention's importance is dramatized by the story of **King Yaśodhara**. He has shed blood in war, but since **death was never his goal** he remains pure. Then, for complicated reasons, he resolves to perform an animal sacrifice — and, to avoid killing anything real, has a **fake rooster made out of flour** (a practice attested in other texts too). No real rooster dies, yet "the Jaina storyteller cries foul": Yaśodhara is **elaborately punished in the afterlife**, *simply because his intention was violent.*
- [00:16:37] Notice, too, that the tale implies a **Jaina king could pursue military aims**; Jaina laypersons have frequently served as soldiers. It's even been proposed that the **heroic ethic** of Jaina asceticism was seen as continuous with the warrior heroism of the great Hindu epics. Still, only laypersons fight — the way of the **renouncer** would be to **die rather than do violence**.

## Against utilitarianism: an individualist goal
- [00:17:08] How does this stack up against modern views? There's an easy contrast with **utilitarianism**, which tries to **minimize total harm and suffering**, animals included. Jainas do try to persuade others toward non-violence, but their ancient approach to ahiṃsā aims at a **different, more individualist goal**: not simply reducing the overall amount of violence (though that follows too), but **not being personally implicated** in any act of violence.
- [00:17:53] There's an irony here. The Jaina renouncer is sometimes accused of inflicting violence **on himself**, his asceticism is so severe — yet from a utilitarian standpoint he can look as though he's **concentrating only on his own self-interest**. That's why he needn't worry that his lifestyle depends on some hiṃsā committed by the laity: like other ancient ethical traditions in and beyond India, **Jainism was about shaping and perfecting the self**. The renouncer leaves the world better than he found it, but the main thing is to **leave it and not come back** — purifying the soul into a karma-free kevalin.

## The Buddhist challenge: the middle way
- [00:18:36] Jaina teaching was challenged by rivals of its own time. The **Buddhists complained Jaina asceticism was excessive**. The Buddha himself *began* his quest with Mahāvīra-like discipline and self-denial, including a **starvation diet** — but came to adopt a **middle way** between the extreme ascetic and the comfortable householder.
- [00:19:00] He even parted ways with a follower, **Devadatta**, who wanted the community to adopt **more rigorous** rules, including strict vegetarianism. Instead, Buddhist writings suggest **meat might be allowed** for the monk on the **same basis** Jainas used for their vegetarian diet: it is permissible **provided the meat was not prepared with the monk in mind**. This rule may even have been **absorbed into Buddhism from Jaina practice**.

## Hindu intention: how sacrifice becomes non-violence
- [00:19:34] Hindu authors could exploit the **same idea of intention** to legitimate "that most un-Jaina of practices," the **animal sacrifice**. The **Laws of Manu** say slaying animals **in a ritual context** is in fact ahiṃsā and **doesn't count as real killing**. As the **Bhagavad-Gītā** suggests, sacrifice is needed to **hold the world together**.
- [00:19:53] This was likewise the basis on which **Arjuna** was urged into battle: so long as the fight is undertaken **with Kṛṣṇa in mind** and not from the intention of achieving any result **for himself**. The upshot is a **shared philosophical rationale for ahiṃsā** across these traditions, despite huge variation in which practices counted as consistent with it.

## Agreement buried under insult
- [00:20:13] Given the competition between rival groups, **disagreement got more emphasis than agreement**. For centuries Hindus would sneer at the "squalid lifestyle" of the dissident sects, Buddhists would snipe at Jainas for "absurdly excessive self-punishment," and Jainas would proudly claim to be the only ones who really take non-violence seriously.
- [00:20:43] One Jaina text mocks the Buddhist willingness to eat animals that **died of old age**: this, the author remarks, is like **seducing a widow after her husband has died** — the underlying desire is the same as a man's who would slaughter an animal for meat, or murder a husband to steal his wife.

## A split within Jainism: white-clad vs. sky-clad
- [00:21:03] Disputes over the demands of ahiṃsā and asceticism even erupted **within the Jaina community**. Early on it split into two branches: the **Śvetāmbara** ("white-clad") and the **Digambara** ("sky-clad") — referring respectively to the Śvetāmbara's white clothing and the **nudity** of the Digambaras, itself a form of rigorous asceticism.
- [00:21:28] The Digambaras' stringent reading surfaced in debates about **diet**: they insisted a perfect kevalin like Mahāvīra — and any liberated renouncer — would **no longer need to eat at all**, freeing them even from indirect contact with hiṃsā. The Śvetāmbaras endorsed the more common-sense view that the need for food, unlike that for sex or comfort, is an **inevitable, if regrettable, part of embodied life**.

## Philosophy or religion? A blurry line
- [00:22:01] A recurring question about these traditions is whether they are really **philosophical or religious**. The case of non-violence shows the line is "at best a blurry one." Ahiṃsā may seem simple — it boils down to **doing no harm** — yet it has taken the form of **both a religious taboo and an ethical precept**, advanced by means ranging from **doctrinaire assertion to subtle debate to crude insult**.
- [00:22:43] That mix is "going to stay with us" — especially the subtle debate, though also the occasional crude insult. Nothing typifies the next period, the **Age of the Sūtra**, so much as disagreement and debate, between and within schools: traditions will split (as the Jainas did), the disputatious climate will spur new reflection on **argumentative method**, and some groups — the Jainas included — will be led to ask whether **any viewpoint is really better off than its rivals**.
- [00:23:20] But before the sūtras, the next episode turns to a less-celebrated feature of ancient Indian thought: the **participation of women in philosophy** — already glimpsed in the women who appeared as interlocutors in the Upaniṣads. "And by the way, no animals were harmed in the making of this podcast."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **ahiṃsā** — "non-violence" / "non-injury"; broader than vegetarianism, covering humans, animals, plants, even harm via fire or unstrained water.
- **hiṃsā** — violence / injury; the harm ahiṃsā seeks to avoid.
- **kaṣāya** — "glue" or "resin"; the Jaina word for passion, which makes karma (likened to dust) stick to the soul.
- **kevalin** — "one who is alone"; a soul that has shed all karma and attained liberation.
- **sallekhanā** — the Jaina practice of fasting/immobility unto death; defended as avoiding new karma, not as suicide; legendarily including Chandragupta.
- **ford-makers (tīrthaṅkaras)** — the 24 Jaina saviours who provide a "ford" out of reincarnation; **Mahāvīra** was the last, hence the honorific **Jina** ("conqueror") that names Jainism.
- **Mahāvīra** — contemporary of the Buddha; exemplar of superhuman self-restraint and devotion to ahiṃsā.
- **Laws of Manu** — Hindu text that laments meat-eating yet rules that ritual animal sacrifice counts as ahiṃsā, not real killing.
- **Mahābhārata / Bhagavad-Gītā** — Hindu epic containing both non-violence teaching and the dialogue urging Arjuna into battle with Kṛṣṇa in mind, not self-interest.
- **Padmanabh Jaini** — scholar of Jainism: "the desire for food is the prime cause for all forms of hiṃsā."
- **Yaśodhara** — king punished in the afterlife for sacrificing a flour rooster, because his *intention* was violent — illustrating intention's centrality.
- **Devadatta** — Buddhist follower who wanted stricter rules (incl. vegetarianism); the Buddha instead chose the **middle way** and a "not-prepared-for-me" meat allowance.
- **Śvetāmbara / Digambara** — the "white-clad" and "sky-clad" (nude) branches of Jainism; split partly over whether a liberated kevalin still needs to eat.
- **Peter Singer / Plutarch / Porphyry** — modern utilitarian (pro-vegetarian) and the rare ancient European thinkers who argued about meat — a foil for India's early, central engagement with the question.

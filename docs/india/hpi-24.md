> **In brief:** Śaṅkara's radical "non-dual" Vedānta holds that the everyday world is an illusion (māyā/avidyā) masking the single, formless reality of brahman, and that liberation comes from knowing one's self (ātman) to be identical with it. The episode lays out his scriptural method, his attack on the Buddhists, the charge of inconsistency levelled by his critic Bhāskara, and a sympathetic reading that turns his apparent self-contradictions into stages on the path.

## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **Śaṅkara** — **8th-c. CE** commentator, founder of Advaita (non-dual) Vedānta; the world is illusion, brahman alone is real [00:01:06].
- **Śabara** — the great commentator on Pūrva Mīmāṃsā, defender of common sense, set up as Śaṅkara's foil [00:01:06].
- **The Vedānta-Sūtra** of **Bādarāyaṇa** — the text on which Śaṅkara wrote his most celebrated commentary (*bhāṣya*) [00:04:05].
- **Gauḍapāda** — Śaṅkara's "Paramaguru," precursor who already held many Advaita ideas and analogies [00:06:49].
- **Bhāskara** — rival Vedānta commentator, Śaṅkara's near-contemporary critic, who charged him with hypocrisy [00:06:10].

## Two temperaments: leave the world alone, or overturn it
- [00:00:37] Some philosophers want to "leave the world pretty much as they find it," seeking a deeper understanding of reality *as it seems*, trusting everyday experience. Others want to overturn common sense, believing reality is very different from how it appears.
- [00:00:56] These two approaches are usually ascribed to **Aristotle** and **Plato** among the Greeks — but the contrast fits, with at least as much accuracy, the Indian commentators **Śabara** and **Śaṅkara**, the authoritative interpreters of **Pūrva Mīmāṃsā** and **Vedānta**.
- [00:01:14] On the face of it the two belong to a single tradition, devoted to interpreting the earlier and later parts of the **Veda**. But Śabara and the **Mīmāṃsākas** who followed him were "implacable defenders of common sense": the world is as we see it, and always has been. Śaṅkara "couldn't agree less."

## Śaṅkara's astonishing thesis: the world is an illusion
- [00:01:36] For Śaṅkara, the world is an **illusion** that masks the single underlying reality of **brahman**. To accept the reality of the everyday world is **ignorance (avidyā)**. To know the sole reality of brahman, and the identity of the self (**ātman**) with brahman, is to achieve **liberation**.
- [00:01:53] What led him to this view? "Nothing but the authority of Vedānta" — the later parts of the Veda, i.e. the **Upaniṣads**. Śaṅkara never tires of insisting his doctrine is stated plainly in numerous Upaniṣadic passages [00:02:08]: "Brahman is without a before and an after, without an inner and an outer"; "Brahman is this self here, which perceives everything"; "the finest essence here, that constitutes the self of this whole world… that is the truth, that is the self"; "it is brahman alone that extends over this whole universe."
- [00:02:40] Other Vedic thinkers knew these passages too, but it was Śaṅkara who put them at the heart of Vedānta. Before and after him, other Vedāntins accepted that brahman is the single source of all things *yet also* that the world we see is real — for them everyday reality is a **modification of brahman**, which has taken on the forms of the things around us.
- [00:03:04] Śaṅkara denied there could be **two levels of reality**, one apparent and one fundamental. Brahman "is not two": it is single and supreme, unchanging and ineffable. Hence the name of his tradition — **Advaita**, or **non-dual Vedānta**.

## Who Śaṅkara was, and how he wrote
- [00:03:30] One thing about him is utterly familiar: we don't know exactly when he lived. The traditional birth date is **788 AD**, probably a bit late, but he was safely active in the **8th century** — making him a rough contemporary of **Kumārila**, the great exponent of Pūrva Mīmāṃsā.
- [00:03:46] He wrote commentaries on several Upaniṣads and on the **Bhagavad-Gītā**, which he held to encapsulate the essence of the whole Veda. His most celebrated work is his lengthy commentary, or **bhāṣya**, on the **Vedānta-Sūtra** ascribed to **Bādarāyaṇa**.
- [00:04:12] Adamson's joke: you may feel cheated, wading through so much material only to learn it is all unreal except brahman. A shortcut exists — a far briefer introductory work, the **Upadeśasāhasrī**, which encapsulates his teaching. Still, it's worth reading as much Śaṅkara as you can, because he is "great philosophical reading."
- [00:04:35] Unlike the compressed, elliptical Vedānta-Sūtra, Śaṅkara mounts elaborate arguments, often while refuting rival schools. He also has a good line in **analogies** [00:04:47]: the apparent reality of life is like the **foam on the sea**, like a **seashell that seems to be silver**, like a **snake that turns out to be a coil of rope**. To the puzzle of how a single brahman manifests as an illusory world, he answers that the single **moon can appear as two** to someone with double vision. The soul that finally gives up ignorance and accepts brahman is like a **weary carpenter laying down his tools**, or a **tired falcon alighting to rest**.
- [00:05:27] Śaṅkara is also credited with founding **four major centres of Hindu worship**, taking a leaf from **Buddhist monastic practice**; the most famous is at **Śṛṅgeri**, where a temple still stands. All this earned him a leading place in the history of Indian religion and philosophy.

## Advaita as only one strand — and early critics
- [00:05:48] We should remember Advaita was only one sub-school within the larger Vedānta tradition. Centuries later a rival approach emerged, pointedly called **Dvaita** ("dual," as opposed to non-dual) Vedānta.
- [00:06:05] Already within a generation or two, Śaṅkara was heavily criticized by other Vedāntins, including a major commentator on the Vedānta-Sūtra named **Bhāskara**. Bhāskara was appalled by Śaṅkara's tendency to abandon the **ritual** side of the Vedic tradition — the inevitable consequence, it seemed to him, of deeming the world of actions and fruits a mere illusion.
- [00:06:32] Yet modern scholars find that, despite Bhāskara's disdain, the two commentaries share much common material — suggesting both drew on a rich earlier Vedānta tradition, now largely lost.
- [00:06:49] We do know of a precursor, **Gauḍapāda**. Many of Śaṅkara's key ideas, and some of those nice analogies, are already in Gauḍapāda. Śaṅkara speaks of him with admiration, calling him **Paramaguru** — possibly "the teacher of Śaṅkara's teacher." But Adamson sets aside the question of how much credit belongs to each; the real questions are *what the doctrine amounts to* and *whether there is any reason to believe it*.

## The method: scripture, not argument
- [00:07:26] On whether there is reason to believe it, Śaṅkara is clear. Just as Pūrva Mīmāṃsā taught that matters of **dharma** are known only from the Veda, so Śaṅkara holds we could never come to know the truth of brahman if left to our own devices. Advaita is grounded in the interpretation of the sacred texts — **śruti** — not in philosophical arguments.
- [00:07:45] Since many rivals (like the **Sāṃkhya** school) also accept Vedic authority, Śaṅkara often refutes them simply by quoting scriptures that fit his view better than theirs. He even complains that Sāṃkhya **makes inferences from experience** of the world when it should appeal to śruti: their empirical approach is wrong-headed, because the world is precisely an illusion masking brahman, so we cannot learn about brahman by observing it.
- [00:08:22] In itself brahman is **formless**, so even with revelation it eludes both thought and speech. The closest we can come to experiencing it is an experience that is **empty, utterly devoid of form** — what happens in **deep, dreamless sleep**.

## Liberation through knowledge alone
- [00:08:41] This may sound mystical and anti-rationalist, but in another sense Śaṅkara is "a philosopher's philosopher." Most ancient Indian thinkers held that even the deepest understanding had to be combined with **long practice**. For Śaṅkara, meditation and ritual may be useful *steps* toward liberation, but ultimately **liberation is secured through knowledge** alone.
- [00:09:04] If you truly understand that your individual self is identical to brahman, you are immediately freed from all suffering — and, for that matter, from all pleasure. This is because brahman is **unchanging**, unaffected by the processes the individual self undergoes through its ignorance.
- [00:09:27] We reach liberation by being slowly **weaned from the individual self**, giving up identifying features such as **caste, family affiliation, and even the desire to perform ritual** — since brahman can never acquire the fruits of rituals. (You can see why a Vedic traditionalist like Bhāskara was annoyed.)

## Is Advaita just nihilism? The pure consciousness reply
- [00:09:55] Our own worry: doesn't Advaita amount to a kind of **nihilism**? If brahman is formless and ineffable, then saying "brahman alone is real" may seem to say the only reality is **nothing at all**.
- [00:10:12] Not quite. Though brahman is not to be confused with the individual self — which Śaṅkara calls the **jīva** — ātman is still recognizably a kind of self: it is, for lack of better terms, **consciousness or awareness**. But a special, **pure** consciousness that is not aware of anything in particular.
- [00:10:34] Back to deep sleep: when asleep there is not pure emptiness, but rather "an act of **seeing without any visible object**." Consciousness is evident to itself even when there is nothing of which it is conscious — the way the **sun is luminous in itself** before it illuminates anything else.
- [00:10:58] A fine distinction — does it matter whether there is *nothing at all* or *nothing but a conscious self conscious of nothing in particular*? To Śaṅkara it makes all the difference, as emerges from his critique of Buddhism.

## The critique of Buddhism — and Śaṅkara's debts to it
- [00:11:09] The critique is remarkable partly because Śaṅkara seems **deeply influenced by Buddhism**, likely absorbing its ideas indirectly via Gauḍapāda. The Advaita rejection of everyday reality looks very Buddhist, as does its faith that liberation follows a proper understanding of the self, and its admission that Vedic ritual becomes irrelevant for the liberated.
- [00:11:39] With his dismissal of caste and family as features of the illusory self, Śaṅkara also seems to hold out liberation to **all people**, as the Buddhists did. This is problematic for the lowest class, the **Śūdras**, since his own authoritative text, the Vedānta-Sūtra, seems to exclude them. Śaṅkara admits they cannot study the Vedas, but offers the prospect of **other forms of learning**.
- [00:12:10] An aside: a legend tells that Śaṅkara once shied from touching a man of mixed caste as impure, and the man **castigated him** — pointing out that Śaṅkara's own Advaita implies such distinctions are irrelevant to the true self.
- [00:12:26] These borrowings did not stop Śaṅkara attacking the Buddhists. He complains the **Buddha taught several inconsistent theories, all false**, perhaps because he was such a spiteful character that he deliberately wanted to confuse people. This "breathtakingly rude" remark shows Śaṅkara felt no sympathy — and that he didn't realize, or didn't care, that the supposedly inconsistent teachings actually belonged to **different rival strands within Buddhism**. Impolite and lacking historical nuance, the critique is nonetheless philosophically astute.

## Against the no-self doctrine: causation and memory
- [00:13:15] Śaṅkara aims at the Buddhist theory that there is **no enduring self** — only momentary features and acts of consciousness, the **skandhas**, which cause one another across time and aggregate into the illusion of a coherent, enduring individual.
- [00:13:33] First argument: it's hard to see how a skandha existing only fleetingly at one moment can cause another skandha at the next — for by the time it should be causing the new one, **it is already gone**. (Śaṅkara here invokes his own, non-Buddhist, assumption that effects depend on causes that exist **simultaneously** with them.)
- [00:14:02] Second, **memory** shows the same individual survives over time: part of what I remember in recalling a past experience is precisely that *it was me* who had it. Remembering an **almond croissant** eaten this morning, I am in no doubt that the person who enjoyed it is the same person I am now.
- [00:14:25] By itself the memory argument isn't sufficient — the Buddhist can reply that the former aggregate only *seems* identical to the present one because of the **causal links** between them. But Śaṅkara has already blocked that move by denying that momentary aggregates can form causal chains at all.

## Against Vijñānavāda — and the charge of hypocrisy
- [00:14:53] A more surprising diatribe targets a **Mahāyāna** teaching called **Vijñānavāda**, which rejects everyday reality as illusion. Śaṅkara dismisses it scornfully: it is futile to deny that **external things cause our experiences**, since this is patently obvious. Experience clearly involves both a **subject and an object**, yet these Buddhists deny both — doubting both the enduring self *and* the real things we perceive.
- [00:15:30] But hang on: how can **Śaṅkara of all people** insist on the reality of the world? Doesn't he, like his Mahāyāna opponent, take the world to be mere illusion? This is exactly the objection made by **Bhāskara**, who accused Śaṅkara of **rank hypocrisy** — insisting on realism against the Buddhists, only to lapse into anti-realism in setting out his own Advaita.
- [00:16:00] The suspicion deepens elsewhere in the commentary. For every passage stressing the **illusory** nature of all but brahman, there is another suggesting brahman **really does produce** the world by taking on form: brahman makes things without any instrument or pre-existing material, like the **spider spinning its web from itself**; brahman is both the **stuff** things are made of and the **agent** that makes them; and there is a distinction between a **higher brahman** (formless) and a **lower brahman** (which takes on forms available to experience, or plays the role of the embodied self). What is the point of all this if the things made, and the individual self, are nothing but illusions?

## Resolving the contradiction: stages on the path
- [00:17:08] A sympathetic reader might take refuge in **history**: perhaps Śaṅkara includes contradictory material because he is **reproducing earlier commentators**, many more realist than Gauḍapāda or himself — so his bhāṣya would be a "microcosm of the Vedānta tradition." One historian wrote that, since Vedānta itself split and absorbed ideas from other schools, its history is "a miniature copy or reflection of the entire history of Indian philosophy."
- [00:17:45] But is Śaṅkara really so thoughtless as to compile disparate material without trying for consistency — especially in the very section where he insults the Buddha for inconsistency? Some scholars think his **own ideas developed** over his career, moving from Gauḍapāda's radicalism toward a more realist understanding.
- [00:18:13] A more satisfying solution extracts a **defensible position** from the text. Remember: we cannot reason our way to brahman; that truth is found only in **śruti**, which the Buddhists reject. So it is entirely consistent for Śaṅkara to say the **Buddhist has no basis for rejecting everyday experience**, even though he himself ultimately will — on the authority of śruti, not argument.
- [00:18:43] Moreover, even once śruti is accepted, abandoning everyday experience is **not the first move in his philosophy but the last**. Along the way it is a useful step to think of brahman as the **underlying cause** of all things, then to realize that **effects are implicitly present in their causes**. An effect is not wholly non-existent before it appears — not like "the **son of a barren woman**," Śaṅkara's example of something that cannot exist at all. If effects were not somehow pre-contained in causes, anything could come from anything — **clay pots from milk**.
- [00:19:28] This account of causation improves on the Buddhist one, but it is "like an **unfired clay pot**, not yet finished." The next realization: effects are **really nothing other than their causes** — pots are just clay in a certain shape. On that basis the forms imposed on the cause can be seen as **unreal**. Śaṅkara calls this **superimposition**: the guise brahman wears is a kind of **mask**, and it is sheer ignorance to mistake the mask for what lies beneath — to confuse a real thing with a property falsely ascribed to it, like the **seashell that looks like silver**.
- [00:20:21] Only after this "arduous and challenging" chain of thought are we ready for the radical conclusions of Advaita. And — crucially — without the prompting of **śruti** we would have no warrant to follow the argument to its end. For all his sophistication, Śaṅkara looks ultimately to the **Upaniṣads** as the sole source of our knowledge of the unchanging consciousness that is brahman.

## Where we go next
- [00:20:56] Having examined the Vedānta-Sūtra and its most famous commentary, we are nearly ready to move to other schools — but first, another body of literature from the context of **Vedic exegesis**. As we saw with **Pāṇini**, the study of Sanskrit grammar often yielded philosophical insight, and in the age of the sūtra grammarians went on producing ever more nuanced linguistic analysis, even as all schools adopted Sanskrit as the language of discourse.
- [00:21:31] One of the most important of these grammarians, **Bhartṛhari**, was apparently also an important member of the Vedānta tradition. Next time: "grammar school" — bring an apple for the teacher.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Śaṅkara** — **8th-c. CE** commentator, founder of Advaita Vedānta; the everyday world is illusion, brahman alone is real, and liberation comes from knowing ātman = brahman.
- **Advaita Vedānta** — "non-dual" Vedānta; denies two levels of reality, holding brahman to be single, unchanging, ineffable.
- **brahman** — the sole ultimate reality; formless, eludes thought and speech; both higher (formless) and lower (form-taking) aspects appear in Śaṅkara's text.
- **ātman / jīva** — ātman is the self, identical with brahman; pure consciousness aware of nothing in particular. The *jīva* is the individual embodied self we must be weaned from.
- **avidyā** — ignorance; accepting the reality of the everyday world.
- **māyā / illusion** — the everyday world as a mask over brahman; illustrated by foam on the sea, a snake that is a coil of rope, a seashell that looks like silver, two moons in double vision.
- **śruti** — the sacred revealed texts (esp. the Upaniṣads); for Śaṅkara the *sole* source of knowledge of brahman, since reason alone cannot reach it.
- **Vedānta-Sūtra** — the text ascribed to **Bādarāyaṇa** on which Śaṅkara wrote his great commentary; compressed and elliptical.
- **bhāṣya** — a commentary; Śaṅkara's bhāṣya on the Vedānta-Sūtra is his most celebrated work.
- **Upadeśasāhasrī** — a brief introductory work encapsulating Śaṅkara's teaching; the "shortcut."
- **Śabara / Pūrva Mīmāṃsā / Mīmāṃsākas** — the commentator and school of common-sense realism, treated as Śaṅkara's foil; the world is as we see it.
- **Gauḍapāda** — Śaṅkara's "Paramaguru" and precursor, source of many Advaita ideas and analogies, more radical than later realist Vedāntins.
- **Bhāskara** — rival Vedānta commentator who attacked Śaṅkara for abandoning ritual and for hypocrisy (realism against Buddhists, anti-realism in his own view).
- **Dvaita** — the later "dual" Vedānta, named in contrast to Advaita.
- **skandhas** — the Buddhist momentary aggregates of consciousness that supposedly create the illusion of an enduring self; Śaṅkara refutes them via causation and memory.
- **Vijñānavāda** — the Mahāyāna teaching rejecting external reality, which Śaṅkara attacks for denying both subject and object.
- **superimposition** — Śaṅkara's term for mistaking the form (mask) for the underlying real cause; confusing a thing with a property falsely ascribed to it.
- **Bhartṛhari** — Sanskrit grammarian, also said to belong to the Vedānta tradition; subject of the next episode.

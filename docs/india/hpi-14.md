## Thinkers & texts introduced
- **The Bhagavad-Gītā** — "Song of the Lord," the section of the *Mahābhārata* in which Kṛṣṇa counsels Arjuna; a many-authored text read here as philosophy, not mere plot device [00:00:32].
- **Arjuna & Kṛṣṇa** — the doubting warrior-prince and his charioteer, secretly the supreme god [00:02:25].
- **Sāṃkhya philosophy** — the nature/observer (*prakṛti* / *puruṣa*) framework whose anticipations the Gītā contains; its central text is **Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā*** [00:14:09].
- **Yoga** — spiritual discipline already intertwined with Sāṃkhya in the Gītā, made another name for the path of unattached action [00:16:05].

## A text for all time — and its political charge
- [00:00:40] The episode opens with the words of BJP Minister Sushma Swaraj, who urged everyone to read two couplets of the Gītā daily (700 couplets, finishable in a year) and proposed declaring it India's **national book**. Adamson notes this was "not an entirely innocuous suggestion": officially enshrining a **Hindu** classic sends a "none too subtle message" to non-Hindus.
- [00:01:18] But you needn't be a Hindu nationalist to admire it. **Gandhi** memorized parts and drew from it inspiration for non-violent resistance; **Aldous Huxley** found in it a source for his "perennial philosophy"; the American writers **Thoreau** and **Emerson** admired it greatly.
- [00:01:40] At the other end of the spectrum, **Nazi-era Indologists** in Germany read the Gītā as evidence of a "pure monotheism untainted by supposedly Semitic ideas," and **Robert Oppenheimer** quoted its line "Now I am become death, the destroyer of worlds" about his work on the atomic bomb. As Adamson quips, when it comes to a sentiment for any occasion, "no greeting card company can compete with the Gītā."

## Arjuna's dilemma: a genuinely philosophical doubt
- [00:02:12] The universal feeling that this is a text "for all time and all people" is surprising, given how few of us must decide whether to "unleash devastating war against several dozen siblings." That is the choice facing the hero **Arjuna** in the *Mahābhārata*.
- [00:02:31] The action — "or perhaps we should say the *inaction*" — opens on the brink of the final battle between the **Pāṇḍavas** and **Kauravas**. Arjuna, the greatest Pāṇḍava warrior, brings both his chariot and the entire epic's narrative "to a grinding halt because he is beset by doubt."
- [00:02:52] His dilemma is a conflict of **dharma**. As a **kṣatriya** (elite warrior) — a prince, son of the god **Indra**, a fabled archer whose bow has its own name — his birth was greeted not with cuddly toys but with the prediction that "the fire god will be fully sated with the fat of all the creatures in the Khāṇḍava forest." Yet he is reluctant to kill his own kinsmen. He feels two pulls: the **kṣatriya-dharma** (law of the warrior) and the **kula-dharma** (law of kinship).
- [00:03:44] His proposed answer: do nothing — give up the warrior's life entirely and become a **renouncer**, the same temptation voiced last episode by his elder brother **Yudhiṣṭhira**.
- [00:04:02] Crucially, Arjuna's problem is "distinctively philosophical": it is not weakness of will (knowing the right thing but lacking courage) but **genuine puzzlement about his duty**. Fortunately he has a resource unavailable to the average moral philosopher — "God is, quite literally, his co-pilot." His charioteer **Kṛṣṇa** is an incarnation of the highest divinity, creator of the universe, and the speaker of most of what follows; hence the title **Bhagavad-Gītā**, "Song of the Lord."

## Svadharma and "many authors, one chorus"
- [00:04:31] Kṛṣṇa sets out an argument urging Arjuna to follow his true path, his **svadharma** — the dharma uniquely appropriate to him. "It is even better to carry out one's own path badly than another's path well," and Arjuna "was born for this occasion." As **Bimal Matilal** puts it, "the entire Bhārata war was for Arjuna. It was his game and he should play."
- [00:05:04] Is the Gītā then just a plot device to restart the stalled narrative after letting Arjuna air his scruples? "Hardly." Its authors seize the chance to explore the deeper question of **why any of us should ever undertake any action at all**.
- [00:05:17] "Authors, not author, advisedly": scholars hold that the Gītā, like the whole *Mahābhārata*, was composed by **numerous authors with varying agendas**, situating Arjuna's choice within several different philosophical and religious contexts. The result is that **Kṛṣṇa becomes a kind of chorus** — a single voice expressing numerous viewpoints that may or may not finally fit together into one unified basis for action.

## First moves: shame, reincarnation, and the indifference to outcomes
- [00:06:01] Kṛṣṇa begins with the most obvious tactic: he tells Arjuna to stop being a wimp — "actually, to stop acting like a eunuch." Refusing to fight will earn only **shame**. This is a direct appeal to kṣatriya-dharma, but it cannot suffice, since it ignores his worry about kula-dharma.
- [00:06:26] So Kṛṣṇa appeals to **reincarnation**: the relatives Arjuna may kill — and Arjuna himself, if slain — will all be reborn. Discarding bodies is "no more dramatic than changing our clothes." The **true self is invulnerable**: "swords do not cut him, fire does not burn him, water does not wet him, wind does not parch him."
- [00:06:53] Having lowered the stakes, Kṛṣṇa adds that the **outcome** of fighting should be irrelevant to a true kṣatriya, who must fight bravely regardless of result. Here he plants the seed that blossoms across the rest of the Gītā: **no one, warrior or peasant, should act with an eye to the fruits of their action**.

## The core problem: why act at all? — unattached action vs. renunciation
- [00:07:13] This idea of **unattached action** was touched on in episode six on **karma**, but it raises Arjuna's own objection: if the fruits of action are nothing to me, why act at all? Why not "disengage entirely and become a renouncer"?
- [00:07:37] That solution was adopted "in spectacular style" by ascetics like **Gosāla** and his **Ājīvaka** movement, and by the early **Jainas**. But Kṛṣṇa gives it short shrift: **complete inaction is simply impossible** — "no one lives even a moment without doing some act, for the forces of nature cause everyone to act." And "no one who has a body can renounce all acts completely; he is the true renouncer who renounces the *fruits* of his acts."
- [00:08:08] Kṛṣṇa adds that **inaction too has fruits** — our "sins of omission." If you watch a child be hit by a truck and do nothing, it's no defense to tell the parents you're "in the action-renouncing business."
- [00:08:37] Other passages, though, concede that deliberate inaction is at least a **stage on the right path** — just a less advanced one than unattached action. Kṛṣṇa distinguishes the simple-minded strategy of **mere relinquishment** from the **genuine renunciation** that consists in acting without regard to results.
- [00:09:01] Here the Gītā offers a real innovation (also found in some presumably contemporary **Upaniṣads**), and like the Upaniṣads it coins striking metaphors: the true renouncer is **like a tortoise** — "as the tortoise draws in its limbs, so the renouncer withdraws even from his senses and focuses on his interior insight."

## "All this is strung on me": devotional theism
- [00:09:33] But Arjuna might still ask: toward *what* should interior insight be directed? Kṛṣṇa's answer is simple — **"Think of me."** "I am the origin of this entire universe and its dissolution… all this is strung on me, as strings of pearls are strung on a string." (The episode's title image.)
- [00:09:56] This is the core teaching of the Gītā — "or at least of some of its authors." It justifies action through a **devotional theism** that does not abandon a plurality of gods but insists one highest god is the true object of all worship — close to later Upaniṣads like the **Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad**, which likewise makes a single god the cause of all things.
- [00:10:27] Also Upaniṣadic is the Gītā's interest in **sacrifice**, which it frequently approves ("the world is not of him who fails to sacrifice"). The authors had reason to be nervous: the **Brahmins** "offered more fruits than a greengrocer," promising that correct sacrifices bring wealth, family, cattle and a good rebirth. Like the **Buddhists** and other dissidents, the Gītā distances itself from this **"cash cow" view of ritual** — yet urges us to keep sacrificing anyway, since ritual sacrifice becomes the **paradigmatic approved action**, one we must undertake "with our minds set upon God."

## War as sacrifice, devotion open to all, and keeping the wheel rolling
- [00:11:17] So Kṛṣṇa's exhortation is not merely to fight but to **wage war as a kind of sacrifice** — an action expressing nothing but devotion to him. It may be this idea of **devotion** that has made the text so meaningful across the centuries.
- [00:11:35] You need not be a mighty warrior to devote your life to God: the Gītā explicitly upends the stratifications of Vedic culture, with Kṛṣṇa promising that even **women and the low-born śūdras** can be **liberated through sincere devotion** to him.
- [00:11:47] The point is not just to direct our thoughts to Kṛṣṇa as we act, but to take him as our **model**: as he created the world and continues to hold it together, so we should act to **maintain cosmic harmony** — to "keep the wheel rolling." It is *this*, not any fruits accruing to us, that motivates the true renouncer.

## Fatalism: "I am time, grown old to destroy the world"
- [00:12:22] One could hardly blame Arjuna for wondering how his going to war helps hold the world together. Here the **narrative context merges with the philosophical thrust**: Arjuna is simply *there to fight*, and the story can only resume once he resolves to do so. The war — "spoiler alert, it's going to be very destructive indeed" — is **destined to take place**.
- [00:12:55] The Gītā thus fits the *Mahābhārata*'s recurring suggestion that its events were **foreordained**. The theistic setting deepens this **fatalism**: part of Kṛṣṇa's divine role is to ensure things unfold as they should; from his lofty vantage the future is already present — he knows the events "will, and indeed must, occur."
- [00:13:18] This is perhaps the sense of Kṛṣṇa's saying that he is **"time, grown old to destroy the world"** — an alternative translation of the line Oppenheimer quoted. One might say Kṛṣṇa is not so much persuading Arjuna to fight as **informing him that he is going to fight**, so he may as well get on with it: "fettered by your own task which springs from your nature, you will inevitably do what you in your folly do not want to do."

## The Sāṃkhya strand: prakṛti and puruṣa
- [00:14:00] The religious teachings — divine plan, sacrifice — can make the Gītā seem "like an Upaniṣad inserted into the epic." But scholars detect **another strand on which its pearls are strung: Sāṃkhya philosophy**.
- [00:14:16] Dating is uncertain — "educated guesses" place the Gītā's composition in stages between the **4th century BC and the 4th century AD** — but it must **predate** the central Sāṃkhya text, **Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā***, which itself predates its **6th-century** Chinese translation.
- [00:14:50] The most striking anticipation is the fundamental contrast between two principles, **prakṛti** and **puruṣa** — roughly **nature** and **observer mind**. Non-attached action can be restated in these terms: like the tortoise, the renouncer **withdraws from prakṛti (nature)** and focuses on his true self, his **puruṣa (observer mind)**.
- [00:15:22] The Gītā describes how the two interrelate: both are **beginningless**; prakṛti is a cause "as the agency and production of products," puruṣa a cause "in that he experiences happiness and unhappiness." The puruṣa is the **purely observing self**, unchanging despite its connection with body and, through body, with all of nature.
- [00:15:52] So the Gītā can be seen **responding to Buddhism by reasserting an enduring self** — a notion already present in earlier Upaniṣads.

## Yoga: the discipline of the unattached self
- [00:16:05] But "all-seeing Kṛṣṇa" looks forward too. The later Sāṃkhya movement will intertwine with the tradition of **yoga**, and that intertwining is already in the Gītā (and elsewhere in the *Mahābhārata*). The Gītā frequently treats **yoga as a path toward enlightenment**, implying the would-be sage needs **training and spiritual discipline**, not just knowledge of the nature/self contrast.
- [00:16:36] Yoga thus becomes **another word for the path of unattached action**, and the **yogin** is synonymous with Kṛṣṇa's true renouncer — superior to those who merely embrace asceticism and refuse to act. Through **Sāṃkhya's insight** Arjuna learns about his **puruṣa / brahman**, a true self transcending nature; through the **practice of yoga** he learns to see the world solely from that self's vantage. Since actions and consequences unfold in the realm of nature (prakṛti), **they cannot touch him**.

## Royal wisdom — and the abiding theme of violence
- [00:17:14] One final implication, not lost on original readers: though the Gītā's message is for everyone, Arjuna *is* a kṣatriya prince, and the text has a special message for **elite readers**. Kṛṣṇa calls his teaching a **Rāja-Vidyā** — "royal wisdom" or "knowledge of kings." The king is "uniquely appropriate" for imitating the **divine king** through his actions.
- [00:17:57] Arjuna emerges as an **ideal ruler** who acts "because he ought to, and out of devotion to the highest lord, rather than in pursuit of his own advantage." One can imagine the real-life king **Aśoka** nodding in approval.
- [00:18:13] The epic supplies a **negative example**: the Kaurava leader **Duryodhana**, whose most heinous sin is putting himself and his own interests above all else "rather than submitting to the divine rule of Kṛṣṇa."
- [00:18:33] Few of us face Arjuna's (or Duryodhana's) exact situation, but "in a broader sense we all do." Warfare is a common human experience, and commoner still is the **opportunity to do violence** to humans and animals.
- [00:18:52] **Violence is an abiding theme** of the *Mahābhārata*, which depicts both Arjuna's hesitation and the "horrendous devastation" of the battle. It may seem strange that Kṛṣṇa urges him on — but consider that Kṛṣṇa also endorses **animal sacrifice**: apparently "some blood must be spilled if the wheel of the world is to keep turning."
- [00:19:14] Yet other ancient movements opposed such sacrifice — notably the **Jainas**, who sought to avoid all violence, intentional or unintentional. This commitment to **non-violence** is a legacy carried from antiquity to modern times, in India (Gandhi) and across the world; many non-Indian vegetarians take inspiration — "and recipes" — from Indian culture. Next time, Adamson signs off, "we give peace a chance."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Bhagavad-Gītā ("Song of the Lord")** — the *Mahābhārata* section of Kṛṣṇa's counsel to Arjuna; a many-authored text reading Arjuna's dilemma through several philosophical and religious lenses.
- **Arjuna** — greatest Pāṇḍava warrior, kṣatriya prince and son of Indra; halts the epic in doubt over killing his kinsmen.
- **Kṛṣṇa** — Arjuna's charioteer, incarnation of the supreme creator-god; speaks the Gītā as a "chorus" of many viewpoints.
- **dharma (kṣatriya- vs. kula-dharma)** — duty/law; Arjuna's conflict is between the warrior's law and the law of kinship.
- **svadharma** — one's *own* uniquely appropriate duty; better done badly than another's done well.
- **renouncer / renunciation** — the one who gives up not action (impossible) but the **fruits** of action; distinguished from "mere relinquishment."
- **unattached action** — the Gītā's central doctrine: act without craving the results; restated via prakṛti/puruṣa and as yoga.
- **tortoise image** — the renouncer withdrawing his senses as a tortoise draws in its limbs.
- **devotion (bhakti) / "all strung on me"** — devotional theism making one highest god the object of worship and the motive for action.
- **sacrifice** — approved as the paradigmatic action done "with minds set on God," even as the Gītā rejects the Brahmins' "cash cow" ritual bargain.
- **Rāja-Vidyā** — "royal wisdom / knowledge of kings"; the Gītā's special message to elite rulers who imitate the divine king.
- **prakṛti / puruṣa** — Sāṃkhya's "nature" and "observer mind"; the renouncer withdraws from prakṛti into his unchanging puruṣa (true self).
- **Sāṃkhya / Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā*** — the nature/self philosophy anticipated by the Gītā; its central text (pre-**6th c.**), covered in a future episode.
- **yoga / yogin** — spiritual discipline equated with the path of unattached action; the yogin *is* the true renouncer.
- **fatalism / "I am time"** — the foreordained war and Kṛṣṇa's knowledge of the necessary future ("time, grown old to destroy the world").
- **Jainas / non-violence** — opponents of sacrifice who sought to avoid all violence; antiquity's legacy of *ahiṃsā* down to Gandhi.

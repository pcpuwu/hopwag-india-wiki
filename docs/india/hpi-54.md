> **Speakers:** Peter Adamson with Graham Priest · **Runtime:** 00:46:28
> **In brief:** Graham Priest (Distinguished Professor of Philosophy, City University of New York Graduate Center) explains how the same "non-classical" logics that Western logicians built in the 20th century — many-valued and paraconsistent systems that reject excluded middle or non-contradiction — turn out to capture what the Buddhists' catuṣkoṭi and Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka were already doing. The real philosophical payload of Buddhist "logic" lies not in its epistemology but in its metaphysics, which can make true contradictions look respectable.

## How Priest came to Buddhist logic
- [00:00:28] Adamson introduces **Graham Priest**, Distinguished Professor of Philosophy at the **City University of New York Graduate Center**, famous for his work in contemporary logic, who later "turned his attention to the resources that Buddhism has to offer in logic."
- [00:01:04] A bit of autobiography: Priest trained as a **mathematician** (his doctorate is in maths), but by the end of it he'd decided philosophy was "an awful lot more fun." He was hired into a philosophy department knowing "virtually no philosophy," and has spent his career teaching himself the subject.
- [00:01:31] After ~20 years he'd absorbed a lot of **Western** philosophy but little Eastern thought — "in the Western academy, no one really knows much about it." The turning point was meeting **Jay Garfield**, who became a close friend (Garfield then in Tasmania, Priest in Brisbane — far apart, but Australia's philosophical community is small).
- [00:02:18] Garfield, also trained as a Western analytic philosopher, had moved into the Asian traditions: he taught himself **Tibetan** and produced one of the best translations of the **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā** — the most important text by **Nāgārjuna** (1st–2nd century CE; Priest notes the exact dates are unknown), arguably the most important philosophical text in Buddhism. Adamson mentions he'd just read that translation while writing scripts for the series.
- [00:02:47] Priest had just finished his own book *Beyond the Limits of Thought*; comparing notes with Garfield, he realised many of Garfield's preoccupations overlapped with his own. Talking to Garfield made him see "I knew at best half the world's philosophy" — he knew nothing of China or India. So he set out to teach himself, partly by **teaching** ("you can't teach it unless you get your head around it"; Adamson adds that podcasting works the same way), studying in India and Japan.
- [00:03:58] Adamson probes the motive: was it the **technical resonance** with his logic, or just filling a gap? Priest: there was a real logical resonance, but it wasn't only that — the views on the nature of reality, the ethics, the political philosophy coming out of China, India, Tibet, Japan and Korea are "just fascinating ideas, even without logic connection." He wasn't "mining" Buddhism for logic material; he was after whatever was interesting.

## What "non-classical logic" is
- [00:05:35] Before the Buddhist material, Adamson asks Priest to explain **non-classical logic**. Priest's first provocation: "**classical logic** is something of a misnomer" — it has nothing to do with the classical civilisations (Egypt, Greece, Rome). It was essentially **invented at the end of the 19th century**.
- [00:06:03] The key figure was **Gottlob Frege**, who initially didn't get the credit he deserved. When the new ideas spread through Europe, North America and Australia, the credit went first to **Russell** (and sometimes **Wittgenstein**); only by the **mid-1950s** did people recognise the debt to Frege — even though Russell had openly drawn on him. Readers went to Russell's *Principia* (**1910**) rather than back to Frege, partly because **Frege's notation is terrible** and Russell's symbolism looked more familiar to mathematicians.
- [00:07:06] "Classical logic" is therefore "a masterstroke of propaganda" — the name makes it sound ancient when it isn't. Logic *has* developed in the West for two and a half thousand years; logic, as modern logicians understand it, is "**a theory about what follows from what**" — given some claims, what can you infer? Many different theories of that have been proposed from ancient Greece, through the great medieval period, to the present.
- [00:07:48] Priest's gripe: logic today is taught **ahistorically** — you're handed a logic book, told "this is logic" (usually some classical system), as if it "dropped from the sky," like Moses bringing tablets down from Sinai. It's taught more like physics than ethics — yet even physics students know physics has a history, while most logic students don't know logic has one.
- [00:08:23] Set the gripe aside: classical logic was "a fantastic advance," so much more powerful than anything before it that within 20–30 years it became orthodox, investigated by brilliant mathematicians — **Hilbert** (Göttingen, 1910s–20s) and **Gödel** (Vienna, 1920s–30s, later Princeton). Philosophers then seized it as a tool: roughly **1920–1960**, the heyday of **analytic philosophy** and **logical positivism / empiricism**, was spent applying logic to the philosophy of science and language.

## Two ways to deviate: expressive extensions vs. genuine revision
- [00:09:48] But using a tool reveals its **limits** (here it took 40–50 years). Logicians split: some look for work-arounds within classical logic; others conclude you need to "revamp" logic itself. The result, especially over the last 40 years, is **non-classical logics** — systems that deviate from classical logic — and they fall into two broad kinds.
- [00:10:45] **Kind one — expressive extensions:** thinkers who think classical logic's expressive resources are *too limited*, so they **add** machinery. The paradigm is **modal logic**, which adds tools for possibility and necessity (going back to **C. I. Lewis** in the 1920s).
- [00:11:14] **Kind two — genuine revision:** thinkers who say classical logic is profoundly *wrong*. This starts early, with the Dutch mathematician **Brouwer**, working on **intuitionist logic** around **1910** — roughly contemporary with *Principia Mathematica* — and developed into a full system by another Dutchman, **Heyting**, by the **1920s**.
- [00:11:51] The headline casualty in intuitionism is the **principle of excluded middle**: classically, every statement is either true or false. Intuitionists deny this — there are statements that are *neither* — and build a logic in which "A or not-A" is **not a logical truth**. So non-classical logic's origins sit very close to classical logic's; they were just ignored because classical logic worked so well. By the **1960s** logicians stopped ignoring them ("like a pair of shoes that bite — you live with it a while, then think maybe I can do better with a new pair").

## Can a meaningful statement be *neither* true nor false?
- [00:13:01] Adamson presses: does the classical logician really resist the idea of a statement that is neither true nor false? It seems an obvious observation — they discussed it in the **Nāgārjuna** episodes under **category mistakes**: "How heavy is the colour blue?" has no good answer because the question makes a false assumption (that colours have weight). Couldn't a classical logician just say: be precise, factor out category mistakes, and the problem vanishes?
- [00:13:39] Priest grants it: some logicians do propose a third value ("category mistake") alongside true and false, but most classical logicians simply assume a cleaned-up language with the category mistakes "factored out in the first place." So category mistakes have **not** been a tough challenge for classical logic. The tougher challenges lie elsewhere.
- [00:14:15] The earliest serious challenge was **intuitionism**, driven by the **philosophy of mathematics**. **Brouwer** asked: what *is* truth in mathematics? Truth about the physical world looks fixed by a world "out there" (contentious, but at least defensible). For mathematics, one option is a Platonic mathematical world out there — but to some that's "just a kind of **mysticism**."
- [00:15:08] If mathematical truth isn't constituted by an external reality, what constitutes it? Brouwer's answer: **provability** — "to be true in mathematics is to be provable." But there are statements you can prove neither true nor false; in those cases, said Brouwer, you **cannot apply excluded middle**, at least until you have a proof one way or the other.
- [00:15:34] Adamson: can't you sometimes prove that you can't prove *either* A or its negation? Priest: yes, within a given formal system — that's a standard reading of **Gödel's** great theorems. But Brouwer meant **provability period**, not relative to any one system; he had "a very jaundiced view of formal systems," holding that proof is about **mental constructions** that may not be captured by any single formalism.
- [00:16:12] So intuitionism is an early challenge resting on a **verificationist** theory of truth. Classical logic stayed orthodox until about the 1960s, then people began to worry; it's probably still orthodox, especially in philosophy, but "we're not as sure that the orthodox view is right as we were 50 years ago." Logic is in an exciting state.

## Can a statement be *both* true and false? Paraconsistency and dialetheism
- [00:23:37] Turning to the **third corner** — both true and false — Priest gives more history. The issue here is the **principle of non-contradiction**, which rules out something being both true and false. It has been "high orthodoxy" in Western logic since **Aristotle** — *more* entrenched than excluded middle.
- [00:24:12] Aristotle defends non-contradiction at length in the *Metaphysics*, but "most modern commentators think these arguments really don't work" — they're unclear and several "beg the question." Still, Aristotle names his targets (**Heraclitus**, **Protagoras**), so he thought there was a real fight, and his arguments — good or not — secured the principle a place of high orthodoxy. Hardly anyone has bothered to *defend* it at length since, because virtually every Western philosopher simply **accepted** it.
- [00:25:12] The important exception is **Hegel**. Interpreting Hegel is "a dangerous business," and there are attempts to "consistentize" him — but read with an open mind, Hegel holds that **some contradictions are true**. The modern name for that view is **dialetheism**; a **dialetheia** is something true *and* false.
- [00:25:50] Priest's example from Hegel's *Logic*: **motion**. To be in motion is not to be on the right at one time and the left at another, but "to be here and not here, in one and the same place, at one and the same time" — motion itself "realises contradictory states of affairs" (Hegel then draws on Zeno's paradoxes, e.g. the arrow). Hegel is the great exception precisely because he's a major figure; that there are so few others shows how orthodox non-contradiction has been. (Adamson notes the series hasn't reached Hegel "with some trepidation.")
- [00:26:42] Around the **1960s**, contemporary logicians built systems where what fails is **not** excluded middle but **non-contradiction**: **paraconsistent logics**. In classical logic, once you have a single contradiction "all hell breaks loose" — a contradiction implies *everything* (so one contradiction gives you everything). The **defining feature** of paraconsistent logic is that this is **not** the case: a contradiction stays constrained, limited. We now understand these systems well.
- [00:27:44] If a logic can tolerate contradictions, you start to wonder whether some contradictions are actually **true**. Priest's promised example: the **paradoxes of self-reference**, which became central to the foundations of mathematics around 1900 (**Russell's paradox** "crashed Frege's program" — "we're not playing games here").
- [00:28:31] The oldest of these is the **liar paradox**, traced to the **5th century BCE** Greek thinker **Eubulides**. Take "**what I am now telling you is false**." If it's true, then (since it says it's false) it's false; if it's false, then it's true. So *prima facie* the liar sentence is **both true and false** — a candidate dialetheia. Such paradoxes have been argued "ad nauseam" across the three great periods of logic (ancient, medieval, modern), with **still no consensus** on what, if anything, goes wrong. The dialetheist's gambit: maybe nothing is wrong with the argument — maybe it's a "radical argument for something that's true," and the truth is a contradiction.

## Did the ancient Buddhists have a "logic"?
- [00:17:36] Adamson asks how fair it is to ascribe a *logic* to ancient Buddhists, who weren't doing what Russell does in *Principia*. Priest: they certainly weren't doing anything recognisable as contemporary formal logic — "things are much more complicated and nuanced."
- [00:18:02] The great Buddhist "logicians," **Dignāga** and **Dharmakīrti**, you "wouldn't really want to call logicians in the modern sense." They used the word *logic* to mean something like **epistemology** — concerned with the **sources of knowledge** (perception, testimony, inference). They do engage with **inference**, and they formulate a formal logic "a bit like Aristotelian syllogistic," but at roughly that level of power or less.
- [00:18:55] So for Priest the contemporary interest does **not** lie in Buddhist theories of inference (their epistemology is interesting in its own right, but the inference theories won't excite a modern logician). The interest lies "**elsewhere, and a lot further back**."
- [00:19:14] It's hard to draw the line between **logic and metaphysics**. In the old positivist era, people thought logic was *metaphysically neutral* — "that is almost certainly false," and most philosophers now agree. (Intuitionist logic is obviously not neutral — it's bound up with the nature of mathematical and physical reality.) Probe almost any system of logic and you find **metaphysical underpinnings**. The Buddhists and Jains are interesting because they bring **very different** metaphysical underpinnings — "it's not in the epistemology that you find things interesting for modern logic; it's in the **metaphysics**."

## The catuṣkoṭi (tetralemma) — and the Jains' seven
- [00:20:20] In Buddhism you get the **catuṣkoṭi**, literally "**four corners**" — four possible answers to a question. Priest's canonical case: someone asks the Buddha what happens to an **enlightened person after death** — do they exist? not exist? both exist and not exist? neither exist nor not exist? Adamson notes this is what the series earlier called the **tetralemma**; Priest confirms.
- [00:21:00] The **Buddha refuses to answer yes to any** of the four ("that's another story") — but crucially, all four are treated as genuinely "on the cards." Their picture of reality **allows** these four possibilities. In the West, "at least until recent times," only **two** are on the cards — **yes and no**; you can't have both, you can't have neither. (A wrinkle: Aristotle does suggest "neither" about future contingents.)
- [00:21:48] These thinkers never "worked it up into the rigour of a modern logical system" — but we now know exactly how to, thanks to contemporary non-classical logic, where there are logics with precisely these four possibilities.
- [00:22:05] "The **Jains** are even cooler." Where the Buddhists of that period had four possibilities, the Jains have **seven** — the **saptabhaṅgī** (seven answers). Why seven? Because it's **2³ − 1**. This is bound up with the Jain **metaphysics of aspects**: reality has many aspects, and a thing can hold one aspect and not another, so you must take all aspects into account — "a long story, but that's how you get seven."

## Nāgārjuna's use of the four corners
- [00:22:48] Adamson returns to the third corner ("both") as the clearest way to see why this is a *different* way of doing logic. The "neither" corner is easy to swallow (category mistakes; or, on a provability theory of truth, statements neither provably true nor provably false; or Aristotle's undecided future — "the battle will happen tomorrow"). But why would it ever be useful to call a meaningful proposition **both true and false**? (Priest answers this with the liar paradox above.)
- [00:30:25] Adamson observes that the great wielder of the tetralemma is **Nāgārjuna**, who usually deploys it to lay out four options *all of which he refutes* — e.g. on motion or causation: self-caused, caused by another, both, neither. Strikingly, when he reaches the **third (contradiction) option**, he doesn't dismiss it as "obviously silly"; he gives a **detailed argument** against it.
- [00:31:22] Why? Because Nāgārjuna is heir to **700 years** of Buddhist tradition: the tetralemma is **canonical Buddhism**, "it's in the sūtras." A good Buddhist won't say "the Buddha was crazy." Just as the Buddha *refused to answer* the disciple's question rather than dismissing "both exist and not exist" as ruled out by non-contradiction, Nāgārjuna stays faithful to the tradition by taking the contradictory corner seriously.
- [00:32:11] But Priest pushes back on Adamson's summary: Nāgārjuna deploys the catuṣkoṭi in a **novel** way. Nāgārjuna is the foundational philosopher of **Madhyamaka**, one of the two kinds of Indian **Mahāyāna** Buddhist philosophy, whose central metaphysical idea is that **everything is empty**.
- [00:32:56] "Empty" most certainly does **not** mean non-existent — it's like a glass being **empty of beer**. Empty *of what?* Of **svabhāva** — literally "**self-being**," translatable as **intrinsic nature**. Early Buddhism (the first ~500 years) held that ultimate reality is composed of building blocks called **dharmas** (not atoms in the modern sense), and that these dharmas *have* intrinsic nature. With Madhyamaka, **Nāgārjuna launches a severe attack** on that older metaphysics — one of the great turning points in Buddhist philosophy.
- [00:34:08] How does the catuṣkoṭi feature in the attack? Many chapters of the **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā** perform a **reductio** on the claim that something has svabhāva. Take the **causation** chapter (the MMK's first): there's a tacit, unspoken assumption that causation *has* svabhāva, an intrinsic nature. If it does, there are **four possibilities** about it (true, false, both, neither) — and Nāgārjuna reduces each of the four to absurdity.
- [00:35:04] It's "a pretty standard reductio": assume something, show the possible cases, show none works. In the West there'd be only **two** cases (true, false); because Nāgārjuna works within the **catuṣkoṭi**, he has **four**, and must dispose of all of them. The ultimate aim is a reductio on the assumption that something has svabhāva.

## The fifth possibility: the ineffable and the paradox of expressibility
- [00:35:46] Adamson notes Nāgārjuna at least *entertains* the "both" option before rejecting it, and asks whether there are places where ancient Buddhists genuinely **assert** a contradiction, reasoning like dialetheists.
- [00:36:09] In the MMK's reductios he runs the four cases and rejects all four — but he's rejecting the **initial assumption**, so that alone doesn't establish a fifth possibility. **However**, elsewhere in the text Nāgārjuna does suggest a **fifth possibility** — cases where *none* of the four applies — and not in a reductio context: he's putting forward a positive view. Here interpretation gets much more contentious among scholars.
- [00:37:05] Priest's natural reading: it's standard in Buddhism that there are **two kinds of reality** — an **ultimate** reality we're normally unaware of, and a **conventional** reality we are aware of. In the older tradition ultimate reality was composed of **dharmas**. You might expect Nāgārjuna, having attacked the dharmas, to abolish ultimate reality — but he **doesn't**, because the sūtras of his time talk about ultimate reality and he must stay faithful to them.
- [00:37:42] So what is ultimate reality for him? The view that evolves over the next couple of centuries: reality is **what remains when you strip away the conceptual overlay**. Take a chair and peel off its blueness, its four-leggedness, its chair-ness — all concepts we apply — and what remains, before any conceptual overlay, is ultimate reality.
- [00:38:21] But then: what is ultimate reality *like*? You can't say — **any answer applies concepts**. Can't you at least call it **empty**? No — "emptiness" is itself a concept. So ultimate reality is **ineffable**, and *that* is the **fifth possibility**: true, false, both, neither — and **ineffable**.
- [00:38:50] "Now we're facing real problems." If it's ineffable, aren't you **talking about it** right now? Yes. Isn't that paradoxical? Yes. Madhyamaka seems to have a genuine **paradox**, and Buddhist scholars respond to it in different ways — "in exactly the same way that Western logicians have responded to the **liar paradox**." Priest's own inclination: take the contradiction at face value — accept that **some of the things that are ineffable, you can nonetheless talk about**.
- [00:39:39] This is what reconnected Priest with **Jay Garfield**: Garfield arrived at it via his Nāgārjuna translation, Priest via contemporary logic, and they recognised a shared phenomenon worth working on.
- [00:40:04] The liar isn't about ineffability, but there are **paradoxes in the same family** that are — arising with **large infinities**, where some things are "so large you can't describe them": there are more things in infinity than you can possibly describe, yet you can *point* to specific such things via a standard set-theoretic construction. This is **König's paradox** (after **Julius König**, a Hungarian mathematician/logician, ~1910–20s). So paradoxes of ineffability "look as though they turn up in modern logic as well" — Priest had paradoxes of ineffability, Garfield had what looked like the same, and they began thinking about them together, producing a number of joint papers.

## Does the influence run both ways?
- [00:40:26] Adamson's last question: is this using the **ancient Buddhist tradition** to find resources for contemporary logic, or using the **tools of non-classical logic** to understand Nāgārjuna (which is what Priest's papers — "lots of symbols and mathematical logic" next to svabhāva theory — actually do)?
- [00:42:07] "**It goes both ways.**" The history of philosophy matters for contemporary philosophy because there have been many great philosophers — "most of them wrong about most things; that's philosophy after all" — yet all had genuine points of insight. We can "**mine** the history of philosophy for insights to learn anew," just as the West has repeatedly mined Plato, Kant or Hegel — and the same goes for the Asian traditions.
- [00:42:59] The metaphysical insights of **Madhyamaka** are "profound," and applying them "you naturally come up with something like a **four-valued logic, even a five-valued logic**." This shows that when modern logicians built such systems they weren't merely "playing games" / "squiggling symbols like chess." Since every logic has metaphysical underpinnings, the Buddhists and Jains are actually showing us **what some of those underpinnings could be** — they have "something very important to teach us" about the metaphysics behind modern logical systems.
- [00:44:11] The influence runs back the other way too. It would be silly to suppose Nāgārjuna (or Plato) had the tools of modern formal logic — but those tools let us **articulate** their views far more carefully than the thinkers themselves could. It's no criticism: Aristotle lacked a **microscope**, but would have used one brilliantly; modern logic is "the logical equivalent of the microscope."
- [00:45:04] So we can return to the **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā** and reformulate it "with all the clarity and precision of modern logic" — see what it implies, examine its arguments, check how good they are — exactly as we apply modern logic to **Anselm's** and **Descartes'** arguments for God, understanding them better than they could because we have the tools. "The Enlightenment goes both ways": Priest loves taking insights from both modern and historical philosophy, East and West, "to help me understand the other better."
- [00:46:15] Adamson thanks Priest, who calls it a pleasure, and signs off — "join me and **Jonardon Ganeri** next time" — noting the lesson that the history of Indian philosophy "can be very important for contemporary philosophy, and in its own right."

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Graham Priest** — Distinguished Professor of Philosophy, City University of New York Graduate Center; logician (a leading defender of dialetheism) who came to Buddhist thought through Jay Garfield.
- **Jay Garfield** — Western analytic philosopher turned scholar of the Asian traditions; learned Tibetan, made a leading translation of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā; Priest's collaborator on the ineffability papers.
- **Nāgārjuna** — 1st–2nd c. CE founder of Madhyamaka; attacks the older Buddhist metaphysics of svabhāva-bearing dharmas via reductio arguments structured by the catuṣkoṭi.
- **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (MMK)** — Nāgārjuna's central text; its chapters (e.g. the opening one on causation) reduce to absurdity the assumption that things have svabhāva.
- **Madhyamaka** — one of the two Indian Mahāyāna Buddhist schools; its core claim is that everything is "empty."
- **svabhāva** — "self-being" / intrinsic nature; what things are *empty of* in Madhyamaka (emptiness ≠ non-existence — empty "of svabhāva," as a glass is empty of beer).
- **dharmas** — for early Buddhism, the intrinsic-nature-bearing building blocks of ultimate reality, which Nāgārjuna attacks.
- **catuṣkoṭi (tetralemma)** — the "four corners": four answers on the cards for a question (true / false / both / neither), e.g. about the enlightened person after death, which the Buddha refused to answer.
- **the fifth possibility (ineffable)** — Priest's reading of certain MMK passages: beyond true/false/both/neither, ultimate reality (what remains once the conceptual overlay is stripped away) is ineffable — generating a paradox, since one still talks about it.
- **saptabhaṅgī** — the Jains' *seven* answers (2³ − 1), tied to their metaphysics of aspects (reality has many aspects, held under some and not others).
- **Dignāga & Dharmakīrti** — the great Buddhist "logicians," better called epistemologists; "logic" for them meant the study of the sources of knowledge (perception, testimony, inference).
- **classical logic** — a "misnomer"; invented in the late 19th century (Frege; popularised via Russell's *Principia*), a theory of "what follows from what," made orthodox by Hilbert and Gödel.
- **non-classical logic** — systems deviating from classical logic, of two kinds: expressive extensions (e.g. modal logic, C. I. Lewis) and genuine revisions.
- **intuitionist logic** — Brouwer (~1910) and Heyting (1920s); rejects the principle of **excluded middle**, grounded in a verificationist/provability theory of mathematical truth.
- **paraconsistent logic** — 1960s onward; rejects (the explosive form of) the principle of **non-contradiction** so a contradiction no longer implies everything.
- **dialetheism / dialetheia** — the view that some contradictions are true; a dialetheia is a statement both true and false (Hegel on motion; the liar paradox as candidate cases).
- **liar paradox** — "what I am now telling you is false"; traced to Eubulides (5th c. BCE); appears both true and false, a stock example for dialetheists.
- **König's paradox** — a paradox of ineffability from large infinities (Julius König, ~1910s–20s): there are more things in infinity than can be described, yet specific such things can be picked out.

## Who Nāgārjuna was, and his one great text
- [00:00:29] The episode is an interview about the Buddhist philosopher **Nāgārjuna** with **Jan Westerhoff**, introduced as Associate Professor in Religious Ethics at the University of Oxford and a fellow tutoring theology and religion at Lady Margaret Hall, Oxford [00:00:40].
- [00:01:00] Dating: Nāgārjuna probably lived in the **2nd–3rd century CE**, though traditional biographies inflate his lifespan to several hundred years. He was likely active somewhere in southern India (the exact place is unknown) and was most likely a Buddhist monk — that is about as far as the firm data go.
- [00:01:32] His most famous work is the **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā** ("Fundamental Stanzas / Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way"), a text of 450 verses, and most of the material discussed comes from it.
- [00:01:47] The text is closely aligned with the **perfection of wisdom sūtras** (the **prajñāpāramitā** texts): it provides a systematic way of spelling out claims those sūtras make. Westerhoff stresses the link is also mythological [00:02:08] — the perfection-of-wisdom texts were said to have been taught by the historical **Buddha**, then entrusted to underwater serpent spirits called **nāgas**, who guarded them until the time was right to return them. Nāgārjuna's name carries "nāga" precisely because he is the one who brought these texts back from the nāgas to the human world and explicated them [00:02:38]. Adamson notes this is a "rather untraditional way of transmitting philosophical" texts.
- [00:02:54] A concrete textual link: the praise of the Buddha at the very opening of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā is a close paraphrase of a passage in the Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra in 25,000 lines.

## Commentator or independent philosopher?
- [00:03:17] Adamson asks whether Nāgārjuna is essentially a commentator, traceable to root texts, or a freer thinker. Westerhoff: the kārikās are **not a commentary** — they don't gloss an earlier root text, and they name only one sūtra in the whole work [00:03:44].
- [00:03:53] The better description is a **philosophical explication** of specific claims in the perfection of wisdom sūtras. Those sūtras are unusual: they make many philosophical claims but **do not supply the arguments**. So Nāgārjuna can be read as supplying the arguments — especially for the claims about **emptiness** that the prajñāpāramitā sūtras make [00:04:20].
- [00:04:48] He is not a philosopher "doing his own thing": his stated aim, declared at the very start of the text, is to explicate the Buddha's message in the way the prajñāpāramitā sūtras state it. So it is "a commentary" only in the sense of explicating something present across many texts.

## Emptiness and what it rejects: svabhāva
- [00:05:18] His most prominent doctrine is **emptiness (śūnyatā)**, and it is easier to grasp via what it rejects, which goes under the name **svabhāva** — roughly "stable reality."
- [00:05:38] Etymology: svabhāva compounds **sva** ("own") and **bhāva** ("existence/being") — literally "own being," which by itself says little. Western translators have rendered it as **substance**, **essence**, or **intrinsic nature**; none captures the whole, but each gets fairly close.
- [00:06:19] Westerhoff distinguishes **three dimensions** of svabhāva:
  - **Ontological** [00:06:25] — svabhāva as a **ground of being** (closest to "substance"). The Indian commentary literature spells this grounding out several ways: mereological grounding (the most fundamental parts, the atoms), causal grounding (a first cause), and grounding of our conceptualization of reality (some non-conceptual "stuff" our concepts are about). Reject svabhāva and all of this is rejected.
  - **Semantic** [00:07:09] — a view of language on which there is a "ready-made and structured world out there" and a language here, the two related by **structural similarity**. To work "with svabhāva" is for language to structurally match an objectively structured reality [00:07:40]. Example [00:07:56]: if you analyse sentences into agents, actions and objects — what grammarians do — and assume a one-to-one match between grammatical and ontological structure, that is a svabhāva reading of semantics. Westerhoff cites Sanskrit grammar's **kāraka theory** of sentence components [00:08:09].
  - **Cognitive** [00:08:33] — "in a way the most important" — svabhāva as a **cognitive default we automatically project onto the world** whenever we perceive anything. The aim of the Buddhist practice Nāgārjuna's philosophy supports is to **train us out of** this intuitive superimposition.
- [00:09:08] So Nāgārjuna is a philosopher who treats philosophy as a kind of **therapy** — one dimension of the point, Westerhoff agrees.

## Is this skepticism? Selflessness extended to all dharmas
- [00:09:16] Adamson worries the position sounds extremely skeptical — nothing has essence (ontological), language doesn't mirror reality (semantic), thought doesn't latch onto reality (cognitive) — yet many of Nāgārjuna's opponents hold "ambitious metaphysical postulations" anyway, like the unchanging **ātman** of the Vedic tradition. So is Nāgārjuna telling us to abandon **common sense**, or merely not to be a Hindu philosopher [00:10:14]?
- [00:10:23] Westerhoff resists the label "skepticism": the claims are **not exclusively about how we know the world**. Though Nāgārjuna has much to say about epistemology, the enterprise is "at least as ontological as it is epistemological."
- [00:10:58] What Madhyamaka does is **extend the Buddha's idea of selflessness**: not only the selflessness of persons but the **selflessness of all dharmas** — the fundamental phenomena identified by early Buddhist philosophy. So emptiness is not just a rejection of the Hindu **ātman** [00:11:21].
- [00:11:27] It is also a rejection of the entire **Abhidharma** metaphysics, which Westerhoff reads as a kind of **trope theory**: a fundamental level of reality consisting of **momentary tropes** that conglomerate into the "medium-sized dry goods" around us. A trope is a particular instance of a universal — this red, this sweet taste; a particular property [00:11:56].
- [00:12:01] The ethical payoff: if I accept not only that I have no enduring self but that, say, chocolate cake has no enduring reality, it can help me **lose my attachment** to it. Westerhoff: that is one reading, though the link between Madhyamaka ontology and ethics is complicated [00:12:21] — rejecting even the *conventionally* real ātman raises hard questions about **karmic causality, karmic responsibility, and who is reborn**. But at some level this is indeed an attempt to **combat attachment**.

## Is there a positive ontology? Emptiness is empty of itself
- [00:13:01] Having covered the negative side — the critique of ātman and of svabhāva in general — Adamson asks whether there's a **positive ontology**, any positive description of the world.
- [00:13:32] The "intriguing" claim: the theory of emptiness **applies to itself** — emptiness is itself empty. So Madhyamaka holds it is **not possible to give any objective account** of what the world is ultimately like. A materialist says "ultimately everything is material"; the Madhyamaka does **not** correspondingly say "ultimately everything is empty" as a master thesis [00:13:56].
- [00:14:02] Method: a **piecemeal approach** — take specific concepts especially prone to being conceptualized in terms of svabhāva and take them apart, one by one. This is open-ended (you can't predict what svabhāva-like notion an opponent will produce next), which is why there is **no master argument** for Madhyamaka that settles everything for all comers [00:14:34]. You shoot down each positive ontological claim individually.
- [00:14:50] What's unique: Madhyamaka applies its **dissolving analysis to its own theory**, yielding a theory that says **there is no ultimately true account of the world** — raising the question of whether that even counts as an ontological/philosophical theory, since such theories are usually meant to tell us what the world is ultimately like [00:15:18].
- [00:15:33] The pressure comes from the **self-refutation objection**: opponents say "you refute yourself — you claim everything is empty, but that very claim can't be empty, or it couldn't be true." Nāgārjuna's reply [00:16:05]: yes, the theory of emptiness **is empty too**, but that doesn't stop it doing philosophical work, such as refuting other theories. His analogy: just as an empty **chariot** can still carry wood, an empty philosophical theory can still do many things without being an ultimate account of reality [00:16:16]. This returns us to the therapeutic reading [00:16:26].

## The status of conventional reality
- [00:16:33] If emptiness leaves us with **conventional reality**, is conventional reality simply left standing — we just drop the idea of a svabhāva behind it — or is conventional reality itself put in question?
- [00:16:56] This becomes a huge issue in the post-Nāgārjuna commentary tradition, with **two main positions**:
  - **Conventional reality is false** [00:17:14]: it carries the superimpositions of svabhāva; we interact with it for practical purposes, but it can't tell us what the world is really like.
  - **Stratified conventions** [00:17:31]: there is no ultimately true theory, but that doesn't make every conventionally true theory **as bad as every other**. Conventional theories can be ranked **better or worse** even though none is best.
- [00:17:56] Adamson presses: couldn't a brahmanical opponent then say "fine — if our theory of ātman and the gods is the *best* conventional reality, we'll settle for that"? Westerhoff: no — that concedes Brahman is **not ultimately true**, just a convenient fiction; you've pulled the metaphysical rug out from under it [00:18:20]. The brahmanical opponent won't settle for mere pragmatic utility, so the two schools can't be reconciled this way [00:18:37].

## Personal identity and rebirth
- [00:18:48] A doctrine that sounds positively ontological: that one and the same person is **reincarnated** — we're told that without liberation we'll be reborn, and that actions yield fruits in a next life. But if you take **no-self** seriously, it's hard to see how anyone could be reborn, or even be the same person as fifteen minutes ago [00:19:31].
- [00:19:42] This problem is as old as the no-self doctrine (which goes back to the historical Buddha). The standard **Abhidharma** solution: treat the person as a **causal complex of momentary phenomena** — physical and mental — forming a **stream** unified by one moment causing the next [00:19:55]. For the Abhidharmikas this stream is the underlying reality on which "person" is superimposed, and it does all the work — explaining memory, karmic responsibility, and what is reborn.
- [00:20:24] The **candle** analogy: light one candle from another and you can't say it's strictly the same flame, but there's a **causal chain** from the first to the second — which is how you differentiate persons.
- [00:20:42] The Madhyamaka twist: all of this underlying machinery (the moments, etc.) is only **conventionally real**, since the Madhyamakas allow nothing ultimately real. Is that still enough to underwrite karmic responsibility and rebirth [00:21:04]?
- [00:21:10] Nāgārjuna's answer: calling something **conventional** does **not** mean we're making it up or can decide what it's like. The recurring metaphor is the world as a **magical illusion** or a **dream** [00:21:28]: in a dream you can't choose what you dream, and once inside it you can't manipulate it freely — it follows certain laws. In the Buddhist case, those "laws of the dream" in our waking world are the **karmic laws** — none of which is ultimately real [00:21:59].
- [00:22:04] Adamson notes this fits the **second** (stratified) reading of conventional reality. Westerhoff [00:22:32]: the real question is whether, within Madhyamaka, you want anything like a **scientific picture** — productive relations between theories. Either you say "the world is just the way it appears, full stop," or you say a theory like **physics** is in certain ways more useful than the **manifest image**. The first version takes conventional reality as the way things seem (and empty); the second sees **levels** of conventional reality [00:23:15].
- [00:23:29] Labels: the **first** interpretive position is associated with the commentator **Candrakīrti**, the **second** with the commentator **Bhāviveka** — two streams of reading Madhyamaka that developed an intricate dispute in later India and Tibet [00:23:42].

## The tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi)
- [00:23:59] Beyond ontology, Nāgārjuna is striking for his attention to **methodology** — the self-emptiness move was one example; another is his mastery of the **tetralemma** [00:24:38].
- [00:24:44] Caveats: in Buddhist literature the tetralemma is complex — its form varies (some have only two alternatives, some four), and authors do different things with it (usually rejecting the four, but occasionally affirming all four). Westerhoff focuses on the **dominant form** (four alternatives), the dominant handling (**reject all four**), and the dominant interpretation [00:26:27].
- [00:26:31] The four alternatives: **A**, **not-A**, **A and not-A**, **neither A nor not-A** — all rejected. The "A" can be things like the existence of nirvāṇa, the existence of persons, the assertion of emptiness, or the existence of the Buddha after death [00:26:41].
- [00:26:56] One interpretation: all four positions share an **objectionable presupposition**, and all four are rejected because the **presupposition itself** is to be rejected. The example from early Buddhist texts [00:27:18]: blow out a **fire** and ask "where did it go — north, south, east or west?" You deny all four, because the question falsely assumes the fire goes to some place once extinguished.
- [00:27:48] A more complex version, from **chapter 22** of the kārikās: "empty should not be asserted, non-empty should not be asserted, both or neither should not be asserted, since these are only said for the purpose of **designation**." Westerhoff unpacks the four [00:28:10]:
  - "Empty should not be asserted" — because emptiness must not be asserted as an **ultimately true** theory; no theory is, not even emptiness.
  - Nor (conventionally or ultimately) that all things **fail** to be empty and so have svabhāva — that's exactly what Madhyamaka denies.
  - Nor that **some but not other** objects are empty — some having svabhāva, some not.
  - Nor that there are objects that **both have and lack** svabhāva — which seems to violate the **law of non-contradiction** [00:28:47].
- [00:28:51] Adamson's objection: wouldn't the **fourth** limb *always* violate non-contradiction, so you get it "for free"? Westerhoff [00:29:01]: "another can of worms" — a good objection. The famous handling, due to **Bhāviveka**, distinguishes **two forms of negation** used in the tetralemma — one that preserves certain presuppositions of the negated proposition and one that doesn't — and once you see how they interact you can't dispose of the fourth limb so quickly [00:29:31].
- [00:29:42] A further logical worry: refusing *both* of two contraries ("everything is empty" / "not everything is empty") looks like violating the **law of the excluded middle** / **bivalence** — a meaningful claim should be true or false, so "neither true nor false" seems to break logic [00:30:00].
- [00:30:16] Westerhoff's reply, again via **presupposition failure**: ask "is the number three yellow or not?" — three isn't yellow, but if "not yellow" presupposes it has *some other* colour (green, blue, red), you'd negate that too, since an **abstract object** has **no colour at all** [00:30:33]. So you can negate **both** alternatives of a contradictory pair when the opponent setting them up makes a presupposition behind each that you reject. Given Nāgārjuna's **global rejection of svabhāva** [00:30:56], there are bad presuppositions behind an enormous number of propositions — perhaps all of them — so you can apply the tetralemma "all over the place," which is pretty much what he does [00:31:13].

## Reception in India and Tibet
- [00:31:16] A puzzle: Nāgārjuna is highly **reactive**, spending much of his time criticizing rivals, and such philosophers often don't found living traditions — yet he is one of the most influential Indian thinkers [00:31:39].
- [00:31:55] The most important split in reading Madhyamaka in India concerns the **status of conventional reality** — specifically whether some conventions can be **better than others** given there's no ultimately real reality. **Bhāviveka**: yes, some conventions are better. **Candrakīrti**: no — conventional reality is the way it appears, it is deceptive, and there we leave it [00:32:20].
- [00:32:35] When Buddhism (and Madhyamaka) is **translated into Tibet**, the tradition continues, and Madhyamaka read according to Candrakīrti — known as **Prāsaṅgika** Madhyamaka — becomes the **dominant** school in Tibetan Buddhism, in effect the official party line [00:32:57]. Other schools (**Yogācāra**, Abhidharma) are relegated to **propaedeutic** steps toward the final correct view [00:33:03].
- [00:33:22] A key Tibetan debate returns to the opening question: does Madhyamaka ask us to **reject the common-sense view of the world**? This ties to what the **object of negation** is — if everything is empty and nothing has svabhāva, what exactly is being rejected [00:33:41]? The Tibetan scholastic tradition sometimes posits a **falsely existent object** (what Madhyamaka rejects) alongside a **conventionally existent object** left untouched by the analysis [00:33:56].
- [00:34:03] This is "two sides of a coin." On one side [00:34:11]: confining the object of negation to a "falsely existent" object risks turning svabhāva into a **scholastic epiphenomenon** — why care that some false nature is rejected if a conventionally existent true nature is "still flying around"? It would mean a lot of work just to tell us the things we thought were true are true [00:34:31]. If Madhyamaka analysis applies only to "philosophical straw men" and not to "shoes and ships and sealing wax," it's hard to see what the fuss is about [00:34:40].
- [00:34:47] The other side: defenders say that if Madhyamaka **negates common sense / the way things appear**, you get a **nihilistic** theory on which nothing exists. Opponents reply [00:35:04] that it would be very surprising for any philosophical theory to "make the world disappear" — believing the arguments doesn't make things stop appearing. Rather, you're forced into a **discrepancy** between the theory ("it's all an illusion, it's not there") and the appearance of it as real [00:35:22]. This tradition claims that very **tension** is what can generate a **direct experiential awareness** of what the Madhyamakas are trying to show [00:35:40].
- [00:35:53] Adamson thanks Westerhoff — "one of my favourite Indian philosophy scholars talking about one of my favourite Indian philosophers" [00:35:57] — and promises to continue the series with Jonardon Ganeri next time.

## Key thinkers, texts & terms
- **Nāgārjuna** — 2nd–3rd-c. CE Buddhist monk, probably of southern India; founder of Madhyamaka, who supplied the arguments behind the perfection-of-wisdom sūtras' claims about emptiness.
- **Mūlamadhyamakakārikā** — Nāgārjuna's chief work, 450 verses ("Fundamental Stanzas / Wisdom of the Middle Way"); not a commentary but a philosophical explication of the prajñāpāramitā sūtras.
- **prajñāpāramitā (perfection of wisdom) sūtras** — make many philosophical claims without supplying arguments; said (mythologically) to have been guarded by nāgas until Nāgārjuna returned them.
- **nāgas** — underwater serpent spirits who, in the legend, safeguarded the perfection-of-wisdom texts; the source of Nāgārjuna's name.
- **śūnyatā (emptiness)** — the doctrine that nothing has svabhāva; crucially, emptiness is itself empty.
- **svabhāva** — "own being"; substance / essence / intrinsic nature. Has three dimensions: ontological (ground of being), semantic (language mirroring objectively structured reality), and cognitive (a default we project onto experience). Emptiness rejects all three.
- **dharmas** — the fundamental phenomena of early Buddhist analysis; Madhyamaka extends no-self from persons to the selflessness of all dharmas.
- **Abhidharma** — the metaphysics Madhyamaka also rejects; read here as a trope theory of momentary particulars, and the source of the causal-stream account of personal identity (the candle-flame analogy).
- **conventional reality** — what is left after emptiness; not "made up," but law-governed like a dream. Two readings: false-throughout (Candrakīrti) vs. stratified/better-or-worse (Bhāviveka).
- **tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi)** — four-cornered scheme (A / not-A / both / neither), here all rejected via presupposition failure (the blown-out fire; the colourless number three); chapter 22 applies it to emptiness itself.
- **Candrakīrti / Prāsaṅgika** — commentator holding conventional reality is uniformly deceptive; his Prāsaṅgika reading became dominant in Tibet.
- **Bhāviveka** — commentator allowing a stratification of conventions (better/worse); also the source of the two-negations solution to the tetralemma's fourth limb.
- **kāraka theory** — Sanskrit grammar's analysis of sentence components; an example of reading language as matching ontological structure (a svabhāva view of semantics).
- **Jan Westerhoff** — the guest; Oxford scholar of Madhyamaka who frames Nāgārjuna's philosophy as therapeutic and stresses emptiness as ontological, not merely skeptical.

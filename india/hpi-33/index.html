    HoPWaG India · Ep 33 — "Standard Deductions: Nyāya on Reasoning"¶

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:19:41 In brief: Having defended perception, Nyāya turns to its second source of knowledge — inference (anumāna). The episode lays out the anatomy of an inference (sign, locus, thing-inferred), Gautama's three kinds of reliable sign and Vātsyāyana's competing readings of them, Nyāya's case-based "give an example" style of reasoning, and the five fallacies that mark out a bad inference — a body of logic so rigorous that "even the most determined skeptic" must concede it is philosophy. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Ganari"; Nyāya ≈ "Niyaya/Niyya/Naya"; anumāna ≈ "unumana"; pramāṇa ≈ "pramana/promana"; Gautama ≈ "Gautima/Scoutima/Gautoma/Gautaum"; Vātsyāyana ≈ "Batsyayyayana/Vatsyayana/Zayena/Zayana/the Ayyana/Batsya Jaina"; Caraka Saṃhitā ≈ "Chara Samhita"; hetu/liṅga ≈ "hetu/linga"; pakṣa ≈ "pachshha"; sādhya ≈ "Sadiya"; pūrvavat ≈ "Purvavat"; śeṣavat ≈ "Sheshavat"; sāmānyatodṛṣṭa ≈ "Samay…"; jāti ≈ "Jati"; hetvābhāsa ≈ "Hedvabasa"; vyāpti ≈ "Vyapti"; Gaṅgeśa ≈ "Gungesha"; Śāntarakṣita ≈ "Shantarachita"; Dignāga ≈ "Dignaga"; Nāgārjuna ≈ "Nagajuna"; probans/probandum ≈ "probands/probandum"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 27 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019).     Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Gautama (Akṣapāda) — founder of Nyāya, who lists inference as a pramāṇa and classifies signs and fallacies [00:00:13].
    • Vātsyāyana — Gautama's commentator, who offers two rival interpretations of the three kinds of sign and supplies most of the worked examples [00:05:02].
    • The Nyāya-Sūtra — its final book, on genuine versus bogus resemblance, may be a separate work [00:10:50].
    • The Caraka Saṃhitā — an early medical text whose diagnostic reasoning shaped Gautama's theory of inference [00:02:10].
    • Gaṅgeśa — the 13th-century founder of "later Nyāya," celebrated for five definitions of vyāpti [00:03:38].

    Perception is not enough — we have to infer¶

    • [00:00:13] Today's episode: "Standard Deductions — Nyāya on reasoning." According to the Nyāya school we can know the world simply by perceiving it; as the previous episode showed, Gautama and his commentators mounted a spirited defence of perception as a source of knowledge — our senses tell us about things, their properties, even about absences.
    • [00:00:45] But we cannot get through life on perceptions alone. Adamson's set-piece: you are an ancient Indian walking through a forest and you hear a distinctive low growl, unmistakably the sound of a tiger. The right response is to "run like hell" — but to apply that rule you must do more than perceive the sound. You need to make an inference: you flee not from the sound but from the unseen tiger whose presence you infer from the sound.
    • [00:01:26] Inference is therefore as crucial to survival as perception, and Gautama duly lists it alongside perception as a pramāṇa (source of knowledge). The close link is built into the very word for inference, anumāna — "that which follows after perception." Inference is what lets us go beyond the directly perceived to what we cannot perceive: hidden, like the tiger in the trees, or in principle imperceptible, like the illness inferred from the symptoms it produces in the body.
    • [00:02:04] The medical example is chosen advisedly. In framing his theory Gautama seems to have been influenced by early medical works like the Caraka Saṃhitā. Diagnosis is the paradigm case of inference: cough and fever are signs of pneumonia — "less dangerous than a tiger, but still nothing to sneeze at."

    The anatomy of an inference: sign, locus, and thing-inferred¶

    • [00:02:27] The standard Indian example is the one already met: the mountain has fire because it has smoke. We perceive smoke on a distant hillside and from that sign arrive at knowledge of an unseen fire. Latin-showoffs would call the smoke the probans ("that which offers proof") and the fire the probandum ("that which is proven").
    • [00:02:58] The Nyāya thinkers, "not so strong on scholastic Latin," call the smoke a hetu or liṅga — a mark or sign. The technical vocabulary has three slots:
    • the sign (hetu/liṅga) — the smoke, the growl — which must always be present in the object the inference is about;
    • the pakṣa — that object, the locus: the mountain where smoke is seen, the trees from which the growl emanates;
    • the sādhya (sādhya-dharma) — the thing whose presence we infer from the sign: the fire on the mountain, the tiger in the trees.
    • [00:03:29] Nyāya logicians also gave great weight to the ability to cite an example — a second case where the same kind of sign revealed the same kind of signified thing, e.g. the smoke-producing fire in the kitchen. "When I tell you to get that cough checked out, I explain that I knew a guy with just these symptoms who died of pneumonia — or would have, if he hadn't first been eaten by a tiger because he was walking in the woods listening to a podcast with the volume turned up."

    From the book — ch. 27

    The book supplies the Sanskrit name for this corroborating "example" — dṛṣṭānta — the second case (the smoke-producing kitchen fire) cited to back up an inference.

    Which signs are reliable? Gautama's three kinds¶

    • [00:04:04] Giving one parallel example does not guarantee the inference yields knowledge. Suppose you see fire and infer there must be smoke — that is bad reasoning: fire sometimes makes smoke, but we have also seen fiery things with no smoke, so fire is not a dependable sign of smoke. And, come to think of it, tiger-sounds are not an absolutely reliable sign of tigers either — perhaps someone is doing an incredibly realistic tiger impression, or playing a tape recorder ("younger listeners may want to ask their parents what a tape recorder is").
    • [00:04:45] So if the Naiyāyikas want inferences to count as unimpeachable knowledge "in some cases," they owe us a story about which signs are reliable. We can piece one together from Gautama and his commentator Vātsyāyana. Gautama names three kinds of dependable sign: pūrvavat ("based on what is prior"), śeṣavat ("based on a remainder"), and a third, glossed as observation of a generality (sāmānyatodṛṣṭa (?)).
    • [00:05:16] The original meaning of these three terms is obscure — even the earliest commentators were unsure — and Vātsyāyana was uncertain enough to offer two alternative interpretations.

    Vātsyāyana's first reading — cause, effect, and generalization¶

    • [00:05:30] On the first reading the three signs mean reasoning from cause to effect, from effect to cause, and from a generalization to a particular case:
    • cause→effect: it will rain because of the large black cloud;
    • effect→cause: it has been raining, because the river is swollen;
    • generalization→particular: the sun must be moving now, even though it doesn't look like it, because over a longer period it is observed to change position.
    • [00:06:00] Despite the humble examples, this credits Vātsyāyana with sketching an account of scientific inference and prediction — with obvious applications in medicine, and (the moving sun) in a field like astronomy.

    Vātsyāyana's second reading — three different sorts of reasoning¶

    • [00:06:22] On the second reading the three terms name three different kinds of reasoning:
    • "based on what is prior" covers all causal inference (including everything in the first interpretation);
    • "based on a remainder" is reasoning by process of elimination — Vātsyāyana's example: sound is a substance, because it is not a universal, not a quality, not a motion, and so on. We fix a finite list of possibilities that exhausts the options and then eliminate all but one. Adamson likens this to doctors using tests to rule out diagnoses, and to Sherlock Holmes's maxim that "when you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth";
    • observation of a generality means assigning a property to something because of the class it belongs to — e.g. infer that tigers are predators from the fact that they are cats, since all cats are predators. Vātsyāyana's own example: mental states are qualities; qualities have subjects; therefore there is a subject of mental states, namely the self.
    • [00:07:47] That last example is a flag that this whole discussion matters for philosophical method, not just medical diagnosis and tiger-avoidance — Nyāya will use exactly this style of argument to establish the existence of the self.

    Positive and negative examples¶

    • [00:08:00] The Naiyāyikas are keen on examples, and elsewhere in the Nyāya-Sūtra Gautama explains that there are two kinds that can motivate an inference:
    • positive examples, which turn on a similarity — smoke indicating fire on the mountainside, as it also does in the kitchen;
    • negative examples, which cite a case where the sign is absent and the signified is also absent — instead of the kitchen, the surface of a lake, where neither smoke nor fire is found. By disanalogy, the mountain, which does have smoke, should have fire too.
    • [00:08:49] Whether positive or negative, such reasoning moves straightforwardly from one case to another — kitchen or lake to mountainside — with no appeal to any law of nature or universal rule.

    In praise of case-based reasoning — and its limits¶

    • [00:09:01] This may seem naïve, but there is much to be said for case-based reasoning. To persuade you it is about to rain: "Look at that large black cloud. Last time you saw a cloud like that, what happened? It rained. It's the same now, so it's definitely going to rain." The Nyāya-Sūtra offers a range of such cases: seeing ants carrying their eggs, one infers it will rain; seeing a full, swiftly-flowing river, one infers it has been raining; seeing smoke, one infers an unseen fire.
    • [00:09:43] One might dig deeper and ask why a sign is regularly linked to a thing — we have noticed ants carry their eggs before rain, while remaining "mystified as to how ants predict the weather." The same applies to inference by sampling: from the salty taste of one drop of seawater we infer the whole sea is salty; tasting one grain of rice, we infer all the rice is cooked. We tacitly assume a common underlying structure between sample and rest — this grain sat in the same pan, in the same water, for the same time.
    • [00:10:38] But case-based reasoning can go awry, so it becomes "a matter of cardinal importance" to distinguish genuine from bogus resemblance. This is the subject of the whole final book of the Nyāya-Sūtra — a section some modern scholars read as an independent work. Either way it shows Gautama was as interested in how things go wrong as in how we get them right.

    When resemblance is false: the jāti fallacy¶

    • [00:11:11] Argument from a false resemblance suffers the fallacy called jāti — a merely apparent commonality between an example and a locus. Recall inferring a tiger in the trees: in other cases that sound did accompany a real tiger, but in this case, "hopefully," it is only a tape recorder. The resemblance is superficial; the inference fails.

    Gautama's five fallacies (hetvābhāsa)¶

    • [00:11:33] Gautama in fact identifies five kinds of fallacy that undermine arguments. The Sanskrit for fallacy is hetvābhāsa — literally "something that merely has the appearance of being a sign." Their importance is partly tied to Nyāya's interest in debate (spotting a fallacy in an opponent's argument is a sure way to win), but also to the dependability of scientific prediction and medical prognosis — and mapping how arguments go astray is "an indirect way of showing what a sound argument is." Avoid all five and you can be sure you are reasoning correctly. The five — "which, coincidentally, are also the five pitfalls faced by teenagers when they start dating":

    From the book — ch. 27

    The book records Gautama's own Sanskrit names for the five, which the spoken version leaves untranslated: the deviating is savyabhicāra, the incoherent viruddha, the indecisive prakaraṇasama, the same-predicament sādhyasama, and the mistimed kālātīta.

    • [00:12:34] 1. The deviating. The sign is present but the inferred property is not. Vātsyāyana's example: sound is eternal because it is intangible — but we can name intangible things that are not eternal, e.g. pain (intangible, but thankfully it goes away). The lesson, turned around: a dependable sign must not deviate from the property — wherever the sign occurs, so must the signified. An analogy is not enough; there must be a universal rule connecting sign and signified. Only if tiger-noises always signify tigers can you confidently infer a tiger (though "you might want to run anyway, just to be safe").
    • [00:13:31] This universal rule, vyāpti (pervasion), became a major topic of later Nyāya, initiated in the 13th century by Gaṅgeśa with his celebrated five definitions of vyāpti.

    From the book — ch. 27

    A book footnote adds that before reaching the pain example, Vātsyāyana first offered a tangible-but-eternal parallel — an atom — against "sound is eternal because intangible." The book flags this earlier attempt as logically irrelevant: how things stand with tangible objects says nothing about the intangibles.

    • [00:13:45] 2. The incoherent. A conclusion should not contradict your other well-established beliefs, even if it looks plausible out of context. This is not a purely formal defect inside a single inference; rather, we reason within a larger set of commitments, and if an inference clashes with firmly held beliefs it is rational to doubt your premises. The strained example: fire is cold, because it is a substance — more plausible than it sounds, since all substances other than fire and fiery things are indeed cold; but fire is the exception, and our wider net of beliefs raises a warning flag ("fire is in fact hot"), so the argument must have gone wrong.

    • [00:14:51] 3. The indecisive. Two equally strong arguments lead to opposite conclusions; though neither can be faulted on formal grounds, we should reserve judgment. Example: sound is eternal because it is eternal — formally valid (a tautology, like "Socrates is mortal and all mortals are mortal, therefore Socrates is mortal"), but useless, because one could equally argue sound is non-eternal because it is non-eternal. With no ground for preferring one tautology, neither can be accepted.

    • [00:15:34] This is "highly reminiscent" of Greek skeptics like Sextus Empiricus, who induced suspension of judgment by arguing both sides of an issue. But Gautama is not endorsing skepticism: he assumes such standoffs arise only occasionally, not pervasively throughout philosophical argument as Sextus claimed.

    • [00:16:15] 4. The same predicament. A sign "not distinguished from what is signified… because it itself needs to be proved." Vātsyāyana's example: a shadow is a substance because it moves. This looks fine, since movement is unique to substances — but the sign itself (that a shadow moves) has not been demonstrated. It is no good arguing for a doubtful proposition on the strength of another doubtful proposition; we must resolve the doubtful case by appeal to known facts.

    • [00:16:56] 5. The mis-timed. The most enigmatic — commentators could not agree on Gautama's meaning. The basic idea: sometimes merely formulating a thesis is enough to show it cannot be true, so offering a reason in support would be pointless, hence "mistimed." Later Naiyāyikas read it differently — as cases where the intended conclusion has already been shown false by other means, making argument redundant; but Adamson finds that implausible (it would just be a case of the incoherent — a redundancy the Buddhist Śāntarakṣita in fact pointed out). The better reading: a thesis that is just obviously false — self-contradictory, like "there is a tiger in the trees but no tiger in the trees," or patently absurd, like "there is a tiger in the trees, but don't worry, he's a vegetarian." Reasoning for such a thesis is a waste of time.

    Why this counts as philosophy — and where Nyāya goes next¶

    • [00:18:10] Gautama's treatment of argument and its failures shows "a keen sensitivity to the nature of philosophical activity and to the demands of science." We have lingered on the logic because Nyāya is so notable for its analysis of method — and "even the most determined skeptic would have difficulty denying that this is philosophy." From a Western angle one may be puzzled by Nyāya's lack of a sharp line between the purely rational and the empirical — between (more Latin showing-off) the a priori and the a posteriori.
    • [00:18:46] Nyāya was not the only Indian contributor to logic. Nāgārjuna, "certainly a determined skeptic," subjected the theory of reasoning to a searching critique, and another Buddhist, Dignāga, would give a more precise definition of the good inferential sign — both to come later in the series.
    • [00:19:08] But the groundwork for the Nyāya–Buddhist contrast is not finished. The Buddhists were notorious for denying any stable self that persists through time as the locus of awareness; Nyāya, true to its optimism about reasoning, is optimistic here too — Gautama and his followers affirm that there is a self and investigate the faculties of thought and attention. Next time: from Nyāya epistemology and logic to Nyāya philosophy of mind.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • anumāna — inference; "that which follows after perception," the second pramāṇa, which takes us beyond the perceived to the unperceived (the hidden tiger, the imperceptible illness).
    • pramāṇa — a source/means of knowledge; Gautama lists inference alongside perception.
    • hetu / liṅga — the sign or mark from which we infer (the smoke, the growl); must always be present in the locus. Latin: probans.
    • pakṣa — the locus, the object the inference is about (the mountain, the trees).
    • sādhya (sādhya-dharma) — the thing inferred, whose presence the sign reveals (the fire, the tiger). Latin: probandum.
    • pūrvavat / śeṣavat / sāmānyatodṛṣṭa (?) — Gautama's three kinds of dependable sign ("based on what is prior," "based on a remainder," "observation of a generality"), given two rival readings by Vātsyāyana (cause/effect/generalization vs. causal inference / elimination / class-membership).
    • example (positive & negative) — a second corroborating case: positive turns on similarity (kitchen fire), negative on disanalogy (smokeless, fireless lake). Nyāya reasons case-to-case, with no appeal to a law of nature.
    • jāti — the fallacy of a merely apparent commonality between example and locus (false resemblance).
    • hetvābhāsa — "fallacy"; literally something that only appears to be a sign. Gautama lists five.
    • the five fallacies — the deviating (sign present, property absent), the incoherent (conclusion clashes with established beliefs), the indecisive (equal arguments both ways), the same predicament (the sign itself is unproven), and the mis-timed (reasoning for an obviously/self-evidently false thesis).
    • vyāpti — the universal "pervasion" rule connecting sign to signified; the central topic of later Nyāya.
    • Gautama (Akṣapāda) — founder of Nyāya; author of the Nyāya-Sūtra's theory of signs and fallacies.
    • Vātsyāyana — Gautama's commentator; offers the worked examples and the two interpretations of the three signs.
    • Caraka Saṃhitā — early medical text; its diagnostic reasoning influenced Gautama's theory of inference.
    • Gaṅgeśa — 13th-century founder of "later Nyāya," famed for five definitions of vyāpti.
    • Nāgārjuna / Dignāga — Buddhist thinkers who, respectively, critiqued the theory of reasoning and refined the definition of the good inferential sign.
    • Śāntarakṣita — Buddhist philosopher who noted the redundancy in one reading of the "mis-timed" fallacy.
    • Sextus Empiricus — Greek skeptic; the "indecisive" fallacy resembles his arguing-both-sides, but Gautama denies it leads to general skepticism.

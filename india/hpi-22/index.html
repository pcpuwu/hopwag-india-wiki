    HoPWaG India · Ep 22 — "Elisa Freschi on Mīmāṃsā"¶

    ← Ep 21

    Ep 23 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson with Elisa Freschi 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson with Elisa Freschi · Runtime: 00:35:29 In brief: An interview with Elisa Freschi (Austrian Academy of Sciences, Vienna; University of Vienna) on the Mīmāṃsā school — how an exegetical tradition built around Vedic sacrificial injunctions became a serious philosophy of language, action, and the self, and how the rival Bhāṭṭa (Kumārila) and Prābhākara (Prabhākara) sub-schools split over whether its conclusions extend beyond the Veda. Topic order, not strict narration; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gennari"; Elisa Freschi ≈ "Elisa Freschie/Eliza Feski"; Mīmāṃsā ≈ "Mimansa/Mianza/Riemansa/muonza"; Kumārila Bhaṭṭa ≈ "Kumara/Kumala/Komal/Khamarabhara"; Prabhākara Miśra ≈ "Prabhaka/Pramagara/Parabaga"; Śabara ≈ "Shabara"; Jaimini ≈ "Jiamini/Jamini"; Bādarāyaṇa ≈ "Baddharayana"; Śrautasūtra ≈ "Shraudashu/Shratasudra"; Vṛttikāra ≈ "Vritikara"; Asko Parpola ≈ "Asco Parpola/Ascovarvara"; Johannes Bronkhorst ≈ "Johannes Brankhurst"; Francis Clooney ≈ "Francis Clooney"; Kiyotaka Yoshimizu ≈ "Kiyodaka Yoshimitsu"; śyena ≈ "Shiena"; Pāṇini ≈ "punanee").

    Who the Mīmāṃsā are: exegesis of the Veda¶

    • [00:00:28] Adamson introduces the guest: Elisa Freschi, a researcher at the Austrian Academy of Sciences in Vienna and lecturer at the University of Vienna. The topic: philosophical ideas in Mīmāṃsā, "a school that's associated with the exegesis of the sacred Vedic literature."
    • [00:01:01] Freschi: Mīmāṃsā originated from a school of exegesis of the Veda. The Veda spans many genres — from the hymns of the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā through the Upaniṣads — but Mīmāṃsā scholars care specifically about the Brāhmaṇas, the parts containing sacrificial injunctions. Not the mythical or mystical portions: just the parts that say "if you want rain, sacrifice with the X sacrifice."
    • [00:01:42] Adamson notes wryly that this is "the part of the Vedas we discussed least" — almost not at all.

    Where the philosophy comes from¶

    • [00:01:48] Adamson presses: why is any of this philosophical? Freschi concedes there's "nothing philosophical in this part of the Veda" itself — "you can find physics everywhere" too. What's philosophical is the way Mīmāṃsā approaches it.
    • [00:02:08] Contrast case: the Śrautasūtra tradition also does exegesis of these texts, but only to "make a ritual out of the ritual prescriptions" — how many spoons of butter to use, etc. Purely technical, nothing philosophical.
    • [00:02:25] Mīmāṃsā instead thinks systematically about the prescriptions, which forces deep questions: how is the Veda valid cognition, and how sure can we be? Who is eligible to sacrifice (→ the concept of the subject)? The nature of language; the form of action.
    • [00:03:21] Were they self-aware about this? Yes — and here's the key difference from the Śrautasūtra. The Śrautasūtras just systematize Vedic texts to enable sacrifice (technical). Mīmāṃsā describes itself as concerned with the itikartavyatā of the Veda — its "procedure," a reflective element the thinkers were conscious of. This self-awareness increases over time, from Jaimini → Śabara → Kumārila.

    The three textual layers (and the lost texts behind each)¶

    • [00:04:19] Mīmāṃsā, like most schools of the period, is not monolithic — it subdivides and develops historically. Freschi gives the outline:
    • [00:04:25] The oldest extant text (not necessarily the oldest that existed), recognized as foundational by all later authors, is the Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra, attributed to Jaimini. But the Sūtra itself names other authors alongside Jaimini — e.g. "thus says Jaimini, as well as Bādarāyaṇa," or "Bādari says." Jaimini appears as just one of many authorities within his own text. (Bādarāyaṇa is credited with the Brahma-Sūtra — coming up in a later episode.)
    • [00:05:11] Despite this, the Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra has strong inner consistency — not a random collection of aphorisms by earlier authors, but (as Francis Clooney and others have shown) arguably a unitary product.
    • [00:05:29] Second layer: after Jaimini comes Śabara, who wrote a Bhāṣya (commentary). But Śabara constantly refers to earlier commentators whose work is lost — so a major development happened between Jaimini and Śabara that we can't directly see. Śabara has far more that looks philosophically relevant.
    • [00:06:18] Adamson systematizes: a Sūtra level (an intelligent compilation of many people's ideas) and a Bhāṣya / commentary level. Freschi refines it: Śabara isn't engaging a vague crowd — he has a "pet opponent," the Vṛttikāra (author of a lost Vṛtti), deeply into epistemology (which instruments of knowledge are reliable). A specific dialectical context, not a mess of voices.
    • [00:06:45] Third layer — the split into two schools, after the Bhāṣya. Kumārila Bhaṭṭa founds the Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā; Prabhākara Miśra founds the Prābhākara school. Both commented on the Śabara-Bhāṣya and accepted the authority of both the Sūtra and the Bhāṣya — but their interpretations diverge sharply.
    • [00:07:30] Even before the split there were divergent traditions: Kumārila refers to someone who can't be Prabhākara (since, following Kiyotaka Yoshimizu's arguments, Prabhākara was a slightly younger contemporary of Kumārila) yet cites ideas later found in the Prābhākara school. So at least two distinct Mīmāṃsā traditions predate the named split.
    • [00:08:09] Adamson's takeaway: at all three levels — Sūtra, Bhāṣya, two-school split — there are lost texts in the background, represented only through the earliest surviving texts. "Quite typical for this whole period" — a good warning.

    The standard injunction: "one who desires heaven should sacrifice"¶

    • [00:08:37] To show why this is philosophically interesting, Adamson asks for the stock example. Freschi gives it: darśa-pūrṇamāsābhyāṃ svargakāmo yajeta — "the one who desires heaven should sacrifice with the darśa and pūrṇamāsa," the new-moon and full-moon sacrifices. (Adamson: "I think it's good that you said that and not me.")
    • [00:09:19] First philosophical wrinkle: this exact sentence isn't found in the Veda. It might sit in a lost portion, but more typically it is an abstraction made by Mīmāṃsā authors — characteristic of their method. They treat the disorderly Brāhmaṇas (full of mythical digressions) as if they were orderly procedural texts, then rationalize them into main prescriptions, auxiliaries, etc.
    • [00:10:00] To do this they invented a large body of exegetical rules (e.g. for deciding whether a passage forms a single unitary statement). These spread through Indian thought — rhetoric, Dharmaśāstra, Vedānta — as rational rules for connecting sentences and determining meaning. Mīmāṃsā were "the first ones who decided how we can define what a sentence is."

    Authorless words and the force of a prescription¶

    • [00:10:49] So one part of the analysis is strictly exegetical/linguistic, and another concerns what a Vedic prescription is really doing. Crucially, for Mīmāṃsā the Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya). They can't say the injunction is telling you God's will — "our normal Christian-influenced answer." So they ask: is there a prescriptive force in the injunction itself? How is it communicated — through your understanding of it, or through the force of the optative/injunctive suffix?
    • [00:11:36] What does "authorless" even mean — where do the words come from? Freschi: language for Mīmāṃsā is anādi, beginningless. Unless you can prove otherwise — "a photo of the Big Bang" — there's no reason to assume language was ever different than it is now. The Veda has always been part of the intellectual landscape; looking for its origin is "an unwarranted move."
    • [00:12:27] So a Vedic prescription is more like a moral prescription — it comes from nowhere, it's just a rule to obey. Freschi: yes, Kumārila argues much like this, treating them as "eternal moral thoughts."

    Desire as the trigger — and as the root of all action¶

    • [00:12:44] The injunction is conditional on desiring heaven — so if I don't desire heaven, I'm off the hook? Freschi: the sixth chapter of the Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra says everyone desires heaven, because heaven is happiness and everyone desires happiness.
    • [00:13:14] For Kumārila, this generalizes into a universal theory of action: "no one undertakes an action unless he desires its result." Not just Vedic injunctions — every action whatsoever is driven by desire.
    • [00:13:44] But the schools diverge. Prabhākara stays focused on the Veda and treats ordinary language as an exception to it, whereas Kumārila centers ordinary life. So for Prabhākara desire is merely the identifier of who is eligible to sacrifice — with no guarantee of the result. It picks you out as the right performer, even if the fruit never comes.
    • [00:14:44] Concretely: everyone must perform the darśa-pūrṇamāsa (everyone desires heaven), but more specific sacrifices — for rain, for conquering a village — apply only to those with that particular desire. Adamson: if there's already too much rain, the "desire rain" injunction simply doesn't apply to me.

    Who is eligible: women, lower classes, the "open-minded" sixth chapter¶

    • [00:15:15] Adamson: don't a lot of these injunctions apply only to the Brahmin class? Freschi calls the sixth chapter (on desire) strikingly open-minded by contemporary standards: it speaks of the omnipresence of desire across all human beings and even animals. Women can desire things, so in principle they'd be entitled to sacrifice; so would lower classes.
    • [00:15:55] The limitations imposed are contingent, not a priori. Women would be entitled to sacrifice but don't own property — so they lack cows to offer the priests. That's different from declaring them irrational or excluded by nature. Similarly, mute people can't perform sacrifices because they can't utter the mantras — an instrumental lack, not a deficit of reason. "They have the desire, but not the instrument."

    Against the Buddhists: can there be desireless action?¶

    • [00:16:31] Adamson: this all sounds uncontroversial — I want X, the rule tells me how to get X. But in India it is controversial, because the Buddhists (and even the Bhagavad-Gītā's ideal of unattached action) say we shouldn't act from desire. Do the Mīmāṃsakas have good arguments for desire's centrality?
    • [00:17:26] Yes — Kumārila is "quite rude" against the Buddhists, mocking the idea that action without desire is possible. His argument: if the Buddha truly reached nirvāṇa and is desireless, why did he start teaching? Teaching requires wanting to communicate. So either he reached nirvāṇa and stays silent, or he teaches — which means he still has desire and is "just a fake."
    • [00:18:05] A Buddhist opponent in Kumārila's text replies: the Buddha doesn't really utter words; they come out of him "naturally," without his meaning them. Kumārila: then you're just a blind believer. Freschi relishes the irony — Westerners reading Mīmāṃsā assume they are the blind believers in the Veda, when in fact the Mīmāṃsakas saw themselves as rational, empiricist, down-to-earth people following common sense, and ridiculed the Buddhists for the "bizarre" notion of desireless action.
    • [00:18:48] Adamson finds the example plausible. But what about the Gītā's unattached action: Arjuna must fight, yet without attachment to the outcome, perhaps dedicating the act to God? Couldn't I have reasons to act while staying open to the action failing?
    • [00:19:46] Freschi: back to the Kumārila/Prabhākara split. Kumārila would say even then there is desire — the desire to comply with a moral law you believe in, to please God, to be a good person. Without some desire you'd never begin acting at all. (Adamson: so the desire is devotion to God rather than winning the battle — yes.)
    • [00:20:18] Prabhākara's answer: the result merely identifies the agent. It picks you out of all possible individuals as the one who must act — but there's no guarantee you'll get the result, and you needn't be attached to getting it. You'd like to win the battle, and that's what brings you onto the battlefield, but you act without knowing you'll succeed.
    • [00:21:01] Freschi notes that later Vedāntin authors (working in a theistic setting the podcast won't reach) used Prabhākara-style arguments about non-attachment to results — dovetailing with the idea that you sacrifice because the Veda commands it, not for the result. The Veda is truthful so it will work, but the reason to sacrifice is the command, not the payoff.

    The self as an agent: Kumārila vs. everyone¶

    • [00:21:41] Lurking behind the theory of action is a self / agent who forms the desire and performs the act — commonsensical, but denied by other traditions. What does Mīmāṃsā say to prove there's a self?
    • [00:22:18] Freschi: the argument again starts from common sense. Without an agent, you couldn't ensure that the karma you accumulate accrues to you and not to someone else. The whole karma framework — shared by almost everyone in Indian philosophy — collapses without an enduring agent. And we know the Buddhists struggled to justify karma without a persisting self.
    • [00:22:50] But the Nyāya / Sāṃkhya / Vedānta opponents are different: they admit there is a self, but it's changeless — aloof from action and cognition, hence useless for explaining everyday acts. Kumārila argues this changeless self is no help at all — it's "very much akin to the Buddhist self" in being useless for explaining how the world works.
    • [00:23:33] In the Ātmavāda ("doctrine of the self") portion of his Ślokavārttika — a main work — Kumārila is even willing to give up the self's nityatva (eternality/permanence) if permanence is taken to mean it can't change. He'd rather keep a permanent self that is no longer changeless, because his self must be a person who engages in actions.
    • [00:24:13] This is genuinely controversial: most schools posit either no self (Buddhists) or a self devoid of action (Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, Vedānta — action belongs to lower, accidental faculties). For Kumārila the self is intrinsically an agent, intrinsically able to cognize, hence able to acquire new cognitions — i.e. to change. His analogy: a baby becomes a child, then an adult, while remaining the same person. A self can change yet stay identical; it needn't be static to be the same person.

    Is this all just exegesis dressed up? And: is it even "religious"?¶

    • [00:25:21] Adamson presses the deep worry: how much of this is just an effort to make the Vedic sentences come out true? There's a nice irony that a "highly religious exegetical project" ends up defending the deliverances of common sense ("yes, I do have a self that acts and knows"). And how much rides on grammatical analysis — going back to Pāṇini's grammar (the nominative-case agent, the action, the instrument, the outcome)? Is Mīmāṃsā essentially claiming the grammar of Vedic sentences mirrors the structure of the world?
    • [00:26:25] Freschi unpacks several tangled things. First, she resists calling Mīmāṃsā "religious": "religion" is hard to define, and in the Christian-influenced West we tie it to God — but Mīmāṃsā is basically an atheist school, so the label would mislead listeners.
    • [00:26:59] On whether the conclusions hold outside the Veda: it depends on which school. Kumārila is "really a philosopher" in the philosophical arena — he wants to debate and, if possible, demolish the Buddhist epistemologists and the Nyāya views, so his ideas are meant to apply beyond Vedic validity.
    • [00:27:31] Prabhākara, by contrast, is Veda-focused and treats ordinary language as an exception. He defends the controversial, counter-commonsensical thesis that language is inherently prescriptive: any sentence is either meaningless or has meaning only insofar as it is subsidiary to a prescription.
    • [00:27:55] Freschi's teaching example: telling a young boy "it's nine o'clock" really means "go to bed, brush your teeth" — language only makes sense as an addition to a prescription. Even her own descriptive sentences would, on this view, be part of the overarching prescription "believe what I'm telling you."
    • [00:28:39] So the answer to Adamson's question depends on the branch: one associates the theory of action with language generally (Kumārila), the other with a specific use of language (Prabhākara). Freschi qualifies: for Prabhākara, Vedic language is the paradigmatic case — not a special kind of language but the main thing; you understand language best by starting with Vedic language and generalizing. From the outside it looks like he's focused on a narrow case; he'd say he's focused on the paradigm.

    Contradictions in the Veda: the śyena sacrifice¶

    • [00:29:15] Another problem: the Veda is a huge corpus, so it can contradict itself — telling you to do one thing here, another there. Do they worry? Very much — it's a hot topic.
    • [00:29:46] The standard case: the general injunction "na hiṃsyāt sarvabhūtāni" — "you should not harm any living being" — versus the śyena sacrifice, which says that if you want to harm your enemy, sacrifice with the śyena and your enemy will die. All Mīmāṃsakas agree you ought not perform the śyena, but disagree why.
    • [00:30:21] The bind: saying "it's immoral, so don't" puts an authority above the Veda, which no Mīmāṃsaka will accept. So they need another solution. One option: a hierarchy of prescriptions — some (typically prohibitions, like the ban on violence) apply more generally than others.
    • [00:30:58] A "quite nice" alternative: the Veda simply states everything, and your desire identifies which injunctions apply to you. If you want to harm your enemy, then yes, you should perform the śyena — but you shouldn't have gotten that far. By desiring to harm your enemy you've already violated the prohibition on harming any living being, which includes harming with your thoughts. So again, desire determines when you fall under an injunction's scope.

    Action as effort, not physical motion¶

    • [00:31:40] An aside Freschi adds: for Mīmāṃsā (unlike Vaiśeṣika), action is the undertaking of the action — the effort toward it — not the physical realization. Adamson: so it's forming the intention and commanding your limbs to move that is the action, not the limbs actually moving. Yes.

    Why no God, afterlife, or rebirth — and the link to Vedānta¶

    • [00:32:02] Final question: Freschi called Mīmāṃsā atheist, and indeed it sounds so focused on parsing the rules that much of even the Vedic worldview goes missing — nothing about afterlife, rebirth, or God (later Mīmāṃsā gets more theistic). Why is that all absent?
    • [00:33:01] One answer (from Asko Parpola): the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra and the Brahma-Sūtra (Vedānta's foundational text) were originally a single text — plausibly, since both cite the same authors and share a structure. On that view the Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra is one part of a whole whose latter part handled the self, God, immortality, etc.
    • [00:33:53] But the thesis "might be wrong": Johannes Bronkhorst argues the Brahma-Sūtra is just a later imitation of the Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra's style. Freschi can't subscribe to Parpola's reading, attractive as it is.
    • [00:34:13] Either way, the two were eventually made to complement each other — Mīmāṃsā analyzing the rules, Vedānta supplying the context of salvation. Historically, Vedāntin authors did adopt Mīmāṃsā as a preliminary stage of their own school — most clearly in the theistic Vedāntas.
    • [00:34:45] The exception is Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedānta (likely the only Vedānta the podcast will cover), which is much more against Mīmāṃsā: being anti-realist and anti-commonsensical, defending a changeless self, Śaṅkara is far less willing to treat Kumārila as preliminary. But the theistic Vedāntas see Mīmāṃsā as the first step in a continuous development.
    • [00:35:11] Adamson: a perfect transition, since the podcast now moves to Vedānta — "almost as if we planned that in advance." He thanks Elisa Freschi; she thanks him; on to Vedānta next time.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Mīmāṃsā — school of Vedic exegesis turned philosophy; atheist, realist, common-sensical; focuses on the Brāhmaṇas' sacrificial injunctions, not the myth or mysticism.
    • Veda / Brāhmaṇas — the sacred corpus; Mīmāṃsā mines the Brāhmaṇas' ritual injunctions ("if you want rain, sacrifice with X").
    • Śrautasūtra — rival exegetical tradition that systematizes ritual only technically (how many spoons of butter), with no philosophy.
    • itikartavyatā — the Veda's "procedure"; Mīmāṃsā's reflective, self-aware concern with it (vs. the Śrautasūtras' bare systematizing).
    • Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra — oldest extant Mīmāṃsā text, attributed to Jaimini but citing other authorities; argued (Clooney) to be a consistent unitary work.
    • Jaimini — named author of the foundational Sūtra, himself one of several authorities it cites.
    • Bādarāyaṇa — authority cited in the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra; credited with the Brahma-Sūtra (Vedānta's foundational text).
    • Śabara / Śabara-Bhāṣya — author of the foundational Bhāṣya (commentary); engages the lost epistemologist Vṛttikāra as his pet opponent.
    • Kumārila Bhaṭṭa — founder of Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā; brought Mīmāṃsā into the philosophical arena; universal desire-theory of action; self as intrinsically an agent; Ślokavārttika.
    • Prabhākara Miśra — founder of the Prābhākara school; Veda-centric; desire merely identifies the eligible agent with no guarantee of result; language is inherently prescriptive.
    • Ślokavārttika — Kumārila's major work; its Ātmavāda section argues the self can change yet remain the same person.
    • apauruṣeya / anādi — the Veda is authorless and language beginningless; no origin to seek, so no God's-will reading of injunctions.
    • darśa-pūrṇamāsa — the new-moon/full-moon sacrifices in the stock injunction "one who desires heaven should sacrifice"; the injunction is itself a Mīmāṃsā abstraction.
    • svarga-kāma — "desirous of heaven"; everyone desires heaven (= happiness), so the conditional injunction binds all.
    • karma — accruing fruits of action; needs an enduring agent to ensure your karma accrues to you (a problem for Buddhists).
    • śyena — the harm-your-enemy sacrifice; its clash with "harm no living being" is the test case for Vedic self-contradiction; resolved via desire/prohibition-hierarchy.
    • na hiṃsyāt sarvabhūtāni — "you should not harm any living being"; the general prohibition trumping the śyena.
    • unattached action (Bhagavad-Gītā / Arjuna) — acting without attachment to results; Kumārila reads even this as desire (to please God), Prabhākara as desire merely identifying the agent.
    • Asko Parpola — argues the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra and Brahma-Sūtra were originally one text.
    • Johannes Bronkhorst — counter-argues the Brahma-Sūtra merely imitates the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra's style.
    • Śaṅkara / Advaita Vedānta — anti-realist, changeless-self Vedānta most hostile to Mīmāṃsā; theistic Vedāntas instead treat Mīmāṃsā as a preliminary stage.

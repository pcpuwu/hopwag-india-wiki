    HoPWaG India · Ep 55 — "Doors of Perception: Dignāga on Consciousness"¶

    ← Ep 54

    Ep 56 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:18:06 In brief: Dignāga's theory of self-awareness (svasaṃvedana): for him there is no perception without an awareness built into the perception itself — a "reflexive" view, against the Nyāya "reflective" theory that the mind must perform a separate second-order act to notice it. The episode reconstructs his double-aspect theory of cognition, his memory and regress arguments, weighs their force, and asks how Buddhists who deny the self can put so much weight on self-awareness. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Dignāga ≈ "Dignaga"; Nyāya ≈ "Niaya/Niyaya"; manas ≈ "manas"; Dharmakīrti ≈ "Dama Kirti/Damar Kirti/Damakirti/Dharma Kirti"; Uddyotakara ≈ "Udiotakara"; Yogācāra ≈ "yoga-chara"; Sautrāntika ≈ "South Trantica"; Birgit Kellner ≈ "Birgit-Kerner"; Aśoka ≈ "Ashoka"; Buddhaghosa ≈ "Buda Gosa"; Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Gernardin Ganari"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 44 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019). Its sources & a reading list are under Further reading below.

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Dignāga — the Buddhist epistemologist whose theory of perception was covered in the previous episode; here, his theory of self-awareness [00:00:33].
    • The Nyāya school — his target; holds the rival "reflective" theory of self-awareness via the faculty of mind (manas) [00:02:09].
    • Dharmakīrti — later Buddhist who develops and radicalizes Dignāga's arguments in an idealist direction [00:08:35].
    • Uddyotakara — Nyāya philosopher who reads self-awareness as proof of a real self [00:15:48].

    Setting the question: is Mick Jagger aware that he is seeing?¶

    • [00:00:33] Adamson picks up the running gag from the perception episode: Mick Jagger still seeing a red door, not yet painted black — fitting, since "you can't always get what you want." The new question: in addition to seeing the red door, is Mick aware that he is seeing a red door?
    • [00:00:56] We are sometimes obviously aware of having an experience — e.g. reflecting on how much we are enjoying a piece of music. But it can seem some experiences come without self-awareness: get immersed enough in music and you may no longer be aware of yourself listening; or, at the other extreme, music plays in a loud coffee shop and you don't notice you're hearing it until someone points it out, then realize a Rolling Stones song has been on the whole time.
    • [00:01:33] Plausible as these examples are, Dignāga would reject the lesson: for him there is no perception without self-awareness. And the awareness he means is one built into the very perception itself — it is just part of seeing a red door to be aware of seeing it, and you can never be oblivious of seeing it while seeing it.

    The opponent: the Nyāya "reflective" theory¶

    • [00:02:04] These were controversial claims, and Dignāga was not the first Indian thinker interested in self-awareness — he was rejecting a leading theory found in the Nyāya school.
    • [00:02:15] For Nyāya, one reason to postulate a faculty of mind (manas) at all is to explain self-awareness. Mick's vision simply perceives the red door; his mind then has to get involved to give him awareness of that perception. This is a reflective theory: the perceiver must reflect mentally on their perception to become aware of it. The mind performs a function called inner apperception — Mick's mind perceives that he is seeing, much as his seeing perceives the door.
    • [00:02:51] On this view awareness is a second-order phenomenon — like a belief about a belief (I believe that Mick's belief that the door is red is correct), or a desire about a desire (Mick wishing he could stop wanting everything painted black).
    • [00:03:09] Dignāga denies awareness is like that. He grants we can engage in second-order reflection on our perceptions, but that is not what awareness is for him. He adopts a reflexive view: awareness of perceiving is built into the perception already at the first-order level.
    • [00:03:31] The Indian framing of the contrast: other-illumination (reflective) vs. self-illumination (reflexive). Either way, you might still think some perceptions occur without awareness — especially on the reflective view, since if I need a separate second-order act to be aware of a perception, presumably I only do that some of the time. The Nyāya can say the coffee-shop patron is prompted to that active reflection when made aware she'd been hearing the Rolling Stones unnoticed.

    From the book — ch. 44

    The book supplies the Sanskrit technical terms the spoken episode gives only in English: self-awareness is svasaṃvitti or svasaṃvedana; the Nyāya mind's "inner apperception" is anuvyavasāya; and the other-illumination / self-illumination contrast is paraprakāśa vs. svaprakāśa.

    • [00:04:08] Even on a reflexive view one could imagine some perceptions have built-in awareness and some lack it — first an unaware perception of the music, then an aware one once it's pointed out. Dignāga, however, insists awareness is reflexive and always present. Why?

    The phenomenalist backdrop and the double-aspect theory¶

    • [00:04:33] It has to do with his theory of perception from the earlier episode. Dignāga is a phenomenalist: we do not immediately perceive external objects but at best grasp them indirectly via what he calls knowable interior forms — representations internal to the perceiver. So awareness of such a representation is part of what it is to perceive.
    • [00:04:55] As Dignāga states in his collected verses on the means of knowing (pramāṇa), every thought or experience has two aspects, objective and subjective. The objective aspect is easy: it's in the nature of thought to be directed at some object — even dreams and imaginings have content (a dream of a red door is of the door). But for Dignāga that content is not an external red door; the thought is directed at an internal representation, and he leaves it open whether anything outside corresponds to it.
    • [00:05:44] The subjective aspect is harder to state. Think of it as the way the content presents itself to us. Analogy: photographs and paintings. A photo of a face has the face as its object but also its own qualities — brightness, sharpness, contrast — that depend on how it was taken. Calling a photo overexposed or underdeveloped attends to features of the photograph itself, not what it shows. Likewise many frescoes are all of the Buddha, but what makes one serene, another typically Burmese, are the subjective qualities of the individual paintings.

    From the book — ch. 44

    The book pins the arguments to specific verses of Dignāga's Collected Verses on the Means of Knowing: the two-aspects-of-cognition passage is §1.9–11, and the memory argument §1.11–12.

    Proving the subjective aspect: second-order reflection¶

    • [00:06:32] To prove perception really has a built-in subjective aspect, Dignāga points to the very phenomenon Nyāya made responsible for awareness — second-order reflection. When I think I am enjoying a piece of music, or tell myself in a dream that I'm only dreaming, I am attending to an experience I must already have been aware of. If the first-order perception had only objective content, this would be impossible.
    • [00:06:55] Take Mick again: if his perception contained nothing but its object (the door), then reflecting on it he could only reflect on the door. But in fact Mick can reflect on the fact that he is seeing the door — attending to his own subjective role, including the way the door looks to him (in foggy London he might think he sees it only through a haze, or that in poor light it almost looks orange).
    • [00:07:27] The painting/photo analogy again: a painting of a painting is not a duplicate of the original; photographing a photograph is not ordering a second set of prints. The second-order image records the way the first image represented its object — photograph an overexposed photo of a face and the object of the second photo is an overexposed photograph, not just the face.

    The memory argument¶

    • [00:07:53] You might think Dignāga could just invite readers to introspect and see he's right — if you're always aware of your perceptions, you ought to know it. But he does not rest on everyday experience, perhaps because this awareness is often tacit, rarely coming to active attention. (Compare: you spend your whole waking day conscious without explicitly thinking about being conscious — perhaps his self-awareness is like that.)
    • [00:08:28] So he offers a more complex argument from memory, later developed by Buddhists from Dharmakīrti onward. Suppose Mick goes home, cheers up, and now thinks the door's red shade was lovely. Looking back, he does not simply remember a red door — he remembers perceiving the red door while standing in front of it.
    • [00:09:05] This is plausible. You'll recall the Buddhist king Aśoka erected ethical inscriptions across his empire — but unless you're remarkably old you don't remember seeing them erected. Dignāga's double-aspect theory explains the difference: when you remember an event you did not experience, you recall only objective content (a belief that it occurred); when you remember experiencing an event, you are recalling the subjective awareness you had of it.

    The regress argument¶

    • [00:09:36] The opponent can resist: when we remember an event, maybe we remember two things — the content of the original experience plus a second-order reflection directed at it. So Mick remembers the red door and remembers reflecting that he was seeing a red door.
    • [00:10:05] Against this Dignāga offers a regress argument. How will Mick remember reflecting on his experience? He'd need to have been aware of that reflective act too, to remember it. But the opponent denies cognitions have built-in awareness — so the second-order thought ("ah, here I am seeing a door") will itself need a third-order thought to be noticed ("ah, here I am noticing that I am seeing a door"), and so on indefinitely.
    • [00:10:40] Since for the opponent a cognition is fixed entirely by its objective content and not by subjective awareness, the higher-order thoughts never get awareness into the picture — never include a first-person perspective. We get only: seeing a red door, thinking of seeing a red door, thinking of thinking about seeing a red door, with awareness perpetually deferred.

    Weighing the argument — does it prove too little?¶

    • [00:11:06] Powerful, but it may achieve less than Dignāga needs. He must show not just that some cognitions include subjective awareness but that they all do — and the regress doesn't show that. The reflective theorist could stop the regress at any stage by saying we've reached a cognition that, unlike simple perception, is self-aware.
    • [00:11:28] Imagine the coffee-shop patron, unaware she's hearing music, who then has it brought to her attention. Isn't it reasonable that in the very act of reflecting on her previously oblivious perception she is aware she is reflecting? ("Ah yes — isn't that sympathy for the devil I hear, and hasn't it been playing for minutes?") Her own mental life is brought to her attention. So the opponent can plausibly say awareness is built into the second-order reflection but not the first-order perception — and that's what she'd remember later.
    • [00:12:08] The same problem hits Dharmakīrti's version, which adds the idea that the original perception is itself somehow in doubt: the perceiver can't be reassured the perception really grasps its object without being aware that it does — "awareness would establish the perception." But add a second-order reflection ("here I am seeing a door") and it seems to need similar ratification. As modern scholar Birgit Kellner notes, Dharmakīrti may be assuming one thing cannot make another known unless it is itself known — fair enough, but it invites the same reply: maybe the second-order reflection is self-ratifying while first-order perception is not.

    Defending Dignāga and Dharmakīrti¶

    • [00:13:02] In their defence: this reply makes a significant concession — that at least some cognitions are reflexively self-aware. If the regress is aimed at someone who insists cognition never has a subjective aspect and so always needs higher-order reflection to be noticed, then the regress works just fine.
    • [00:13:33] And one who concedes some cognitions are self-aware and others not now owes an explanation of why: why is Mick not actively aware of seeing the door, yet aware once he reflects that he sees it? Dignāga has no such problem, since for him awareness is just part of what it is to perceive or cognize. As Dharmakīrti puts it: someone who does not perceive the awareness of something is not aware of anything at all.

    How far the theory reaches: idealism vs. realism¶

    • [00:13:55] One reason Dignāga's account was so influential is that it could be accepted across several strands of Buddhism (though some Tibetan Buddhists later rejected it). Dharmakīrti takes it in an idealist direction, denying there are any external objects at all.
    • [00:14:15] Dignāga himself seems to say only that the immediate objects of perception are internal representations and that we are always aware of our perceptions of them — awareness being what gives us access to the internal objects — which is compatible with those representations representing things out in the world. Dharmakīrti, though, felt that if we are only ever aware of internal representations, there's no role left for external objects to play — yielding the full flowering of Yogācāra Buddhist idealism, so influential in Tibet.
    • [00:14:57] Yet Dignāga's basic principle could also be accepted by the non-idealist Sautrāntika tradition, which does accept the reality of external things. The shared principle: we only grasp things as we are representing them, from a first-person perspective. Whether or not we grasp real objects, our grasp is never "objective" in the sense of view-from-nowhere — we always grasp things in a certain way, as a photographic portrait presents only one particular view of a face.

    The puzzle: why would self-deniers prize self-awareness?¶

    • [00:15:25] It may seem strange this theory arose within Buddhism of all traditions: Buddhists reject the real existence of the self, so why stress self-awareness? Isn't the "first person" in a first-person perspective precisely the self?
    • [00:15:48] This is exactly what Nyāya philosophers like Uddyotakara thought — that introspective self-awareness establishes the reality of the self. Proponents of the self also pointed to memory (the very phenomenon at the centre of Dignāga's proof) as confirming a self that stays identical over time. Indeed some non-Buddhist philosophers after Dignāga borrowed his reflexive account of mind and turned it into a theory of the self, claiming the self just is self-consciousness.
    • [00:16:20] But in denying the self the Buddhists never meant to deny we have a mental life. There are no selves, but there are flowing streams of reflexively self-aware moments of consciousness — streams that include memories of past moments and anticipations of future ones.
    • [00:16:43] The mistake is to allow thoughts of the form "I am happy" or "I am in pain," implying we can draw a firm boundary around one stream of subjectivity and say this and this alone is me. It's like tracking individual waves in the sea and thinking there's a good answer to "is this another wave, or the same wave as before?" — the sort of question the Buddha met with silence. So, despite appearances, the idea that consciousness is reflexive is compatible with there being no self.

    Where we go next¶

    • [00:17:18] That ends the look at Dignāga — but not at Buddhist philosophy. The question just asked was whether there is a single self that comes to awareness whenever we are reflectively or reflexively conscious; the Buddhists say no. But that isn't the only route to an enduring self.
    • [00:17:42] You might think we need a self as a control centre directing our intentions and acts of mind and body. Next time moves to Sri Lanka for a slightly earlier Buddhist who tackled this objection and argued we are like puppets with no one pulling the strings — Buddhaghosa. "So be a doll and join us."

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Dignāga — Buddhist epistemologist; holds that perception always carries a built-in, reflexive self-awareness (svasaṃvedana); no perception without awareness of perceiving.
    • svasaṃvedana (self-awareness / self-illumination) — awareness of perceiving built into the perception itself at the first-order level; the reflexive view, opposed to the reflective "other-illumination" view.
    • reflective vs. reflexive — reflective (Nyāya): a separate second-order mental act makes a perception noticed; reflexive (Dignāga): awareness is intrinsic to the first-order perception.
    • Nyāya — rival school; posits the mind-faculty (manas) performing "inner apperception" to explain awareness as a second-order phenomenon.
    • manas — the faculty of mind which, for Nyāya, reflects on a perception to yield awareness of it.
    • double-aspect theory of cognition — Dignāga's claim that every thought has two aspects: objective (its content/object) and subjective (the way that content presents itself).
    • phenomenalism / knowable interior forms — Dignāga's view that we grasp not external objects directly but internal representations; awareness gives access to these.
    • memory argument — remembering an experienced event recalls the subjective awareness one had of it, not just its objective content (contrast: remembering Aśoka's edicts vs. seeing them erected).
    • regress argument — if awareness required ever-higher reflection, the first-person perspective would be deferred forever; so awareness must be intrinsic.
    • Dharmakīrti — later Buddhist who develops Dignāga's arguments and pushes the theory toward full idealism (denying external objects); "one who does not perceive the awareness of something is not aware of anything at all."
    • Uddyotakara — Nyāya philosopher who took introspective self-awareness (and memory) to prove the reality of an enduring self.
    • Yogācāra — Buddhist idealist tradition (Dharmakīrti's direction) denying external objects, influential in Tibet.
    • Sautrāntika — non-idealist Buddhist tradition that accepts external things yet can still adopt Dignāga's first-person-perspective principle.
    • Birgit Kellner — modern scholar noting Dharmakīrti's assumption that one thing cannot make another known unless it is itself known.
    • no-self / stream of consciousness — Buddhists deny a self but affirm a flowing stream of reflexively self-aware moments; "I am happy/in pain" wrongly fences off one stream as "me," like asking whether a wave is the same wave as before.

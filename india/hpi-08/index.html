    HoPWaG India · Ep 8 — "Case Worker: Pāṇini's Grammar"¶

    ← Ep 7

    Ep 9 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:24 In brief: Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī analyses the whole of Sanskrit through thousands of aphoristic rules — making action (not subject/predicate) the core of a sentence and assigning nouns "thematic roles" (kārakas) — and in doing so the grammarians hand later Indian philosophy a toolbox: the use–mention distinction, double negation, the excluded middle, and an early, oblique version of the problem of universals. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Pāṇini ≈ "Ponanese / panani / Ponini / Pannony / Ponony"; Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Ganari"; Aṣṭādhyāyī ≈ "Ashtad Yahy"; Patañjali ≈ "Patanjali"; Mahābhāṣya ≈ "Mahabashia"; vārttika ≈ "vartica"; bhāṣya ≈ "basya"; kāraka ≈ "caraca / caracca"; Devadatta ≈ "Devadata / De Vedata / divadata"; Bṛhaspati ≈ "Brihaspati"; Naiyāyikas ≈ "Nayayakas"; śiṣṭa ≈ "shishda"; Mīmāṃsā ≈ "Mimamsa"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 7 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019). Its sources & a reading list are under Further reading below.

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Pāṇini — the great Sanskrit grammarian; author of the Aṣṭādhyāyī [00:01:45].
    • The Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Book in Eight Chapters") — his dense, technical grammar of Sanskrit in thousands of aphorisms [00:01:56].
    • Patañjali — author of the Mahābhāṣya, the most important commentary in the grammatical tradition, 2nd century BC [00:03:51].
    • The Naiyāyikas (logicians) — who clash with the grammarians over whether a pot can be an "agent" [00:13:49].
    • The Mīmāṃsā school — foreshadowed at the close: built on the idea that sacred Sanskrit is fixed for all time [00:19:35].

    Language, ritual, and Brahmanic power¶

    • [00:00:41] The power of the Brahmins of ancient India came "not from armies or wealth, but from knowledge" — and was expressed not in wars or building projects but in words and ritual deeds: animal and plant sacrifice, the uttering of chants and formulas.
    • [00:01:02] So intimate was the bond between Brahmanic authority and language that they produced some of the world's oldest literature — literature that constantly refers to language, especially spoken language. The Vedas and Upaniṣads were transmitted orally for many generations before being written down.
    • [00:01:18] Speech is a running theme in the Upaniṣads: the self is said to consist of speech, mind, and breath, and the purifying sacrifice to be enacted through two things, mind and speech. The ritual succeeds only when both are correct — "as a cart cannot roll on only one wheel."
    • [00:01:38] No wonder, then, that ancient India produced one of the great feats in the history of linguistics: the grammar of Pāṇini.

    Pāṇini and the Aṣṭādhyāyī¶

    • [00:01:49] As so often, his dates are uncertain, but he seems to have lived from the 6th to the 5th century BC. His fame rests on the Aṣṭādhyāyī, or "Book in Eight Chapters" — a dense, technical work describing the grammatical structure of Sanskrit through thousands of aphoristic remarks. An astounding achievement, even though it draws on earlier grammarians.
    • [00:02:26] The whole endeavour presupposes a religious context: it is the sacredness of the Sanskrit language that makes it worth analysing in such minute detail, and Pāṇini's most obvious precursors are works discussing Vedic verses word by word.
    • [00:02:44] But you needn't be a historian of religion to appreciate the results. Most scholarship on Pāṇini is by linguists who say nothing about his cultural or religious setting; he is taken seriously as a figure comparable to modern linguists like Noam Chomsky.

    The layers of commentary: sūtra, vārttika, bhāṣya¶

    • [00:03:08] The reception of Pāṇini's work followed a pattern recurring throughout the series: like the philosophers of the various schools, the Pāṇinian grammarians produced layers of commentary on an authoritative source text — here, the Eight Chapters.
    • [00:03:20] Pāṇini's aphorisms were called sūtras — brief remarks to be memorized and passed on orally. Later grammarians added comments called vārttikas, meant to supplement Pāṇini (e.g. by making additional points absent from the original sūtras).
    • [00:03:39] There was also a detailed form of exegesis called bhāṣya, which unfolded the teaching and added illustrative examples. The most important such text was the Mahābhāṣya of Patañjali, written in the 2nd century BC.
    • [00:04:00] Such exegesis generally aimed to explain and expand the founder's remarks — but a bhāṣya could also challenge or correct them.
    • [00:04:11] Patañjali confirms the religious imperative behind the grammar: he writes at the start of his commentary that "grammar should be studied for the sake of the preservation of the Vedas."

    Why a philosopher should care¶

    • [00:04:25] Yet Pāṇini's grammar is no Vedic commentary — it studies the entire Sanskrit language. The grammarians don't necessarily analyse Vedic verses; they attend to everyday examples like "Devadatta is cooking rice."
    • [00:04:44] So why should a philosopher care? Not in hopes of a vision of the universe and the individual's place in it, as in the Upaniṣads. Rather, the grammar is a reflection on the structure and, implicitly, the nature of language.
    • [00:04:54] Patañjali says grammar offers instruction about words — but not the sort children get from parents learning to talk, nor what you'd get in a Sanskrit class. It explains the formation of individual words, yet obviously cannot proceed word by word: that would be never-ending.
    • [00:05:21] Captured by a nice story: the god Bṛhaspati taught the patient Indra about language "for 1,000 years one word at a time," and by then they still hadn't finished. Grammar must instead deploy generalization and abstraction.

    Stand-in abbreviations: variables for language¶

    • [00:05:35] One of Pāṇini's most important breakthroughs is to use stand-in abbreviations to represent a range of possible cases — comparable to how logicians use variables to expose the form of an argument. This lets him state a single rule covering an indefinite number of expressions.

    From the book — ch. 7

    The book pins the comparison historically: Aristotle invented this same variable convention in ancient Greece — but not until "not much later," after Pāṇini.

    • [00:05:59] An English analogy: "take any verb and call it V; then V followed by the ending -ED is its past tense" — deriving walked from walk, cooked from cook. This is pretty much how Pāṇini's grammar works: he builds words out of basic roots with add-ons marking features like the case of a noun.
    • [00:06:26] The procedure will be familiar to anyone who has studied a language with declinable nouns — Greek or Latin, which are related to Sanskrit and share many of the same grammatical features.

    Action at the centre: agent, patient, and the kārakas¶

    • [00:06:41] Even so, some of Pāṇini's proposals are surprising. We're usually taught a sentence's basic components are subject and predicate; Pāṇini instead makes action the core notion. That is why the Eight Chapters are supplemented with a list of all the verbal roots in Sanskrit — the basis of the whole language.
    • [00:07:06] What we'd call the subject is for him the agent of the action. Alongside agent and action, the other key part is the patient of an action — which (for those who know that "declining a noun" doesn't mean refusing to look it up) goes in the accusative case. In "Devadatta is cooking rice," the rice — our "object" — is the patient of cooking.
    • [00:07:42] Further components can be added: the place where the action happens, the instrument used, and so on — "Devadatta is cooking rice in the pot with fire." In Sanskrit one simply marks each noun's function with case endings, where English uses prepositions like in and with.
    • [00:08:09] Insofar as they play different functions, nouns become what Pāṇini calls kārakas — "contributory factors" for some action. Patañjali: "A thing becomes a kāraka with respect to the accomplishment of an action in which it participates." In the example, Devadatta is the kāraka of agent, which is primary and presupposed by all the others, since no action can occur without an agent.
    • [00:08:35] Pāṇini names six such thematic case-roles: agent, patient (or recipient), instrument, donor, target, and locus.
    • "The leaf falls from the tree to the ground": the leaf is the agent (it functions independently), the tree the donor, the ground the locus.
    • "The king gives wealth to the brahman by his own hand": the king is agent, the wealth the object given, the brahman the recipient, and the hand the instrument.
    • [00:09:18] The centrality of action shows in what's excluded: the relation expressed by "of" — the genitive case, as in "the brahman's money" — is not a kāraka. It denotes a relationship between two nouns, not between a verb and a contributory factor.
    • [00:09:42] Hence Pāṇini's kārakas are not simply the grammatical cases of Greek or Latin; they're more like thematic roles that words can play.

    More rules, and rules that override rules¶

    • [00:09:49] Sentences get more complicated. What if the verb is passive ("the rice is being cooked")? What about indeclinable words that take no case endings, or compound words? This is why Pāṇini gives so many rules — guidelines for these and many other circumstances.
    • [00:10:18] In a much-discussed feature, Pāṇini sees that some rules are exceptions to others. There are the most general rules covering all words — which the grammarians compared to "the rain that falls on all alike" — and then more specific rules that trump them.
    • [00:10:37] English parallel: a verb already ending in E adds only D, not ED, for the past tense — liked, shared — "without which no one could describe what they've been doing on Facebook."
    • [00:10:54] To capture this, Pāṇini deliberately places the more general rules earlier in his list of sūtras and specifies that a later entry overrules an earlier one with which it conflicts.

    Descriptive vs. normative: how we talk vs. how we ought to¶

    • [00:11:08] So far it sounds as if Pāṇini merely describes how Sanskrit works — but that isn't the only way to think about grammar. If a friend says "do you want to come with?" — or worse, "do you want to come with Jonardon, Peter, and I?" — a right-thinking person wouldn't just log these as English constructions, even though they're frequent. You'd correct the friend: "come with me," because "with" takes an object.
    • [00:11:44] The ancient Indian grammarians were likewise sticklers, treating their rules as marking correct vs. incorrect language. Their mission was not merely descriptive but prescriptive — philosophers would say normative: not how we do talk, but how we ought to.
    • [00:12:08] Yet they weren't free to decide arbitrarily what counts as correct Sanskrit. As Patañjali said: "If you want pots, go to a potter; but if you want words, don't go to a grammarian" — i.e. go to actual language users and see how they form sentences.

    The śiṣṭa: grammar of the well-educated¶

    • [00:12:26] What's needed is a compromise between the descriptive and the normative: the Pāṇinians describe how people speak, but only when they speak correctly. So they defined a class called the śiṣṭa — the "well-educated" or "learned."
    • [00:12:46] Grammar describes the speech of these people, "not that of the riff-raff on the street." Strikingly, the Pāṇinians held that such elite speakers are distinguished by their upright moral character — not merely their class or level of education.

    From the book — ch. 7

    The book spells out the assumption behind that moral test: the Pāṇinians took speaking correctly to mean thinking correctly, which is why a speaker's character — not just their class or schooling — qualified them as a model of good Sanskrit.

    Can a pot be an agent? Grammarians vs. logicians¶

    • [00:13:03] A telling case. Pāṇini made agents and actions central — so what about "the pot cooks the rice"? One option: rule it incorrect, since it's Devadatta who cooks rice in the pot. But that is not what the grammarians say.
    • [00:13:20] Sanskrit users routinely treat inanimate things as agents — happily saying pots cook rice. We do too: told to get the rice cooker from the cupboard, you don't expect Devadatta stashed inside, but an appliance. The grammarians allowed this and laid down no rule forbidding a pot the kāraka of agent.
    • [00:13:49] This produced a disagreement with the Naiyāyikas (logicians), who insisted that only a living thing deserves the word "agent." Pāṇini's approach was more pragmatic, giving due weight to actual usage: it is correct to say either "Devadatta is cutting the tree" or "the axe is cutting the tree," so the axe must be allowed as an agent — even though we know it is really the instrument of cutting.

    From the book — ch. 7

    The book gives the Sanskrit term the logicians fought over — kartṛ, "agent" — and notes it is the same word used for the agent in the theory of karma from the previous chapter; so the grammar-vs-logic clash over whether a pot can act echoes a question already in play about moral action.

    Syntax vs. semantics: the deepest question¶

    • [00:14:22] Here lies what may be the deepest philosophical question the Pāṇinians raise: does grammar expose the structure of underlying reality, or is it merely the study of linguistic usage that may tell us nothing about the world?
    • [00:14:42] Pāṇini himself doesn't address it — he restricts himself to the rules for forming sentences. But some scholars think he presupposes something deeper beneath surface grammar: the semantic level (meaning), as opposed to the level of syntax (grammatical form).
    • [00:15:08] With this distinction, the pot dispute may be a case of the two sides talking past one another: it is proper to use "pot" in the syntactic role of agent (the grammarians' concern); whether "pot" can actually mean an agent is a different, semantic matter.
    • [00:15:30] Scholars disagree on how much semantics matters in Pāṇini. On one reading, he asks us to look at a word's semantic role to fix its syntactic role: you don't generate a passive just by mechanically tweaking the active form — you go back to square one, see that a passive meaning is wanted, then consult the rulebook for the right ending.
    • [00:15:56] Against this, others argue Pāṇini cares only about constructing words, and out of words sentences, with no interest in semantics. Syntax can be indifferent to meaning — you can form perfectly grammatical sentences that mean nothing.
    • [00:16:20] The grammarians' wonderful example: "There goes the son of a barren woman, with his hair-top bedecked with sky-flower, bathed in the milk of a tortoise, carrying a bow made of the horn of a rabbit."

    Words, meanings, and the lurking problem of universals¶

    • [00:16:32] Later grammarians did attend to how words relate to meanings, if only to stake out grammar's boundaries. Patañjali's example of a cow: the grammarian is not interested in the actual animal "with a tail and hooves" — it is the word that grammar discusses. The word is "that which, when uttered, causes one to think of the cow," the sound by which meaning is understood.
    • [00:17:05] This meaning is permanently linked to the word "cow," and what's meant could be cows in general or some particular cow. Difficult issues lurk: if the meaning is general, must we posit a universal nature of "cowness" for the word to latch onto?
    • [00:17:28] So European philosophers had no monopoly on the problem of universals. The grammarians raised it obliquely, via the function of words; it will be tackled head-on by the Nyāya school, who use the very same cow-and-milk example "for all its worth."

    The grammarians' gift to the philosopher's toolbox¶

    • [00:17:46] We can't credit the grammarians with elaborate reflections on universals, but they forged conceptual instruments that went straight into the Indian philosopher's toolbox.
    • [00:18:03] One, closely connected, is the use–mention distinction: it's one thing to refer to the word cow, another to use the word to refer to cows — the difference between "cow has three letters" / "cow as a noun" and "I see a cow in the field." We mark it today with quotation marks; the ancient grammarians had no such device, yet Pāṇini has nonetheless been credited with drawing the distinction.
    • [00:18:41] Nor were the grammarians blind to logic. Patañjali formulated two basic laws of reasoning: that double negation equals affirmation ("if something is not not a cow, then it is a cow") and that there is no third option between an assertion and its denial — the law of the excluded middle.
    • [00:19:06] That grammarians and logicians could disagree (as over the agency of pots) shows they were "plowing different furrows" in closely adjacent fields.

    Where this leads: looking back to ritual, forward to its critics¶

    • [00:19:23] All this gives the historian of philosophy good reason to take the Pāṇinians seriously — both for their own ideas and as foreshadowing later schools.
    • [00:19:35] For instance, the Mīmāṃsā school will be predicated on the idea that the sacred language of Sanskrit is fixed for all time — a reminder of the religious context that first gave rise to Pāṇini's project, and a reassertion of the grammatical tradition's value, hand in hand with devotion to Brahmanism.
    • [00:19:58] But, as promised repeatedly, the earliest phase of Indian thought was not marked by monolithic agreement on that culture's value. Fittingly, Pāṇini has been "sandwiched" between the Upaniṣads and the critics of Brahmanism. Next time: the greatest of those critics — the legend, legacy, and ideas of the Buddha.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Pāṇini — the great Sanskrit grammarian (6th–5th c. BC?); author of the Aṣṭādhyāyī; makes action the core of grammar and uses variable-like abbreviations to state general rules.
    • Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Eight Chapters") — Pāṇini's grammar of all Sanskrit, in thousands of aphoristic sūtras, supplemented by a list of every verbal root.
    • sūtra — a brief aphoristic rule, memorized and passed on orally; the form of Pāṇini's grammar.
    • vārttika — supplementary comments by later grammarians, adding points absent from the original sūtras.
    • bhāṣya — detailed exegesis that unfolds the teaching with examples, and may even challenge or correct the source.
    • Patañjali — author of the Mahābhāṣya (2nd c. BC), the tradition's most important bhāṣya; ties grammar to "preservation of the Vedas," yet formulates logical laws.
    • Mahābhāṣya — Patañjali's great commentary on Pāṇini.
    • kāraka — a noun's "contributory factor" / thematic role in an action; six in all: agent, patient (recipient), instrument, donor, target, locus. Not the same as Greek/Latin grammatical cases.
    • Devadatta — the stock everyman of the grammarians' examples ("Devadatta is cooking rice").
    • śiṣṭa — the "well-educated/learned," marked by upright moral character; grammar describes their correct speech, not the street's.
    • Naiyāyikas (logicians) — the Nyāya thinkers who, unlike the grammarians, insist only a living thing can be an "agent."
    • syntax vs. semantics — form vs. meaning; the unresolved question of whether grammar exposes reality or only usage.
    • use–mention distinction — referring to a word vs. using it to refer; credited (in seed form) to Pāṇini.
    • double negation / excluded middle — basic laws of reasoning formulated by Patañjali.
    • Mīmāṃsā — school predicated on Sanskrit being fixed for all time; continues the grammar-and-ritual tradition.
    • Bṛhaspati & Indra — the god who taught Indra language "one word at a time" for 1,000 years without finishing — illustrating why grammar needs abstraction.

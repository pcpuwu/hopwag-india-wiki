    HoPWaG India · Ep 20 — "Master of Ceremonies: Jaimini's Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra"¶

    ← Ep 19

    Ep 21 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:26 In brief: The Mīmāṃsā school set out to systematize and defend Vedic ritual, but in doing so it produced a general theory of practical action: Jaimini's Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra grounds all duty (dharma) in the authorless, eternal Veda, makes every action — ritual included — a rule applied in virtue of a desire, and ends by subordinating the human agent to the intrinsic value of the ritual and the text itself. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gennary"; Jaimini ≈ "Jiamini"; Mīmāṃsā ≈ "Mimamsa"; Nyāya / Naiyāyikas ≈ "Niyaya/Neaiacus"; Vātsyāyana ≈ "Vatsyaana"; Maitrāyaṇa Upaniṣad ≈ "Maitrayana Paneshad"; Bādarāyaṇa ≈ "Bararyana"; Śabara ≈ "Shabbara"; Kumārila Bhaṭṭa ≈ "Kumarira Bata"; Prabhākara Miśra ≈ "Prabhakara Misha"; pūrvapakṣin ≈ "Purva Paxin"; siddhāntin ≈ "Siddhantin"; śruti ≈ "Shruti"; ṛṣis ≈ "Rishis").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Mīmāṃsā — the school whose mission is to expound and interpret the Veda so as to establish proper ritual conduct; its generalizable ideas amount to a theory of practical action [00:06:01].
    • Jaimini — author (at least nominally) of the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra, the school's core text [00:08:42].
    • The Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra — longest of the sūtra collections, 2,745 sūtras; founds Pūrva ("prior") Mīmāṃsā [00:08:08].
    • Śabara — the authoritative commentator, centuries later, without whom the obscure sūtra is barely readable [00:12:14].
    • Bādarāyaṇa — named inside the sūtra; also the author of the Vedānta-Sūtra, hinting the two schools are intertwined [00:10:41].

    Ritual as the heart of Brahminical culture — and the target of critics¶

    • [00:00:37] In the Brahminical culture that spawned the coming philosophical schools, nothing was more central than ritual: from a king's spectacular year-long horse sacrifice to everyday offerings of plants or milk into a fire, rituals were how householders propitiated the gods — and the key to the high status of the Brahmin priests.
    • [00:01:02] So it was inevitable that the validity of ritual, and the authority of the Vedic texts on which it rested, would be the central point of dispute between the Brahminical tradition and its critics. For the Buddhists, rituals were nothing special — just another set of actions undertaken to pursue desires we'd be better off without.
    • [00:01:23] Adherents of the Veda called these texts śruti — "what is heard" — a revelatory source of knowledge about the duties laid upon humanity.

    The opponent's three charges: false, inconsistent, repetitive¶

    • [00:01:41] Brahminical texts often record the critics' challenges. The Nyāya-Sūtra, founding text of the logic-minded Nyāya school, sums up the attack: the Vedas cannot command assent because they suffer three epistemological defects — falsity, inconsistency, and repetition.
    • [00:02:12] Falsity: the promises of Vedic ritual aren't always fulfilled. A householder wanting wealth might adapt a boiled-rice ritual by instead boiling 100 gold coins in the water (Adamson notes that someone with 100 gold coins is already doing fine — but set that aside). The sacrificer follows a rule — if you want riches, boil gold coins and offer them — yet may perform it and stay poor, or do a fertility ritual and remain childless.
    • [00:02:53] A possible escape: like the Buddhists and other śramaṇa movements, redirect attention away from concrete goals toward spiritual ones, where the ritual reliably delivers — "or at least where it is harder to tell whether the ritual delivers or not." The Maitrāyaṇa Upaniṣad supplies just such an injunction: one who desires heaven should perform the fire ritual.
    • [00:03:25] But Vātsyāyana, a commentator on the Nyāya-Sūtra, admits the dodge may not be enough: a skeptic can reply that if any ritual injunction in the Veda is false, that brings the Veda's authority as a whole into disrepute. "If you can't rely on the Vedas to get kids and gold, why think they'll get you into heaven?"
    • [00:03:48] Inconsistency and repetition seem less damning, and they worry the Naiyāyikas just as much. The Veda says both after sunrise, perform the sacrifice and the sacrifice performed after sunrise goes to the dogs. And it says the same thing again and again — "the rambling of a drunkard." Implicitly the critic invokes rules of rational, trustworthy discourse — fit the facts, be consistent, say what's needed and no more — and charges that the Vedas violate them.
    • [00:04:24] Tellingly, this line of attack appears most sharply in the logic- and rationality-obsessed Nyāya tradition, raised as an objection against their own position. Actual Buddhist critique of ritual focused more on the violence of animal sacrifices; the Naiyāyikas may even have invented this objection themselves.

    The Naiyāyikas' replies¶

    • [00:04:53] Defending the rationality of their sacred text, the Naiyāyikas answer each charge. Failure: rituals sometimes fail, but when they do it's because of faulty execution by the sacrificer, not a defect in the rules governing the ritual.
    • [00:05:10] Inconsistency: apparently conflicting rules simply apply to different situations. The after-sunrise sacrifice "goes to the dogs" only if you had previously resolved to perform it before sunrise.
    • [00:05:21] Repetition: it's sometimes useful — like reminding a mother in labour, repeatedly, to breathe and push (Adamson leaves it to mothers to judge how helpful that is).

    Why a school was needed: Mīmāṃsā's mission¶

    • [00:05:38] Even the staunchest defender had to admit the Veda is a problematic text: beyond the three difficulties, rituals are often described in insufficient detail. Some rational method was needed to systematize the Vedic injunctions and to proceed in the face of incomplete information.
    • [00:06:01] This was the ambition of the Mīmāṃsā, a school whose mission was to expound and interpret the Veda in hopes of establishing proper ritual conduct. What has this to do with philosophy? Its ideas can be generalized from the ritual context into a general theory of practical action.

    Reasoning by analogy: transfer, adaptation, "rice in the pan"¶

    • [00:06:25] Mīmāṃsā deals with gaps in the Veda by reasoning from one case to analogous cases. When a ritual is under-described, the remedy is transfer, adaptation, and annulment of details: borrow the details of a similar "model" ritual for the one you must perform. Adamson's analogy — replacing a car battery for the first time: you know what to do because you've done it on other cars and can adapt to this design. (The gold-for-rice swap was already an example.)
    • [00:07:13] The logicians called this golden rule of Vedic interpretation "rice in the pan" reasoning: like tasting one grain to judge whether all are cooked, you transfer a property from one thing (the tasted grain) to others (the rest).
    • [00:07:33] Strikingly, Mīmāṃsā applies "rice in the pan" reasoning to duties — dharma in the ritual context. Since dharma has a broader moral connotation too, we can extrapolate to ethical reasoning as a whole: we know the right thing to do in a new situation because, for all its specificity, it resembles other situations where we knew what to do — and the Indian tradition encourages exactly this extrapolation.

    Dharma depends on the Veda — the only guide we have¶

    • [00:08:09] The core text, the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra, declares: dharma depends upon the Veda, so whatever is not Veda is not to be trusted. But how, if the Veda is at best an incomplete guide? Because incomplete or not, it is the only guide we have: most knowledge comes (directly or indirectly) from sense experience, but our senses tell us nothing about morality and ritual — there, the Veda is our sole source.
    • [00:08:42] Jaimini frames the opening sentence of his sūtra by casting the inquiry into Vedic exegesis as an inquiry into dharma.
    • [00:08:52] To this conviction Mīmāṃsā adds another: the world has always been as we see it now. The Veda has always existed and always governed ritual, so we can trust that the rituals handed down to us have a basis in the Veda even when that basis is obscure.
    • [00:09:12] And this authority isn't parochial: the Veda's authority in matters of dharma is not just unchallenged but universal. When the sūtra asks whether a ritual described as applying in one region should be restricted to it, the answer is no — by its very nature, dharma applies in all places and all times.

    The dialogue form: pūrvapakṣin vs. siddhāntin¶

    • [00:09:42] This is typical of the sūtra's structure, which becomes a model for many later texts. It often reads as a dialogue between two voices: the pūrvapakṣin, who states a prima facie view, and the siddhāntin, who overrules him with the decisive, considered position.
    • [00:10:06] The siddhāntin's view coincides with the sūtra's own — tempting to call him the voice of Jaimini, though (as usual) the authorship is historically uncertain. The date is already problematic: perhaps composed c. 4th–2nd century BC, possibly in stages and by more than one author.

    Two branches of Mīmāṃsā: Jaimini and Bādarāyaṇa¶

    • [00:10:35] The sūtra explicitly names earlier and contemporary thinkers, including Bādarāyaṇa — also the name of the author of the Vedānta-Sūtra. So at the very outset we see the Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta schools intertwined; they're sometimes called two branches of Mīmāṃsā.
    • [00:10:53] Jaimini's sūtra founds prior, or Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā; Bādarāyaṇa's Vedānta is posterior, or Uttara-Mīmāṃsā. Jaimini and his followers focus on the original Vedas and their ritual prescriptions; Vedānta means "at the end of the Veda," an allusion to the more obviously philosophical material in the Upaniṣads. Either way, the school stages an inquiry into the underlying principles of its texts — which is what "mīmāṃsā" means: an inquiry into the doubtful or difficult aspects of the Vedic tradition.

    A formidable text and its commentators¶

    • [00:11:36] The Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra is the longest of the sūtra collections of this age — a massive 2,745 sūtras, against (for instance) only 555 in the Vedānta-Sūtra. And it's not just the size: crack it open and you immediately need help. Like other sūtra collections it's difficult and obscure, "perhaps not even meant to be read without an accompanying commentary."
    • [00:12:14] Thank goodness, then, for Śabara, who lived several centuries after the sūtra's composition; his commentary is authoritative for all Mīmāṃsakas. Around the 7th century AD the school split into two rival factions named for two further interpreters, Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Prabhākara Miśra. As the layers of text and commentary accumulate, things grow more sophisticated and philosophical — partly from the need to answer rival schools — but there's already plenty of interest in Jaimini, best understood with Śabara's help.

    Rules about rules: the analysis of action¶

    • [00:12:54] Unlike earlier works of Vedic exegesis, Jaimini operates at an abstract, general level. He's interested in the rules governing rituals, but expresses that interest above all by considering the rules that govern those rules (as with transferring rules between rituals). When he mentions specific practices, it's usually only to illustrate his overall theory.
    • [00:13:20] At the theory's heart is an analysis of human action. Rituals are special actions, but actions nonetheless, so to understand them we explore the mechanisms of action generally. Whenever you do something, you are at least implicitly following a rule or injunction, which applies to you because of some desire you have. In the mood for risotto, you apply the rule: if you want rice for dinner, cook the rice in boiling water.
    • [00:13:56] In general the causal situation involves four elements: an agent with a desire, the object/advantage desired, the instrument by which it's achieved, and the procedure. For risotto: you (agent), the rice (object), cooking (instrument), and the whole sequence — gathering firewood, drawing water, and so on (procedure). For one who desires riches should boil gold coins: the sacrificer (agent), wealth (object/goal), the ritual sacrifice (instrument), and the sacrificial steps (procedure).

    No desire, no duty — the controversial consequences¶

    • [00:14:35] This looks straightforward, but recalling the discussions of Buddhism and the Bhagavad-Gītā shows it's philosophically controversial: Jaimini claims that any action presupposes desire in the one who performs it. Instead of giving up all desire, or detaching from the outcome, you should act by following the rules that apply to you in virtue of your desires.
    • [00:15:01] It seems surprising that Jaimini makes participation in Vedic ritual so contingent on the sacrificer's goals. Don't we have a standing obligation to perform the rites? The answer is no: there is no pure duty to perform the Vedic rites, for without desire the injunction to perform a ritual simply does not apply.
    • [00:15:27] Jaimini goes so far as to say that if a sacrificer loses interest in the outcome partway through, he should just stop, leaving the ritual incomplete.
    • [00:15:38] To the counter that some Vedic rituals specify no intended outcome, Jaimini replies that in such cases heaven serves as the default object of desire — for heaven is happiness, and happiness is desired by all.
    • [00:15:54] Another consequence: women are invited to participate, and not merely as their husbands' living instruments — their involvement is justified on the basis that they too have desires.

    The twist: ritual uses the agent, not the reverse¶

    • [00:16:08] You might think ritual is being subordinated to human whims — but in fact the reverse is true. For Jaimini, what has ultimate, intrinsic value is the ritual itself. One might say the ritual uses the human agent, rather than the other way around: the sacrificer and his desire merely provide the occasion for the ritual. Without human goals no ritual would occur — but the ritual's value isn't exhausted by fulfilling those goals; it exists for its own sake.
    • [00:16:38] A dramatic illustration: it was laid down that if a sacrificer dies partway through, his bones could be placed inside an antelope skin, and this "posthumous puppet" made to carry out the remaining steps.
    • [00:16:55] Francis Clooney sums up Jaimini's view: by seeing himself as only one element in the web of relationships, the sacrificer transcends his self-centeredness even as he admits it, letting it lead him to the sacrifice; he learns to play his part without worrying about ridding himself of the desires that integrate him into the larger whole.

    Mīmāṃsā vs. Buddhism — and the quiet absorption of its rival¶

    • [00:17:20] The Mīmāṃsā viewpoint is diametrically opposed to Buddhism in its total commitment to the value of ritual, the authority of the Veda, and its endorsement of human desire. Yet it also captures something of Buddhism's spirit, and may be responding to the Buddhist challenge by absorbing some of its ideas:
    • a shift away from concrete goals (money, family) toward heaven as a universal aim;
    • not the elimination of desire, but the humbling of the individual's desire to a mere contributing factor in the ritual;
    • most strikingly, an elimination of the supernatural — the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra does not tell us to sacrifice to please the gods or out of absolute religious obligation. Ritual is treated as parallel to humdrum everyday action: if the goal is risotto, consult a cookbook; if the goal has to do with dharma, consult the Veda.

    The boldest claim: the Veda has no author¶

    • [00:18:26] Though scholars often call the Veda revealed, Mīmāṃsā insists it was not revealed by anyone. The Veda has no author, and the seers (ṛṣis) who expounded it are not prophets like those of the Old Testament — they simply transmit an authoritative Veda that existed before them and has always existed.
    • [00:18:50] Again human agency is sidelined, subordinated to the intrinsic value of the Veda just as it is to the ritual. The forefathers who passed on wisdom and reliable practices were admirable and trustworthy, but they did not invent or compose what they handed on. The most a human can do is be a conduit from the past to the future — never a conduit between the divine and humanity, as the Hebrew prophets or Muhammad were. No such conduit is needed: the Veda has always been here, "a permanent source of knowledge set down by neither God nor man."
    • [00:19:32] With this bold claim Mīmāṃsā makes the strongest possible case for Vedic authority: far from false, the Veda is a truth both unimpeachable and originary. But the bolder the claim, the greater the pressure to defend it — and Jaimini already draws the astounding conclusion that if the Veda is authorless and permanent, then language too must be permanent.

    Where we go next¶

    • [00:20:03] Jaimini's commentators provide a further defense of language's permanence and a sophisticated theory of knowledge meant to explain exactly why the Veda's authority is beyond challenge. Those are the next topics — the school justifying religious ritual by philosophical argument rather than divine authority.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Mīmāṃsā — school dedicated to expounding and interpreting the Veda to fix proper ritual conduct; the word means "inquiry into the doubtful/difficult aspects" of the Vedic tradition; its method generalizes into a theory of practical action.
    • Jaimini — author (nominally) of the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra; frames the inquiry into Vedic exegesis as an inquiry into dharma, and analyzes ritual as a species of desire-driven action.
    • Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra — the school's core text; longest of the sūtra collections (2,745 sūtras vs. 555 in the Vedānta-Sūtra); obscure, requiring commentary; structured as a dialogue, datable perhaps c. 4th–2nd century BC.
    • dharma — duty/moral law; depends entirely on the Veda, which is our only source of knowledge about it; applies in all places and all times.
    • śruti — "what is heard"; the Veda as revelatory knowledge of duty (though Mīmāṃsā denies it had any revealer).
    • "rice in the pan" reasoning — the logicians' name for Vedic interpretation by analogy: transfer a property from one case (a tasted grain) to others (the rest of the rice); applied even to duties.
    • transfer / adaptation / annulment — Mīmāṃsā's procedures for filling gaps in under-described rituals by borrowing details from a model ritual.
    • pūrvapakṣin / siddhāntin — the two voices of the sūtra: the one who states the prima facie view and the one who delivers the decisive verdict (the latter speaking for the text itself).
    • Pūrva- vs. Uttara-Mīmāṃsā — "prior" Mīmāṃsā (Jaimini, on Vedic ritual) vs. "posterior" Mīmāṃsā = Vedānta (Bādarāyaṇa, on the Upaniṣads, "the end of the Veda"); two branches of one inquiry.
    • Bādarāyaṇa — named within Jaimini's sūtra; author of the Vedānta-Sūtra; evidence the two schools are intertwined.
    • Śabara — the authoritative commentator (centuries later) without whom the sūtra is barely intelligible to all Mīmāṃsakas.
    • Kumārila Bhaṭṭa & Prabhākara Miśra — interpreters for whom the two rival Mīmāṃsā factions are named after the school split around the 7th century AD.
    • Nyāya-Sūtra / Naiyāyikas — the logic-obsessed school whose founding text records (perhaps invents) the three charges against the Veda — falsity, inconsistency, repetition — and answers them; Vātsyāyana is its commentator.
    • theory of action — Jaimini's claim that every action implicitly follows a rule applying to you in virtue of a desire; its four elements are agent, object, instrument, procedure; hence no desire, no duty (no standing obligation to perform the rites), with heaven as the default desired object.
    • authorless, eternal Veda — Mīmāṃsā's boldest doctrine: the Veda was composed by neither God nor man and has always existed; the ṛṣis only transmit it — implying language too is permanent.

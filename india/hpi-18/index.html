    HoPWaG India · Ep 18 — "A Tangled Web: The Age of the Sūtra"¶

    ← Ep 17

    Ep 19 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:23:54 In brief: A road-map episode for the next stretch of the series. Adamson rejects the tidy "orthodox vs. heterodox / six Hindu systems vs. Buddhists & Jains" scheme and proposes a more neutral organising principle: the age of the sūtra — centuries when philosophy crystallised around terse, thread-like aphorisms (sūtra) that demanded commentaries (bhāṣya) to unpack them. It closes with a whistle-stop tour of all six systems. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gennary"; sūtra ≈ "sutra"; bhāṣya ≈ "basya/Batya"; Nyāya ≈ "Naya/Niyaya"; Vaiśeṣika ≈ "Vaishaika/Vichesha/Vysheysheishika"; Mīmāṃsā ≈ "Mimansa/Mimamsa"; tantra ≈ "warp/tantra"; Vātsyāyana ≈ "Butz Yayana"; Pāṇini ≈ "ponenie/Panani"; Patañjali ≈ "Patanjali"; Pāyāsi ≈ "Paiasi"; Bṛhaspati ≈ "Prihaspati"; Cārvāka ≈ "Charvaca/Chavaka"; Gautama / Akṣapāda ≈ "Akhapada"; pramāṇa ≈ "pramana"; Pūrvapakṣa & Siddhānta ≈ "Purva Paksha and Sidanta"; Śramaṇa ≈ "Shramana"; Ājīvika ≈ "Ajivaka"; Aśoka ≈ "Ashoka/Aschaka"; Ikṣvāku ≈ "Ichvacu"; Rudradāman ≈ "Rudradaman"; Prākrit ≈ "Prachrit/Prachrit"; Kaṇāda ≈ "Kanada"; Jaimini ≈ "Jaimani/Jiamini"; Śabara ≈ "Shavara"; Kumārila Bhaṭṭa ≈ "Kumarela Bhata"; Prabhākara ≈ "Prabhakara"; Bādarāyaṇa ≈ "Badarayana"; Vyāsa ≈ "Vyasa"; Īśvarakṛṣṇa ≈ "Ishvara Krishna"; Sāṃkhya-kārikā ≈ "Samkhya Karika"; Madhyamaka ≈ "Madyamika"; Nāgārjuna ≈ "Nagajuna").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • The six systems (darśanas) — Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta, the so-called "orthodox" schools, here re-described as the sūtra-writing traditions [00:01:21].
    • sūtra & bhāṣya — the "thread"-like aphorism and the commentary that unfolds it; the organising literary form of the whole period [00:03:35].
    • Pāṇini & Patañjali — the grammarian's Aṣṭādhyāyī (sūtras) and the 2nd-c. CE Mahābhāṣya (commentary) supply the model for every later school [00:03:58].
    • Cārvāka / Bṛhaspati's Cārvāka-Sūtra — a sūtra-based tradition outside the supposed orthodox mainstream, materialist heir to the older naturalist Pāyāsi [00:08:06].
    • Rudradāman's inscription (c. 150 CE) — the marker for Sanskrit's rise to dominance as the language of philosophy [00:11:15].
    • Gautama, Kaṇāda, Jaimini, Bādarāyaṇa, Patañjali, Īśvarakṛṣṇa — the founding (or attributed) authors of the six systems, surveyed in the closing roll-call [00:16:40].

    It's at this point that things get complicated¶

    • [00:00:36] The episode is titled "A Tangled Web." Up to now Indian intellectual life has been lively; from here on we face such a proliferation of texts, movements, schools and rivalries that it may be hard to keep them all straight. Historians have stepped in with ways of organising the mass of material.
    • [00:00:59] The standard scheme is a basic split between orthodox and heterodox movements, the orthodox ones growing out of the Brahminic culture of the Vedas. On the orthodox side historians count six systems that took up ideas from the Upaniṣads, parts of the epics, the Dharma-Sūtras and the Laws of Manu.
    • [00:01:21] The six: Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā, and Vedānta. Collectively they are made to stand for "the Hindu perspective," which is then challenged by the sceptical, relativist heterodox thinkers — basically the Buddhists and Jains, who opposed orthodox theories of ritual, metaphysics and epistemology.

    Why the orthodox/heterodox scheme fails¶

    • [00:01:45] The series already voiced dissatisfaction with this neat account. The most obvious problem: it reflects the Hindu point of view itself. "Orthodox" literally means right-believing — so framing it this way is almost like saying there were two kinds of Indian philosophers, "the good guys and the other guys."
    • [00:02:08] It's also problematic to call the six schools Hindu at all. The series has loosely used "Hindu" for works expressing traditional Brahminic and Kṣatriya values (e.g. calling the Mahābhārata a Hindu epic), but applying the label to such early texts is a contentious bit of retrospective labelling.
    • [00:02:28] "Orthodoxy" also implies an unrealistic agreement among the six. True, some movements eventually grow together: in the 11th or 12th century Vaiśeṣika and Nyāya effectively merge, so scholars can speak of a single Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika philosophy, and there's an intimate association of Sāṃkhya with Yoga. But it would hardly be worth distinguishing six systems if they all coalesced into one Hindu outlook.
    • [00:03:05] In fact there were prolonged disputes between the so-called orthodox schools, and even within individual schools, which split into sub-traditions — much as the Jains divided into the white-clad and sky-clad factions. So the picture of a unified orthodoxy doesn't hold.

    A more neutral contrast: the age of the sūtra¶

    • [00:03:29] Instead, Adamson proposes a more ideologically neutral distinction, based on how the texts were actually written. The coming episodes cover what he calls the age of the sūtra — a period stretching over the first several centuries CE, when philosophy centred on interpreting compressed, lapidary statements called sūtras, literally "threads."
    • [00:03:52] These sūtras were so pregnant with meaning that commentary was needed to deliver it fully. The explanatory texts are called bhāṣyas. We've already met this with Pāṇini's Sanskrit grammar, written as sūtras, which received an influential bhāṣya from the 2nd-century CE grammarian Patañjali.
    • [00:04:15] The age of the sūtra is also the age in which Sanskrit, the language of the Vedas, becomes dominant across philosophical writing. So it's apt — and presumably no coincidence — that Pāṇini's eight chapters provided the model for how texts would be written: each school traced its ideas to a founding text in sūtra form, made the subject of authoritative commentary in a bhāṣya.
    • [00:04:45] The pairing of sūtra with bhāṣya was so ingrained that in some cases a single author wrote both the aphorisms and his own commentary on them.

    Form shapes content¶

    • [00:05:00] It may seem perverse to stress the format: isn't content more important than form? But we shouldn't underestimate how the form of the text shapes the content and how readers understand it. Philosophy in this period is moving away from the exhortations and evocations of the earlier "philosophies of path and purpose" toward systematic inquiry — and this goes hand in hand with writing bhāṣya commentaries.
    • [00:05:35] A good bhāṣya uncovered the principles and arguments implicit in the sūtras: exposing their coherence and completeness, and showing how the ideas could be justified and defended against rivals' criticisms.
    • [00:05:51] The commentator's job is to weave the threads into a unified conceptual web — which the commentators themselves called a tantra, literally "warp" (the weaving kind, "not the kind involved in Star Trek"). Vātsyāyana, author of a major commentary on the Nyāya-Sūtra, explained that a tantra is "a system consisting in the statement of a collection of interrelated ideas."
    • [00:06:18] Commentators also describe their task as amplification or expansion of the sūtra. In a more Star Trek-friendly metaphor: a sūtra is like an uncompressed (i.e. compressed) data file that needs decompressing — its meaning unfolded by the commentator who adds the bhāṣya.
    • [00:06:42] This was no easy task. From Pāṇini's eight chapters on, sūtras typically look like nothing more than lists — a series of isolated statements with no organising principle. It was the commentator's job to impose — or, as he'd say, reveal — an intelligible structure: identify the chief overall theme, then divide the sūtras by sub-theme into groups, turning a mere list into a network of ideas and arguments with clear interrelationships.
    • [00:07:11] The goal was not to provide an easier-to-follow replacement for the sūtra, but to guide the reader through the original. Like the Greek commentaries on Plato and Aristotle in the same historical period, the bhāṣyas have a fundamentally pedagogical purpose.

    The fault line: almost, but not quite, the old one¶

    • [00:07:35] This focus on the sūtra form almost divides the terrain along the same fault line as the old orthodox/heterodox contrast: the six systems all used the sūtra-bhāṣya style, whereas the Buddhists did not.
    • [00:07:57] But the division isn't exactly the same, because there were other sūtra-based movements outside the supposed orthodox Hindu mainstream — in particular Cārvāka philosophy. Cārvāka is not one of the six, yet it has its own foundational document, Bṛhaspati's Cārvāka-Sūtra.
    • [00:08:19] A warning: Bṛhaspati did not emerge from nowhere ("boldly going where no one had gone before"). He continued an older tradition of naturalism begun by Pāyāsi, probably a contemporary of the Buddha. And that's typical: each founding text is ascribed to a founding father — Gautama initiating Nyāya with his Nyāya-Sūtra, Patañjali beginning Yoga with his Yoga-Sūtra, and so on.
    • [00:08:55] We must reckon that these supposed foundations were themselves built out of found materials. We usually think of sūtras as the oldest canonical texts of a tradition, but it's safer to call them the oldest surviving texts that came to be recognised as canonical — sūtras may in part be compilations based on still older, now-lost literature.

    Why "orthodox" and "heterodox" leak into each other¶

    • [00:09:20] This also explains a feature that doesn't fit the orthodox/heterodox antithesis: the agenda and terminology of the "unorthodox" traditions keep turning up in orthodox literature. Turn to the Yoga-Sūtra and you find the idea of escaping the cycle of rebirth by eliminating all desire — obviously an echo of Buddhism.
    • [00:09:48] This is unsurprising. Earlier, a gap had opened between Brahminic culture and the Śramaṇa movements — the renouncers who stressed an ascetic path to liberation: Buddhists, Jains, and Ājīvikas. But, "as in a good history of philosophy," that gap was also being closed: motifs like non-attached action and non-violence appeared on both sides.
    • [00:10:16] At the political level, rulers paid respect to sages of both persuasions. The standout is Aśoka, best known for his attraction to Buddhism but who seems to have seen himself as transcending sectarian division — the exception that proved the rule. Later rulers kept bestowing favour on both Brahminic and dissident groups: toward the end of this age, the southern Ikṣvāku dynasty had female members giving gifts to Buddhists while the male kings carried out Vedic sacrifices.

    Sanskrit takes over — the Rudradāman inscription¶

    • [00:10:59] Archaeologists are grateful that later rulers followed Aśoka in another respect: putting up stone inscriptions to communicate with their subjects. There are even cases where a king added his own inscriptions alongside Aśoka's words.
    • [00:11:17] This was done around 150 CE for the king Rudradāman, and what he carved is telling. It shows a continued alliance of royalty and philosophy: Rudradāman boasts that, on top of martial prowess and good looks, he has "knowledge and practice of grammar, music, logic, and other great sciences."
    • [00:11:41] More interesting than what he says is the language: namely Sanskrit, whereas Aśoka's inscriptions are mostly in Prākrit. This is the stage at which Sanskrit establishes itself as the main language you need to read the primary sources of Indian philosophy. The Buddha himself spoke Prākrit, and the Buddhist canon was composed in Pali — but going forward, almost all philosophical literature is in Sanskrit.
    • [00:12:09] For Brahminic authors, Sanskrit — the language of revelation and of Pāṇini's grammar — has a unique status: it alone is eternal. Buddhists never accept this exceptionalist story, but they nonetheless begin to write in the language of their Brahminic adversaries.

    Adversarial structure: Pūrvapakṣa and Siddhānta¶

    • [00:12:33] Adversarial relationships are crucial to philosophy in this age. From the description of sūtra-plus-bhāṣya as a self-contained web, you might imagine these texts resemble the Elements of Euclid more than the dialectical exchanges of Plato's dialogues or Aristotle's treatises. But because the authors are sensitive to challenges from other schools, they constantly raise potential objections and supply reasoned answers.
    • [00:13:02] In Sanskrit these are Pūrvapakṣa (the prior view / objection) and Siddhānta (the established conclusion / reply). In this respect the literature keeps something of the dialogue structure that marked the Upaniṣads, the philosophical sections of the Mahābhārata, and many stories of the Buddha in the Pali canon.

    Not all six are equally "systematic"¶

    • [00:13:33] A final caution about the "six systems": they are not equally systematic. The label fits Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika well, but Sāṃkhya and Yoga less so — these are not so much philosophical systems as bodies of philosophical method, recalling the "philosophies of path and purpose" of the earlier period.
    • [00:13:57] Though Sāṃkhya and Yoga do have canonical sūtras, those were composed retrospectively, to give the traditions the same air of authority and lineage other schools claimed. The Sāṃkhya-Sūtra in fact dates from as late as the 14th century.

    The main topics of debate¶

    • [00:14:14] What are the sūtras and commentaries actually arguing about? Again there's continuity with the earlier period. One dominant question: is there a self underlying all cognition and experience? The Buddhist denies it, Brahminic culture asserts it.
    • [00:14:34] But beware oversimplification. Within Buddhism there are disagreements about how to account for the survival of one and the same person across time and even across incarnations. And on the "orthodox" side there are major differences: the Mīmāṃsā school develops the self as an empirically engaged agent, while other traditions hold the self to be a pure consciousness that remains aloof from cognition and agency.
    • [00:15:03] The means by which the self comes to know the world and its own nature is much debated, with the Nyāya school making especially powerful contributions to epistemology. Against such positive accounts, a more sceptical note is struck within Buddhism and Jainism — the Jains developing the remarkable idea that truth depends on one's standpoint, what we'd call a perspectivist approach.
    • [00:15:34] In metaphysics too there are fundamental disagreements: the Vaiśeṣika school tries to establish six fundamental categories of reality, against rival views including the naturalism of the Cārvākas.
    • [00:15:52] Much action is in philosophy of mind, epistemology and metaphysics, but thinkers don't drop the earlier concern with ethics and salvation. The brilliant Nāgārjuna, founder of the Madhyamaka school within Buddhism, is the example: best known for the radical doctrine of emptiness, he never loses sight that the goal of accepting this doctrine (or "non-doctrine") is freedom from suffering.

    Roll-call of the schools — Nyāya¶

    • [00:16:28] The episode closes with a quick rundown — "probably too much to take in all at once," but useful to refer back to. Nyāya first: its foundational Nyāya-Sūtra was probably compiled in the first or second century CE. The traditional compiler is Gautama, sometimes called Akṣapāda — "one whose eyes are in his feet" — apparently a reference to the foundationalism and empiricism of his system.
    • [00:16:58] Gautama forcefully advocates the pramāṇa method for rational inquiry, a pramāṇa being a way of gaining knowledge or a principle of justification. For him all knowledge rests on three main sources — perception, inference, and testimony — plus a fourth, comparison, whose exact meaning is unclear.
    • [00:17:22] The first and fifth chapters of the Nyāya-Sūtra concentrate on debate techniques, and may originally have been a separate text. So Nyāya's main issues: the nature of philosophical inquiry, the epistemology of the sources of knowledge, and the theory of debate.

    Vaiśeṣika and Mīmāṃsā¶

    • [00:17:47] The allied Vaiśeṣika system has as its canonical text Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra, which may have been composed as early as 100 BCE. Its main claim to fame is a six-category metaphysics that influenced thinkers across the spectrum of Indian philosophy.
    • [00:18:07] Mīmāṃsā was in the first instance a system of Vedic hermeneutics — concerned with interpreting and clarifying the Vedas, defending Vedic authority, and analysing the meaning of ritual injunctions. Its foundational text is the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra, hard to date: perhaps it existed in some form around 400 BCE, with compilation of the version we have over the following centuries.
    • [00:18:42] The Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra is traditionally attributed to Jaimini, while the bhāṣya was written by Śabara around 400 CE. Though focused on Vedic interpretation, this led Mīmāṃsā to a much wider range of issues; it shared a broadly realist epistemology with Nyāya and contributed especially to the theory of perception. In the 7th century the school split into two sub-schools, following Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Prabhākara respectively.

    Vedānta¶

    • [00:19:15] Jaimini's Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra has a twin in Bādarāyaṇa's Vedānta-Sūtra, also called the Brahma-Sūtra. The two authors were contemporaries, with a suggestion of some collaboration.
    • [00:19:33] The division of labour: Jaimini focuses on the ritual formulations of the Vedas and the concept of dharma (duty, the actions one ought to perform) — hence the school's name, mīmāṃsā meaning "investigation," the investigation that illuminates ritual prescriptions.
    • [00:19:53] Bādarāyaṇa's motivation is also in the name: Vedānta means "what comes after the Vedas" — that is, the Upaniṣads. So the school's themes are the Upaniṣadic ones: cosmic unity, brahman, and the quest to discover brahman within one's own self.
    • [00:20:18] The Vedānta-Sūtra also illustrates inter-school rivalry: its four chapters defend the view that brahman is the central concern of scripture and that there is only one consistent interpretation of it — set against the Vaiśeṣika school, and with Buddhist and Jain views subjected to refutation. It also gives an extended description of the spiritual exercises leading one to comprehend one's identity with brahman.

    Yoga and Sāṃkhya¶

    • [00:20:54] That reference to spiritual exercises leads to the Yoga-Sūtra, attributed to the mythical Vyāsa — though more likely both it and the Yoga-bhāṣya were written by a Patañjali, probably not the grammarian who commented on Pāṇini.
    • [00:21:13] This Patañjali itemises the elements needed to subdue mental disturbance: restraint and observance (restraint from injuring others, stealing, lying, greed; observance of cleanliness, purification, study), then posture, breath-regulation, abstraction of the senses, concentration, meditation, and trance. Backing this is a detailed theory of mind distinguishing the unchanging self from the states that make up a mental life. The Yoga school became theistic, with God entering as a self of this kind — unaffected by suffering or actions.
    • [00:21:55] Providing further theoretical backbone to spiritual discipline, yet without any obvious initial theism, is Sāṃkhya — whose sūtra, as noted, came only much later. Its real foundational document is Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṃkhya-kārikā ("verses on Sāṃkhya").
    • [00:22:13] Īśvarakṛṣṇa insists on a strict dualism between the self and nature/matter. Out of original matter the material world evolves, on the principle that an effect pre-exists in its cause. Strikingly, what evolves first is what we'd call mental phenomena: first intellect, then sense of self, then mind, then the five domains of sense experience and the senses, and finally the five material elements — air, light, water, earth, and ether. All this is part of nature, with the true self transcending all natural evolution.

    Where we go next¶

    • [00:22:53] Along with the Cārvāka naturalists, these six schools will occupy the next ~20 episodes. After surveying them, it will be time to turn to the critical voices among the Buddhists and Jains.
    • [00:23:18] But since sceptical worries form such an important part of the context, the series will begin by exploring the emergence of philosophical doubts about the efficacy of action — especially Vedic ritual — and the possibility of knowledge. Sceptical arguments were put forward both against and within the schools. Next time: picking up the threads in the age of the sūtra.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • sūtra — a compressed, lapidary aphorism; literally a "thread." Each school traces itself to a founding text in this form; sūtras often look like bare lists.
    • bhāṣya — the authoritative commentary that "unfolds" a sūtra's meaning, weaving it into a structured, defensible system; pedagogical in purpose, like Greek commentaries on Plato and Aristotle.
    • tantra — the unified "web" of interrelated ideas a commentator weaves from the sūtra-threads; literally the "warp" of weaving (per Vātsyāyana on the Nyāya-Sūtra).
    • Pūrvapakṣa / Siddhānta — the objection (prior view) and the established reply; the built-in adversarial, dialogue structure of sūtra-age texts.
    • orthodox / heterodox — the rejected scheme: six "right-believing" Hindu systems vs. Buddhists and Jains. Faulted as Hindu-biased, anachronistic, and overstating agreement.
    • the six systems (darśanas) — Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta; re-described here as the sūtra-bhāṣya-writing traditions (though not all equally "systematic").
    • Cārvāka — materialist/naturalist tradition outside the six, with Bṛhaspati's Cārvāka-Sūtra, heir to the earlier naturalist Pāyāsi (a contemporary of the Buddha).
    • Śramaṇa movements — the renouncer traditions (Buddhists, Jains, Ājīvikas) stressing asceticism and liberation; their motifs (non-attachment, non-violence) leak into Brahminic texts.
    • Sanskrit vs. Prākrit/Pali — Sanskrit becomes the dominant philosophical language (Rudradāman's c. 150 CE inscription is the marker); for Brahmins it "alone is eternal," whereas the Buddha spoke Prākrit and the canon was in Pali.
    • Nyāya — Gautama (Akṣapāda)'s Nyāya-Sūtra (1st–2nd c. CE); epistemology of the pramāṇas (perception, inference, testimony, + comparison) and theory of debate.
    • Vaiśeṣika — Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra (perhaps c. 100 BCE); a six-category metaphysics; later merges with Nyāya (11th–12th c.).
    • Mīmāṃsā — "investigation"; Jaimini's Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra (bhāṣya by Śabara, c. 400 CE); Vedic hermeneutics, ritual, dharma, theory of perception; splits into Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Prabhākara sub-schools (7th c.).
    • Vedānta — "what comes after the Vedas" (the Upaniṣads); Bādarāyaṇa's Vedānta-Sūtra / Brahma-Sūtra; brahman, cosmic unity, identity of self with brahman; polemical against rivals.
    • Yoga — Yoga-Sūtra attributed to Vyāsa, more likely a Patañjali (not the grammarian); discipline to arrest mental disturbance + a theory of the unchanging self; became theistic.
    • Sāṃkhya — Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṃkhya-kārikā; strict self/matter dualism; the world evolves from primal matter (effect pre-exists in cause), mental phenomena evolving before the five elements.
    • Nāgārjuna / Madhyamaka — founder of the Madhyamaka school; the doctrine (or "non-doctrine") of emptiness, whose aim remains freedom from suffering.
    • pramāṇa — a means of gaining knowledge / principle of justification; central to Nyāya's program.

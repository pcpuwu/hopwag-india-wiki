    HoPWaG India · Ep 42 — "In Good Taste: The Aesthetics of Rasa"¶

    ← Ep 41

    Ep 43 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:40 In brief: Ancient India's theory of aesthetic emotion — how fiction stirs fear and longing without putting us in real danger or love. Built on Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra, the concept of rasa ("flavour") names the savoured aesthetic response a drama, poem, or piece of music is engineered to evoke; later theorists tie it to suggestion (dhvani), and finally to philosophical ideals of detachment and liberation. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Gernardin Gennary"; Bharata ≈ "Barata"; Nāṭyaśāstra ≈ "Natcha Shastra/Natya Sastra"; rasa ≈ "Rasa"; Ānandavardhana ≈ "Ananda Vardana/Anandavardana/Ananda Vratana"; Abhinavagupta ≈ "Abina Vagukta/Abinava Gupta"; Mahima Bhaṭṭa ≈ "Maima Bata/Mahima Bata"; dhvani ≈ "Thvani/divani"; śleṣa ≈ "Schleza"; Bhartṛhari ≈ "Batrihari"; Vālmīki ≈ "Valmiki"; Rāmāyaṇa ≈ "Ramayana"; Dattilam ≈ "Tatilam"; jāti ≈ "jatis"; artha ≈ "arta"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 45 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019). Its sources & a reading list are under Further reading below.

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra ("Treatise on Drama") — the key text of the Indian aesthetic tradition, probably compiled in the early centuries CE; source of the rasa theory [00:01:49].
    • rasa — "flavour/savour"; the aesthetic response a performance is built to evoke [00:03:01].
    • Ānandavardhana — 9th-century Kashmiri literary theorist who developed the theory of suggestion (dhvani) [00:07:17].
    • Mahima Bhaṭṭa — 11th-century aesthetician and critic of Ānandavardhana; closes the episode with the cleanest account of dramatic emotion [00:07:54].
    • Abhinavagupta — 10th-century commentator who reads rasa as a universal emotion linked to liberation [00:14:11].

    Horror movies, rom-coms, and the puzzle of fictional emotion¶

    • [00:00:36] Horror movies are a puzzling form of entertainment. Many people don't enjoy them, and those who do would be hard-pressed to say why — in everyday life you wouldn't thrill to the sight of innocent teenagers chased by an axe-wielding psychopath. Why enjoy watching it on a screen?
    • [00:00:57] The obvious reply — "they know it isn't real" — only deepens the puzzle: if the audience is aware the danger is entirely fictional, why should it provoke any emotional response at all?
    • [00:01:10] The same goes for the romantic comedy. We know Jennifer Aniston and "that handsome actor whose name escapes us" aren't really falling in love, yet we're swept away by their flirtation. How do fictional representations solicit emotions like fear and romantic yearning — without generating exactly the responses we'd have if we were really being pursued by an axe-wielding psychopath (or by Jennifer Aniston)?
    • [00:01:44] These questions were central to ancient Indian writings on aesthetics.

    Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra and the basics of rasa¶

    • [00:01:49] The tradition's key text is the Nāṭyaśāstra ("Treatise on Drama"), ascribed to an author named Bharata and probably compiled in the early centuries CE. (Listeners of Kit Patrick's History of India podcast will have met it in his episode 3A.) It is a very lengthy work offering detailed instructions on theatrical performance — from the construction of the theatre space to the gestures of actors and the metrical features of the recited text.
    • [00:02:21] Because drama involves poetry and music, Bharata says much about those arts too, so later theorists of literary and musical aesthetics draw on him. For music there was also a roughly contemporaneous early work, the Dattilam.
    • [00:02:40] Unlike the modern horror movie, ancient Indian dramas were not out to surprise you with plot twists — the audience already knew the stories. The real art lay in the effective and affecting presentation of those familiar tales.
    • [00:03:01] The key idea is rasa: an aesthetic response elicited by the drama. Though intimately associated with emotion, rasa is not the same thing as an emotion — rather it derives from emotion.
    • [00:03:24] The chain: an emotion is originally felt by the poet who authored the piece; it is then represented by actors through good dramatic technique. Erotic love, for instance, is conveyed by sidelong glances and gestures the skilled actor must master.
    • [00:03:43] A poet may touch many emotions in passing for brief effects, but the most important are the eight "emotional dispositions" (bhāva) around which a whole drama can be built. Bharata says the transitory effects serve to heighten these eight, "like attendants surrounding a monarch."
    • [00:04:06] Corresponding to the eight dispositions are eight kinds of rasa: the erotic, the comic, the pathetic, the furious, the heroic, the terrible, the odious, and the marvellous.

    From the book — ch. 45

    The book prints Bharata's full pairing of the eight emotional dispositions (sthāyibhāva) with their corresponding rasas, which the spoken list leaves implicit: erotic love (rati) → the erotic (śṛṅgāra) · mirth (hāsya) → the comic (hāsya) · sorrow (śoka) → the pathetic (karuṇa) · anger (krodha) → the furious (raudra) · energy (utsāha) → the heroic (vīra) · fear (bhaya) → the terrible (bhayānaka) · disgust (jugupsā) → the odious (bībhatsa) · astonishment (vismaya) → the marvellous (adbhuta).

    "Flavour": the food metaphor¶

    • [00:04:18] Bharata compares the rasas to the overall taste of a mixed beverage, alluding to the literal meaning of rasa — the savour or nutritive juice in food. (We meet it with that sense in ancient Indian medical works.)
    • [00:04:38] Take a successful horror film like Hitchcock's Psycho. On Bharata's analysis, every aspect — the disturbing music, the smallest detail of Anthony Perkins' uncanny performance as the hotel owner — is designed to evoke a continuous feeling of dread. Moments of disgust or surprise, like the famous reveal of the owner's mother, only serve to build up that core feeling, achieving the rasa of the terrible, the goal of the whole piece. Hitchcock would have this in mind even in innocuous scenes, not just the famous moments of violence.
    • [00:05:24] Of course, ancient Indian dramas did not centre on murderous hotel owners. Even when aiming at the terrible, they sought to impart moral instruction, serving a social function not usually associated with Hollywood horror.
    • [00:05:42] Producing rasa is a matter of consummate artistry — compare the skilled chef who balances ingredients for the right overall savour. As the later aesthetician Kuntaka puts it, only when natural charm is imparted by beauty, "coruscating with the innate artistry of the poet" and enlivened by the full complement of aesthetic elements, can even a proper rasa convey its deepest beauty to the reader — "whose heart then overflows with rasa the way a moonstone is liquefied by moonbeams."

    The connoisseur: rasa needs a skilled audience¶

    • [00:06:25] Even when conception and acting are top-notch, rasa results only for an audience member who is equally proficient. Extending the food analogy, Bharata says the skilled audience member is like the connoisseur with a fine appreciation of drama — quite literally "in the best possible taste."
    • [00:06:48] His commentators say the audience's goal is sympathy of the heart with the actors on stage, so as fully to experience the emotion that gives rise to rasa.
    • [00:06:57] The commentators disagree, however, on whether this is the only legitimate goal of drama or poetry. Some suggest rasa could be a mere bonus or ornament — e.g. in a poem whose main purpose is flattery.
    • [00:07:17] The 9th-century Kashmiri theorist Ānandavardhana uses this idea to explain why a piece might evoke two rival emotions (say, sadness and erotic longing), which would seem to block a single overall rasa: only when a single rasa predominates is it the ultimate aim of the poem as a whole. Others argue rasa can never be a mere ornament — all poetry worthy of the name must have rasa as its main purpose.

    What can spoil rasa: redundancy and bad puns¶

    • [00:07:50] Attention also goes to factors that impede rasa. There's a long discussion in another aesthetician, a critic of Ānandavardhana named Mahima Bhaṭṭa. He complains about poets who use redundant expressions, citing: "he who cuts the necks of his evil enemies with the extremely fierce blow of the front end of the drawn sharpened sword held in his hand." Why not just say he who cuts the necks of his enemies with a sword?
    • [00:08:25] Another flaw is the pun that doesn't quite work. The pun (or double meaning) was "almost as important an element in Sanskrit literature as in this podcast series." This device, called śleṣa, exploits ambiguous meanings of words or whole sentences, and could be used to reveal the complexity of a literary character — not for cheap laughs like the play on "executed" a moment ago.

    Rasa in music — and the borrowed idea of suggestion¶

    • [00:08:47] Rasa appears not only in commentaries on the Nāṭyaśāstra and works on poetics but also in treatises on music. The ancient music theorists don't discuss specific written-down compositions; they focus on melody types called jāti, associated with certain moods and emotions.
    • [00:09:19] One early theorist, Matanga, shows how literary ideas penetrated music: he borrowed the poetic concept of suggestion (dhvani) — the idea that a performance can evoke or express something without alluding to it explicitly.
    • [00:09:38] This makes sense. If you want the audience to appreciate sadness, it's counterproductive to have a character say "I'm sad now." Think how you're taken out of a film when the director picks a soundtrack song whose lyrics too obviously name the desired emotion.

    Ānandavardhana on dhvani (poetic suggestion)¶

    • [00:10:01] It was especially Ānandavardhana who developed dhvani to explain how rasa is evoked by poetry. In doing so he appropriated a term from the grammatical tradition: the grammarian Bhartṛhari had said that uttered sounds are dhvani and "make manifest" linguistic meaning.
    • [00:10:26] Taking inspiration from this, Ānandavardhana argues dhvani / suggestion is a special function of poetic language: the poet's art avoids explicit reference to the emotion he wants to produce, and instead implicitly suggests it, thus eliciting the desired rasa.
    • [00:10:47] His example: "Wander confidently, monk — the dog that frightened you has been killed by a lion." The literal meaning is that the frightening dog is gone, and it still stands; but the point of the verse is that the monk should be more frightened than ever — now a lion is on the loose. That is the suggestion, the unstated meaning.
    • [00:11:12] Another example: saying a village is on the river Ganges — true literally, but its full significance (that the village is a holy place) is conveyed only to someone who knows the river's sanctity.
    • [00:11:25] For Ānandavardhana, suggestion is distinct from both the straightforward meaning of words and from metaphor. Poetic language can keep its normal meaning while evoking rasa, whereas metaphor requires giving up the normal meaning. His illustration: when we say a village is "on" the Ganges, we don't imagine a floating village; we transfer the word "on" and understand the village is arrayed on the bank. By contrast, poetic suggestion leaves the literal meaning intact — it is something additional, conveyed only implicitly, which "shines above the parts of the poem like a woman's charm." It's crucial the poet can work indirectly, since direct statement would ruin the effect.

    The poet as inspired creator¶

    • [00:12:34] Ānandavardhana adds that the meaning conveyed (at least to the skilled audience member) is one originally experienced by the poet. He recalls a story about Vālmīki, supposed author of the great epic Rāmāyaṇa: while bathing, the poet witnessed a hunter killing one of a pair of birds and heard the mate's plaintive cry, then cried out in a spontaneous verse that came from divine inspiration.
    • [00:13:05] This fits older ideas of the poet as a kind of religious intermediary, like the seers who pass down the Vedas. Poets were thought to benefit from a special unbidden illumination, distinguished from other visionaries because they actually set their visions down in words.
    • [00:13:28] In an echo of this, Ānandavardhana says the poet may choose any primary meaning he likes in his quest to evoke rasa by suggestion: "In the boundless world of literature, the author is sole creator God. The whole universe takes on whatever form he wills." The resulting poem becomes a means of conveying to the listener the rasa the poet originally felt — "to taste what he tasted."

    Rasa as meaning — and Abhinavagupta's universal emotion¶

    • [00:13:59] For Ānandavardhana, rasa is really an effect of language, like the meaning of words — so it is called an artha ("meaning").
    • [00:14:11] But for one of his most important commentators, the 10th-century author Abhinavagupta, the rasa experienced and conveyed by the poet is an emotion that is universal in character. In the Vālmīki story, the dead bird and its mourning mate are simply the occasion that inspired the poet to experience a generalized sorrow.
    • [00:14:29] Likewise the audience member who experiences rasa is not having the everyday emotion: you are not in love with the rom-com characters, nor afraid for your own safety while watching Psycho. Though you feel romance and fear, these feelings are not directed at any particular object.
    • [00:14:55] This recalls Indian ideas of detachment and liberation — and it seems to have reminded Abhinavagupta of the same. By this period the eight canonical rasas had been joined by a ninth, Tranquility (śānta). For Ānandavardhana this is the rasa intended by no less a work than the Mahābhārata, which (like any good poem) has the production of rasa as its sole aim.
    • [00:15:28] Abhinavagupta links this rasa of tranquility to liberation: "This enjoyment of rasa is like the bliss that comes from realizing one's identity with the highest brahman, for it consists of repose in the bliss which is the true nature of one's own self."

    The actor's problem: feeling without being¶

    • [00:15:42] What about the performers? Consider Anthony Perkins in Psycho: we know he's an actor, not really a murderer, yet we respond with fear and revulsion. How does he provoke this without having murder in his heart?
    • [00:16:14] Bharata already stated the actor's role is to express a psychological state without actually being in that same state. Later commentators dug deeper.
    • [00:16:30] One view — imitation: the actor merely imitates the character at the surface level (gesture and so on), inviting the audience to make an inference about the intended emotion. The sidelong glance signifies love, and skilled viewers read it as such, though aware he is acting. As one text nicely puts it: we think the actor is the character, but not that the actor is really the character.
    • [00:16:58] The objection: this inference would be fallacious. Recall inferring fire from smoke on a mountain — a bad inference if what we see is not really smoke but merely looks like it (low-hanging mist). Likewise, if the sidelong glance is only an empty imitation of how a lover would look, it's mistaken to infer that real love is present. Besides, the actor isn't an empty vessel going through the motions — he has his own psychological states, which must somehow connect to the aesthetic effect.
    • [00:17:39] The contrary view — total identification: the actor identifies completely with the character, recreating the character's emotion on stage. But this met skepticism too: how could the actor keep performing while genuinely in the throes of such powerful emotions?

    Mahima Bhaṭṭa's solution: aesthetic emotion is its own kind¶

    • [00:18:02] In the 11th century, Mahima Bhaṭṭa made a convincing proposal that avoids these problems. Referring back to Bharata's statement that rasa is manifested because of the aesthetic features of a theatrical performance, he points out that such aesthetic features simply do not exist in everyday life. You see the actor on stage as a representation of the character, not as the character truly undergoing the drama's events.
    • [00:18:33] As he puts it, "since aesthetic elements are fictitious, whereas cause and effect are real" — the former pertaining to literature, the latter to the world — the two differ in both their natures and their objects.
    • [00:18:50] So when we see a terrorized victim in a horror movie, we do not make an erroneous inference from a fake representation to a real fear (like inferring real fire from fake smoke). Rather, we experience it as a properly aesthetic representation, and the inference we make is to a properly aesthetic emotion — which is precisely the savouring of rasa.

    What the debate shows¶

    • [00:19:07] We can infer something from the whole debate: the ideas of the philosophical schools were not confined to those schools. The wider intelligentsia of Indian society knew them and applied them in new contexts. Just as the philosophers appropriated the apparatus of the Sanskrit grammarians, these literary aestheticians appropriated philosophical terms and concepts.
    • [00:19:42] Discussion of rasa took its initial cue from Bharata and was sustained for the better part of a millennium (and could have gone further). It grew steadily more sophisticated, with later authors keeping the Nāṭyaśāstra as touchstone while connecting it to ideals of detachment and liberation and notions from the philosophy of language.
    • [00:20:05] Whatever we make of their aesthetic judgments, these scholars were right about one thing: the richness and fruitfulness of the theories put forth by the Vedic schools.
    • [00:20:23] But — as intimated many times — some other philosophers were not so impressed. These same schools were subjected to searching critiques by the Buddhists and Jains, critiques philosophically rich in their own right. Next time, the curtain goes up on the last stage of the series: the so-called unorthodox traditions.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • rasa — literally "savour/juice"; the aesthetic response a performance is engineered to evoke. Derived from emotion but not identical to it; there are eight canonical kinds (erotic, comic, pathetic, furious, heroic, terrible, odious, marvellous), later nine.
    • Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra — "Treatise on Drama" (early centuries CE); foundational text of Indian aesthetics, with detailed instructions on theatre, gesture, poetry, and music.
    • bhāva — the eight "emotional dispositions" around which a drama is built; transitory emotions heighten them "like attendants surrounding a monarch."
    • Dattilam — early treatise on music, roughly contemporaneous with the Nāṭyaśāstra.
    • Kuntaka — later aesthetician; quoted on how a poem's natural charm overflows the reader's heart with rasa "the way a moonstone is liquefied by moonbeams."
    • connoisseur — the skilled audience member; rasa arises only for one proficient enough to sympathize with the actors and savour the performance "in the best possible taste."
    • Ānandavardhana — 9th-century Kashmiri theorist; developed dhvani (suggestion); held that one rasa must predominate, and that rasa is an artha (meaning, effect of language).
    • dhvani — "suggestion"; poetic language's special function of conveying an emotion implicitly rather than naming it; borrowed from the grammarian Bhartṛhari's term for meaning-bearing sound.
    • Bhartṛhari — grammarian who said uttered sounds are dhvani that make manifest linguistic meaning; the source Ānandavardhana adapts.
    • artha — "meaning"; Ānandavardhana's classification of rasa as an effect of language.
    • śleṣa — the pun / deliberate double meaning, prized in Sanskrit literature; a poorly-working one is a flaw that impedes rasa.
    • Mahima Bhaṭṭa — 11th-century aesthetician and critic of Ānandavardhana; argued aesthetic emotion is its own fictitious kind, so we infer not real feeling but a properly aesthetic emotion — the savouring of rasa.
    • jāti — melody types in Indian music theory, associated with particular moods/emotions.
    • Matanga — early music theorist who imported the poetic idea of suggestion (dhvani) into music.
    • Vālmīki / Rāmāyaṇa — supposed author of the great epic; his spontaneous, divinely inspired verse on a slain bird models the poet as inspired religious intermediary.
    • Abhinavagupta — 10th-century commentator; reads rasa as a universal, object-less emotion, linking the ninth rasa of Tranquility to liberation and identity with brahman.
    • Tranquility (śānta) — the ninth rasa added after Bharata's eight; the rasa Ānandavardhana sees as the aim of the Mahābhārata, tied to detachment and liberation.

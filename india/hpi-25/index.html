    HoPWaG India · Ep 25 — "Communication Breakdown: Bhartṛhari on Language"¶

    ← Ep 24

    Ep 26 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:19:03 In brief: The grammarian Bhartṛhari makes the study of language "the door to liberation," arguing that meaning (sphoṭa) is a single, partless, timeless reality that whole sentences merely make manifest — and that this indivisible meaning ultimately points back to Brahman, making his linguistics a distinctively grammatical version of Vedānta. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Bhartṛhari ≈ "Batri Hari/Barzrihari/Barthihari/Bhatrihari/Batrijari/Patsri-Hari"; Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janard and Gannery"; Pāṇini ≈ "Panani/Pannoni"; Patañjali ≈ "Patanjali"; Mahābhāṣya ≈ "Mahabashya"; Vākyapadīya ≈ "vaciapadilla/Vakia Padilla"; sphoṭa ≈ "Svota"; śabda ≈ "Shabda"; jāti ≈ "Jiva"; Vṛtti ≈ "Vriti"; Ṭīkā/Dīpikā ≈ "Tika/Dipika"; Brahman ≈ "Brahmann"; Porphyry ≈ "Porfurian"; Dignāga ≈ "Dignaga"; Śaṅkara ≈ "Shankara").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Bhartṛhari — 5th-c. CE grammarian-philosopher who treats the analysis of language as the path to liberation [00:01:01].
    • Pāṇini (6th/5th c. BCE) and his commentator Patañjali (2nd c. CE, the Mahābhāṣya) — the grammatical lineage Bhartṛhari inherits [00:01:20].
    • The Vākyapadīya — Bhartṛhari's "treatise on words and sentences," with its possibly self-authored explanatory Vṛtti [00:02:12].
    • The Mīmāṃsā school — the rival theory of meaning Bhartṛhari argues against [00:06:36].
    • Dignāga — the Buddhist thinker who later reacted to Bhartṛhari and drew out his skeptical implications [00:18:16].

    The grammarian's boast: language is the door to liberation¶

    • [00:00:36] Intellectuals tend to think their own discipline is the most important: historians warn that those who forget the past are doomed to repeat it, mathematicians prize the certainty of their proofs, and philosophers call their field "the queen of the sciences," with everything else a mere "subject."
    • [00:01:01] So it's par for the course that Bhartṛhari, one of the greatest figures in classical Sanskrit grammar, tells us that the study of language constitutes the door to liberation. He's modest enough, though, to credit others with opening that door.
    • [00:01:20] He belongs to the tradition founded by the great Pāṇini (episode 8), but responds directly to the later Patañjali, whose great commentary on Pāṇini — the Mahābhāṣya — inspires Bhartṛhari's own treatises.
    • [00:01:39] The dates span a millennium: Pāṇini worked in the 6th or 5th centuries BCE, his commentator Patañjali in the 2nd century CE, both drawing on shadowier predecessors; Bhartṛhari then arrives in the 5th century CE, a full thousand years after Pāṇini.

    Bhartṛhari's works¶

    • [00:02:00] He was apparently the first to write a super-commentary — a Ṭīkā or Dīpikā — on Patañjali's commentary. But his fame rests on another work based on Patañjali: the Vākyapadīya, the treatise on words and sentences.
    • [00:02:14] Its style is by now familiar — terse, aphoristic remarks that presuppose knowledge of the texts being alluded to. True to Indian habit, the Vākyapadīya itself acquired further commentary: an accompanying explanatory work, the Vṛtti, may even have been written by Bhartṛhari himself. Whether or not it was, the Vṛtti is a helpful guide to the much more compressed Vākyapadīya.

    Grammar is philosophy — and it leads to Brahman¶

    • [00:02:51] More than Pāṇini or Patañjali, Bhartṛhari shows the philosophical importance of the Sanskrit grammatical tradition. His boast that grammar is the door to liberation suggests that for him the study of language simply is philosophy. Where other Indian thinkers saw meditation or ethical discipline as the route to liberation, Bhartṛhari takes a far more intellectualist stance: it is knowledge that sets us free.
    • [00:03:21] Not knowledge of grammatical minutiae — case endings, singular versus plural — though the Vākyapadīya covers those at length. Rather, the analysis of language is a step toward a higher understanding in which we grasp the reality that underlies and gives rise to language. That reality is Brahman.
    • [00:03:48] This is why Bhartṛhari is covered here alongside Vedānta. Like the Vedāntins, he is convinced that Brahman is the single fundamental principle of all things, known to us only because of the Veda. Bhartṛhari offers a distinctively linguistic version of this idea: grammar is worth studying simply to interpret the Sanskrit of the Veda, but more fundamentally, it is language that accounts for the division of the single Brahman into the multiple reality we experience.

    sphoṭa and śabda: meaning and its representation¶

    • [00:04:26] To see how, we must ask what Bhartṛhari takes language to be — not an easy question, since the Vākyapadīya summarizes and refutes many rival views while sometimes leaving his own position unclear, and uses technical terms whose sense isn't obvious. The two most important here are sphoṭa and śabda.
    • [00:04:57] Roughly: sphoṭa is the inner meaning of language, while śabda is the language used to represent that meaning. Straightforward enough — except that śabda (linguistic representation) can occur either in the mind or in actual verbal speech.
    • [00:05:14] Bhartṛhari says language in the mind gives rise to expressed language "like one fire kindling another." When you hear an utterance you depend on your own mental representations to understand it, and so gain access to the inner meaning (sphoṭa) the speaker was trying to express.
    • [00:05:38] So far this sounds commonsensical. Suppose I want to warn you that there's a giraffe in your kitchen: I formulate the sentence in my mind, utter it aloud, and trigger comprehension in your mind (along with the hope the giraffe will clean up last night's dishes). The verbal utterance and the two mental acts all point toward a single shared meaning.

    The partless, timeless meaning¶

    • [00:06:03] It's here that things get controversial. The meaning (sphoṭa) is in a sense the core of language, since capturing meaning is the whole point of the linguistic enterprise. Yet sphoṭa is also non-linguistic: according to Bhartṛhari, meaning in itself has no parts and is not subject to time. Only actual verbal utterances are affected by time, because we must speak one word after another — whereas a meaning can be represented in the mind all at once.

    Against Mīmāṃsā: words don't build meaning, they manifest it¶

    • [00:06:36] This is diametrically opposed to a rival view Bhartṛhari associates with the Mīmāṃsā school. For the Mīmāṃsakas, meaning is not prior to linguistic expression; rather, it is constituted by the verbal utterance, whose parts work together to give rise to the intended meaning. Words like "giraffe" and "kitchen" get fitted into a grammatical structure, and the result is a meaningful sentence.
    • [00:07:05] For Bhartṛhari this gets things backward. Individual words have no meaning in their own right; they signify only in the context of a sentence — just as the sounds making up a word ("jir," "a," "f") are not themselves meaningful. Two arguments:
    • [00:07:25] The words pass away as they are uttered. By the time I reach "kitchen," "giraffe" has already come and gone, so the words cannot literally combine to produce a single united meaning, because they never coexist.
    • [00:07:47] Some words have no referent. In "there is not a giraffe in the kitchen," the word "giraffe" signifies no actual giraffe; it is meaningful only as part of the whole sentence.
    • [00:08:06] So Bhartṛhari concludes that the words in a sentence make an inner meaning manifest, rather than actually constituting it.
    • [00:08:13] The same holds from the listener's side: rather than grasping each word's meaning in sequence, one comprehends the meaning of the whole sentence at once, in a "flash of understanding" that arrives when the sentence reaches its conclusion.

    The objection from contradiction¶

    • [00:08:31] Bhartṛhari anticipates a Mīmāṃsā objection: part-by-part composition would better explain logical relations between sentences, such as contradiction. "There is a giraffe in the kitchen" and "there is not a giraffe in the kitchen" look like the same sentence with one part — the word "not" — added to generate a contradictory meaning.
    • [00:09:06] His reply: relations between sentences, contradiction included, simply have to do with the degree of overall resemblance between the indivisible meanings of the sentences in question. No part-composition required.
    • [00:09:17] Despite this holism, Bhartṛhari still has much to say about the grammatical features of individual words — case, gender of nouns. But ultimately grammar is the study of the way communication breaks down meaning: the grammarian analyzes the parts of speech that let us express meaning, while realizing that the meaning is unified and prior to that expression. The Mīmāṃsā approach, by contrast, is "like trying to understand a pot by starting from broken shards rather than the whole vessel."

    From language to reality: the latent seed of speech¶

    • [00:09:55] Now comes a flash of understanding of our own, noting the affinity between Bhartṛhari's theory of language and the Vedānta theory of reality. Just as Vedānta sees the multiplicity of phenomenal experience as an expression of the single source, Brahman, so Bhartṛhari thinks divisible speech expresses indivisible meaning.
    • [00:10:13] But there's more than analogy here: for Bhartṛhari, language is the very means by which we have experience and come to know the world. Even the most rudimentary perceptions — like those of small children — contain what he calls a "latent seed of language," which cannot yet be communicated. Full-blown cognition, though, is always linguistic, so that our knowledge is permeated by words.
    • [00:10:44] This too looks like common sense: we grasp things only by filing them under descriptions or concepts. Perceiving the alarmingly tall animal in the kitchen as a giraffe arguably depends on my having the word "giraffe" in my vocabulary.

    Universals (jāti) and the primacy of the general¶

    • [00:11:09] These classifying concepts are what philosophers today call universals, and Bhartṛhari uses the same notion; his Sanskrit term is jāti. He's deeply interested in universals, partly because they're so critical to grammar. Some grammarians held that language refers first to individual things — the particular giraffe in your kitchen — but for Bhartṛhari meanings are primarily universal. The universal, indeed, is what we really mean by sphoṭa.
    • [00:11:51] This shows in how words work, especially in a language like Sanskrit where you start with a basic noun and add endings for gender and number. His English-friendly example: actor, actors, actress, actresses — a single universal meaning ("someone who acts on stage") licenses us to apply the related words to many objects.
    • [00:12:23] He again uses resemblance: we call every giraffe "giraffe" because they resemble one another in the relevant way while differing otherwise (this one doing dishes in the kitchen, that one reading philosophy in the study). Even the same individual resembles itself at other times — which is why we can say "you are like yourself," the remark you might make at a school reunion after thirty years.

    From universal meaning to a universe of universals¶

    • [00:12:52] Most Indian thinkers believed language reveals the structure of reality, and as a language expert Bhartṛhari was hardly going to disagree. So the primacy of the universal at the level of meaning shows that reality itself is first universal and only then particular: individual things reveal the universal just as individual words reveal the meaning of a whole sentence.
    • [00:13:24] Why trust that language captures the way things are? Because Bhartṛhari adheres to a belief first met in Mīmāṃsā: the Veda — and hence language itself — is unoriginated. He takes this to imply a primary classification of reality in terms of universals, the eternal meanings revealed in the language of scripture.
    • [00:13:55] That makes him sound a bit like Plato: universals, like Platonic Forms, give significance to our words and serve as the fundamental principles of all things.

    How do the universals relate to Brahman?¶

    • [00:14:06] But for Bhartṛhari the truly fundamental principle is Brahman — so how do his universals relate to that single source? Scholars don't agree.
    • [00:14:20] Reading one: Brahman is effectively the most general universal, identical with being — the class of all things that exist. Other universals add differentiation, progressively narrowing it down: some beings are alive, some living things are animals, some animals are giraffes, and some giraffes (though not many) are in kitchens. On this reading Bhartṛhari sounds less like Plato and more like a Neoplatonist using Aristotelian logic — specifically like Porphyry, whose famous "Porphyrian tree" is just such a hierarchy of genera and species.
    • [00:15:09] Reading two (which has challenged the first fairly persuasively): what we really find in the Vākyapadīya is that the universals are all identical with Brahman, constituting various means of expressing its fundamental reality. This yields a neat parallel: just as the universals divide Brahman while being identical with it, so individual giraffes both divide and yet are somehow identical with the universal giraffe.
    • [00:15:44] Either way, for Bhartṛhari the universals form a second layer of principles that manifest and differentiate the most fundamental reality, Brahman — and the universals are in turn made manifest, on the metaphysical side by individuals, on the linguistic side by mental representation and speech.

    Dualism or non-dualism? Is grammar a ladder to throw away?¶

    • [00:16:12] Less clear is whether this amounts to the radical proposal found last time in Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedānta. Is Bhartṛhari saying that the language and universal meaning he devoted himself to is, in the end, a falsification of the single reality of Brahman — our world of experience, structured by language, just an illusion? If so, grammar would be less a door to liberation than a ladder to be thrown away once Brahman is grasped directly.
    • [00:16:49] As with the Brahma-Sūtra, the Vākyapadīya contains passages supporting both readings:
    • [00:17:01] For dualism (the world of experience is real, but a secondary, lesser reality than Brahman): Brahman is said to possess powers for producing other things and to take on forms when they're produced. Bhartṛhari's celebrated image fits here — Brahman is like a peacock's egg, which reveals a welter of brilliant colors once it hatches into the bird and its plumage.
    • [00:17:30] For non-dualism: passages connecting language with unreality. Words can express non-existent things — compared to the circle we see when looking at a whirling firebrand — and elsewhere Bhartṛhari says the study of language is a path to truth even though it deals with what is unreal. Comments at the start of the Vākyapadīya in the explanatory Vṛtti also tend non-dualist.
    • [00:18:04] This side of Bhartṛhari brings him close not only to the later Śaṅkara but also to contemporary Buddhists, who were more forthright in condemning the world of experience as illusion.

    Dignāga's reaction, and where we go next¶

    • [00:18:16] In fact one thinker who reacted to Bhartṛhari was the great Buddhist Dignāga, quick to point out the skeptical implications lurking in the theory: language may distort reality by dividing it, pulling it apart into an infinity of fragments. Much more on Dignāga, but much later.
    • [00:18:39] For now there are more Vedic schools to consider — soon moving on from Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta to the Sāṃkhya and Yoga schools. But before closing the door on Vedānta, the next episode features a guest, the indologist Francis Clooney, for one more attempt to liberate listeners from confusion about the tradition.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Bhartṛhari — 5th-c. CE Sanskrit grammarian; holds that the study of language is "the door to liberation" and that knowledge of language's underlying reality (Brahman) sets us free.
    • Pāṇini — founder of the grammatical tradition (6th/5th c. BCE), covered in episode 8.
    • Patañjali — Pāṇini's 2nd-c. CE commentator; his Mahābhāṣya inspired Bhartṛhari's treatises.
    • Vākyapadīya — Bhartṛhari's terse, aphoristic "treatise on words and sentences," his main philosophical work.
    • Vṛtti — an explanatory commentary on the Vākyapadīya, possibly by Bhartṛhari himself; tends in a non-dualist direction.
    • Ṭīkā / Dīpikā — Bhartṛhari's super-commentary on Patañjali's commentary.
    • sphoṭa — the inner meaning of language; partless, timeless, non-linguistic, grasped "all at once"; identified with the universal.
    • śabda — the linguistic representation of meaning, occurring either in the mind or in spoken utterance; mental language kindles spoken language "like one fire kindling another."
    • jāti — the universal; for Bhartṛhari, meaning is primarily universal, and universals (revealed eternally in scripture) classify reality.
    • Mīmāṃsā — rival school holding that meaning is constituted by the verbal utterance's parts, not (as Bhartṛhari says) merely manifested by them.
    • Brahman — the single fundamental principle; either the most general universal (being) or the reality with which all universals are identical.
    • Veda — held to be unoriginated; the warrant that language reflects the eternal structure of reality.
    • Dignāga — Buddhist thinker who reacted to Bhartṛhari, exposing the skeptical implication that language distorts reality by fragmenting it.
    • Śaṅkara — Advaita Vedāntin (previous episode); the non-dualist reading brings Bhartṛhari close to him.
    • peacock's egg — Bhartṛhari's image for (dualist) Brahman producing the colorful manifold world.
    • whirling firebrand — image for how words can express non-existent things, illustrating the non-dualist/unreality strand.

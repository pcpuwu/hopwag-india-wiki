    HoPWaG India · Ep 27 — "The Theory of Evolution: Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṃkhya-kārikā"¶

    ← Ep 26

    Ep 28 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:24:06 In brief: Sāṃkhya — the "counting" system — explained through Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṃkhya-kārikā: a dualism of inactive pure consciousness (puruṣa) and productive primordial matter (prakṛti), which spontaneously "evolves" into the 25 principles via three intertwined forces (the guṇas), with liberation coming from identifying with the passive witness and letting everything else go. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gannari"; Sāṃkhya ≈ "Samkhya/Sanjia/Sankya"; Sāṃkhya-kārikā ≈ "Samkhya Karika/Kharika"; Īśvarakṛṣṇa ≈ "Ishvara Krishna/Ishvika/Ishverakrishna"; Kauṭilya ≈ "Cautilia"; Arthaśāstra ≈ "Ata Shastra"; puruṣa ≈ "Purusha/Porusha"; prakṛti ≈ "Prachriti/Procriti/Prokraty"; guṇa ≈ "guna"; sattva ≈ "Satva"; rajas ≈ "Rajas"; tamas ≈ "Tamas"; Kapila ≈ "Kapila"; Āsuri ≈ "Asuri"; Pañcaśikha ≈ "Panchashika/Panchachika"; Gauḍapāda ≈ "Gautapada/Gaudapada"; Patañjali ≈ "Patanjali"; Pāṇini ≈ "Panani"; Śaṅkara ≈ "Shankara"; Chāndogya ≈ "Chandogya"; buddhi ≈ "Budi"; ahaṃkāra ≈ "eye-making"; ākāśa ≈ "akasha"; Bhartṛhari ≈ "Batrihari"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 22 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019). Its sources & a reading list are under Further reading below.

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Sāṃkhya — the "counting / enumeration" tradition; arguably the oldest of the Vedic schools, existing long before it was a school [00:01:31].
    • Īśvarakṛṣṇa — author of the Sāṃkhya-kārikā, the oldest surviving Sāṃkhya treatise; lived c. 350–450 CE, a late entry in the lineage [00:00:29].
    • Kapila, Āsuri, Pañcaśikha — the legendary earlier Sāṃkhya teachers, the "Great Sage" founder and his successors [00:02:56].
    • The Sāṃkhya-kārikā — the verse text itemizing the 25 principles, the 8 behavioural tendencies, and 50 types of result [00:01:52].

    What the Muppets would make of it — Sāṃkhya as counting¶

    • [00:00:37] A "pivotal question": what would the Muppets make of Indian philosophy? Different Muppets, like the Indian thinkers, would differ — Miss Piggy would admire the Jaina ban on violence to animals, the self-abnegating Scooter is "practically a Buddhist already," Sam the Eagle would endorse the strict politics of Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra, and the Swedish Chef would feel kinship with the sūtra texts "since you usually can't tell what the heck they are saying either."
    • [00:01:20] The best fit is Count von Count from Sesame Street, the vampire who teaches children arithmetic. He'd want to get his teeth into Sāṃkhya, whose name "literally means counting or enumeration." Sāṃkhya loves numbers and offers "a whole system of itemizing lists."
    • [00:01:52] The catalogues include the 25 principles that constitute self and cosmos, the 8 tendencies guiding human behaviour, and the 50 types of result for humans — running from five basic misunderstandings of the world to nine kinds of contentment. These lists are mentioned (though usually not fully spelled out) in the oldest surviving treatise of the school, the Sāṃkhya-kārikā, ascribed to Īśvarakṛṣṇa, thought to have lived between 350 and 450 CE.

    From the book — ch. 22

    The book supplies the Sanskrit for the catalogues — the twenty-five principles are tattvas and the eight tendencies bhāvas — and explains the work's title: it is called a kārikā rather than a sūtra purely because of its verse form.

    An ancient school with a late spokesman¶

    • [00:02:28] This sounds ancient — and is — but like other supposed "founding fathers" seen in earlier episodes, Īśvarakṛṣṇa was heir to a still older tradition. You can argue Sāṃkhya is the oldest of the Vedic schools, "especially if you add the caveat that it existed long before being a school."
    • [00:02:51] At the end of the Sāṃkhya-kārikā, Īśvarakṛṣṇa is revealed as a fairly late entry in a list of thinkers. The real founder — called simply "the Great Sage" — is identified by ancient commentators as Kapila, followed by two more teachers, Āsuri and Pañcaśikha, with Īśvarakṛṣṇa coming an unspecified number of generations later.
    • [00:03:15] Whatever the accuracy of the lineage, Sāṃkhya reaches deep into the past. Its characteristic ideas trace back to the earliest Vedas, and there are passages in the Upaniṣads and the Mahābhārata with unmistakable similarities to Īśvarakṛṣṇa's teaching. The enumeration of principles is anticipated in the Chāndogya Upaniṣad, and the legendary figures appear in earlier texts — the Mahābhārata describes how Pañcaśikha defeated 100 other sages in debate.
    • [00:03:53] Sāṃkhya develops "in concert with the tradition of yoga," which gives a practical complement to Sāṃkhya's "enumerative system-mongering." The Mahābhārata says yoga and Sāṃkhya share a single teaching, though it also reports teachers identifying with one school or the other.
    • [00:04:20] In the Sāṃkhya-kārikā, Īśvarakṛṣṇa too was "reaching into the past," retrenching to a more conservative version of the system to challenge more recent innovations. As so often, the oldest surviving text drops us into the middle of an ongoing conversation — so we shouldn't assume his ideas are definitive of Sāṃkhya just because his is the work that survived rather than his rivals'.

    The commentators — and Śaṅkara's hostility¶

    • [00:04:49] On the other hand, Īśvarakṛṣṇa's ideas did come to dominate. More counting: there are no fewer than eight early commentaries on the Sāṃkhya-kārikā. Two are by authors with familiar names — Gauḍapāda and Patañjali (this Patañjali not to be confused with the grammarian who wrote the authoritative commentary on Pāṇini). It's possible the Gauḍapāda who commented here is the same Vedāntin who anticipated Śaṅkara.
    • [00:05:26] What is certain is that Śaṅkara "really had it in for Sāṃkhya." As noted in his episode, he sternly criticized Īśvarakṛṣṇa's whole approach — taking exception to their cosmology, their conception of the self, and their epistemology.

    Dualism: puruṣa and prakṛti¶

    • [00:05:53] Śaṅkara's Advaita ("non-dual") Vedānta sees the distinction between brahman and all else as illusory: only one principle is real. Sāṃkhya, by contrast, is dualist — "the Bert and Ernie of the system," two very different characters in close partnership: puruṣa and prakṛti.
    • [00:06:24] We met puruṣa back in episode three, in the Ṛg Veda cosmology where the gods cut it up in a world-forming sacrifice, fashioning its limbs into the groups of society — there it seemed an originary human complete with body. In the Sāṃkhya-kārikā, puruṣa is far more abstract: "that which witnesses all, yet remains inactive." This is pure consciousness, the subject underlying all objects of awareness — a thoroughly Upaniṣadic idea here used as the foundation of the whole system.
    • [00:07:12] The Vedāntins could agree so far, but would reject the next move: identifying pure consciousness as only one of two fundamental principles. The other, prakṛti, is translated "nature" or "primordial matter" — the principle that gives rise to all other things by undergoing a complex, step-by-step process of transformation, while puruṣa remains inactive, "maintaining an unending silent vigil" as it observes prakṛti develop.
    • [00:07:52] This is not dualism as in Gnosticism: Sāṃkhya shares the idea of two independent principles, neither the cause of the other, but there's no God acting on matter and no good principle in conflict with a counterbalancing source of evil. Rather, nature spontaneously turns itself into all things, while consciousness just bears witness.
    • [00:08:24] Yet puruṣa has a role in explaining the phenomena. Īśvarakṛṣṇa says prakṛti, though productive, is unintelligent — without puruṣa there could be no awareness within nature's products. The two are compared to a blind man cooperating with a lame man: nature is blind (lacking consciousness), consciousness is lame (without nature it stays inert).

    The 23 evolutes and effects pre-existing in causes¶

    • [00:08:47] We've only counted to two; the remaining 23 principles are less fundamental — the phenomena that emerge from prakṛti as it undergoes what Īśvarakṛṣṇa calls modification. Scholars sometimes call these 23 results "the evolutes" — which "sounds more like a 1950s singing group" — but the term fits, since they evolve out of prakṛti in a gradual, stepwise process.
    • [00:09:31] In defence of this evolutionary theory, Īśvarakṛṣṇa appeals to the idea that effects pre-exist in their causes. The same assumption appeared in Vedānta, where Śaṅkara argued all things are virtually present in brahman, "as pots are already somehow in the clay." This explains why pots don't emerge from sheer non-being, and why a given material can only make certain things: "You can put a chicken in every pot, but you can't make a chicken out of a pot" — though Adamson admits it would be a great act for the Amazing Gonzo to attempt.

    Prakṛti the unmanifest, and the three sources of knowledge¶

    • [00:10:09] Taking a closer look at prakṛti is difficult: the Sāṃkhya-kārikā calls it unmanifest and too subtle to perceive. Though it implicitly contains all things, in itself it is none of them, so we cannot grasp it directly — only as a postulate or hypothesis.
    • [00:10:46] To justify this, Īśvarakṛṣṇa touches on a theme explored later with the Nyāya school: the sources of knowledge. Sāṃkhya recognizes three — direct perception, inference, and valid testimony. For prakṛti it is inference that applies: we infer it must be real, without grasping it directly, because there must be a single principle to explain how there is order and limit in things.

    The three guṇas — cords of change¶

    • [00:11:19] The mechanism of change is "one of the most intriguing yet elusive aspects" of the system: the three guṇas, a word literally meaning "cords or threads." At the metaphorical level, think of a rope of three intertwined strands; philosophically, the guṇas are the functional modes or interacting tendencies inherent in prakṛti by which nature transforms.
    • [00:11:53] The three are sattva, rajas, and tamas — terms hard to translate that "really take the cake," reaching deep into Vedic literary history. Originally they may have alluded to sun, sky, and earth (which together make growing food possible), and they're associated with white, red, and black, perhaps a traditional colour scheme for clothing. In the Sāṃkhya-kārikā they are the three forces of change driving the evolution of things from prakṛti:
    • sattva — connected to lightness and pleasure;
    • rajas — change, and the upheaval that causes suffering;
    • tamas — heavy, to do with resistance and despondency.
    • [00:12:49] So each guṇa carries a striking combination of cosmological and psychological traits — a hallmark of the whole system. Within the cosmos, different realms are dominated by the three forces, apparently explaining the difference between the divine/heavenly realm, humanity, and animals and plants; the guṇas govern all things "from Brahmā down to a blade of grass." Psychologically, sattva was historically linked to liberation: individual selves may be dominated by sattva or by tamas — that is, they may live wisely or not.
    • [00:13:40] In both cosmos and person, the central point is that the three guṇas operate together dynamically, each stage of evolution proceeding from the last. The rope metaphor helps — nature extending strands and developing in ever-increasing complexity — or the Mahābhārata's image of guṇas "created in regular order and dissolved in reverse order, like the waves of the sea." Īśvarakṛṣṇa offers his own analogy: the three forces are like a lamp — the commentators explain he means the oil, fire, and wick that together allow illumination.

    The first evolutes: an inner organ, not mere matter¶

    • [00:14:22] Since prakṛti is often understood materially, it's tempting to read this as a purely physical process — but the first results cast doubt on that. The immediate product of prakṛti is buddhi, also called "the Great One." It is the first thing manifest (so we can grasp it directly) and is typically rendered intellect: a kind of impersonal, undirected thought or striving to create further stages.
    • [00:14:58] Next the emerging subject acquires a sense of its own identity, in a stage literally called "I-making" (ahaṃkāra) — i.e. self-awareness, the ability to take a first-person view. Only at the next stage does Īśvarakṛṣṇa introduce mind (manas), responsible for actual thinking and perceiving. Collectively these three powers — buddhi, I-making, and mind — are the interior organ, as opposed to the external sense organs.
    • [00:15:35] So Sāṃkhya's dualism is not that of Plato or Descartes, where a mental principle is contrasted with a physical one. True, one of the two principles is pure consciousness, recalling the Cartesian mind — but all other aspects of mental life sit on the side of the other principle, prakṛti. The capacities for thinking are even the first things generated, though none could be aware at all without "the lurking presence of consciousness," prakṛti's unoriginated partner puruṣa.

    Counting up to 25¶

    • [00:16:15] We're still only at five — the two fundamental principles plus the three aspects of the interior organ. Now it speeds up: the mind generates the five sense faculties (vision, hearing, etc.) plus five anatomical powers to match (like speaking and moving). With this exterior organ added, we seem to have a well-rounded person — mental life, sensation, and abilities to interact with the world.
    • [00:16:48] The catch: there isn't really a world yet, only a person. So these 15 principles are complemented by the five sensible features of the world, which generate five bodily elements — the four familiar from Greek philosophy (air, earth, fire, water) plus ākāśa, meaning empty space or a kind of ether pervading the universe. Counting along, this finally reaches 25.

    Two loose ends: generic sensibles and a boundary-crossing puzzle¶

    • [00:17:22] Following Īśvarakṛṣṇa's example, most philosophical issues are passed over, but two are flagged — one detail, one general puzzle.
    • [00:17:34] The detail: the sensible features generated near the end are very generic — the whole phenomena of taste, smell, sound, not particular flavours or noises. The generic sensibles supposedly give rise to the elements, and particular qualities (a given colour) arise only once we have the physical elements and their compounds. Some authors, including "our new favourite philosophizing grammarian" Bhartṛhari, claim that for Sāṃkhya everyday bodies are made of sensible qualities — so a pot would be nothing more than its colour, hardness, the metallic sound it makes falling on the Swedish Chef's head, and so on. But in the earlier tradition of Īśvarakṛṣṇa, the qualities likely belong to an underlying subject — namely prakṛti, the quasi-material principle ultimately transformed into everyday things.
    • [00:18:44] The broader puzzle: the disconcerting way the 25 principles cut across cosmological and human realms — sensation and the sensible, mental and material, private and public. In the initial stages we get human psychological powers; before long those powers are "acting like a record executive in the disco era and producing earth, wind, and fire."
    • [00:19:15] One suspicion: the physical aspects of the evolution are really an illusion, a show the mind puts on for itself when bound to nature. A late passage in the Sāṃkhya-kārikā says the phenomenal world is "like a dance watched by the audience that is puruṣa." This reading could head toward later commentators (after Śaṅkara) who tried to reconcile Sāṃkhya with Advaita Vedānta's skeptical view of the phenomenal world.
    • [00:19:52] But there may be other reasons the outer world is so closely tied to the world of mind. Vedic literature constantly compared the cosmos to an individual animal, person, or god, and the threefold-guṇa theory supports this boundary-crossing: the same forces that yield a person's mind and body also give rise to the physical universe.

    Why Sāṃkhya and Vedānta cannot fully agree¶

    • [00:20:18] There are major obstacles to reconciling Sāṃkhya with Vedānta. Sāṃkhya embraces two fundamental principles where Vedānta has one, and it resists the Vedāntic notion of a single true self (brahman). The fact that there are different courses of life and experiences proves, says Īśvarakṛṣṇa, that puruṣa is many — "you have one pure consciousness and I have another."

    Liberation: identifying with the passive witness¶

    • [00:20:50] Yet there is deep agreement between the two Vedic schools: both are convinced that knowledge of their teachings brings liberation. The very first sentence of the Sāṃkhya-kārikā states the purpose of the teaching is the escape from suffering. The early thinker Pañcaśikha held this out for all comers, not just the priestly caste: a knower of the 25 principles, "in whatever order of life... whether he wears braided hair, a topknot, or is shaven, is liberated from existence."
    • [00:21:24] But why would counting to 25 help achieve this? One suspicion is that Īśvarakṛṣṇa is just keeping up with the Buddhists, who built their whole teaching around the pervasiveness of suffering and the path of escape. Still, Sāṃkhya is from its foundations built to pursue liberation. Positing puruṣa addresses philosophy of mind by making pure consciousness prior to all experience — but Īśvarakṛṣṇa insists that without puruṣa there can be no liberation: only a pure, contentless consciousness can stand outside the turmoil of the world that emerges from prakṛti. Consciousness can declare itself satisfied at having seen the evolved world, while nature is satisfied at having shown itself and been seen.
    • [00:22:25] So we know what must be given up to be liberated: everything. We must let go of all physical action, all sensation, all mental activity, even any sense of an individual self — saying "I do not exist, nothing is mine, I am not." This is what it means to identify with the passive witness, puruṣa.
    • [00:22:46] Īśvarakṛṣṇa calls this an escape from ignorance. Ignorance is not why the world evolved from prakṛti — that was a spontaneous natural process. Ignorance is rather what keeps us bound to the world once it has evolved. The bondage is unnecessary, because we can simply choose to cast ourselves as audience instead of performer. To take oneself to be puruṣa is to be liberated, simply because puruṣa stands outside the evolutionary process — it was never unfree.
    • [00:23:24] This is asking a lot. Īśvarakṛṣṇa would be content if you keep seeing yourself as "something more modest, the audience of this podcast." Next time: ancient Indian medicine, closely connected to both Sāṃkhya and its partner tradition yoga — complete with medical puns from the Muppet Show's veterinarian's hospital sketch ("Dr. Bob, how can you stand to wait until the next episode?" — "Easy. I have lots of patience.").

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Sāṃkhya — the "counting / enumeration" tradition; itemizes the 25 principles, 8 tendencies, and 50 results; arguably the oldest Vedic school, dualist and aimed at liberation.
    • Īśvarakṛṣṇa — author of the Sāṃkhya-kārikā (c. 350–450 CE), the oldest surviving Sāṃkhya treatise; a late figure whose version came to dominate.
    • Sāṃkhya-kārikā — the verse treatise; states its purpose is escape from suffering and lays out the 25-principle evolution.
    • Kapila / Āsuri / Pañcaśikha — the legendary earlier teachers: the "Great Sage" founder Kapila, his successors Āsuri and Pañcaśikha (who defeated 100 sages in debate and offered liberation to all castes).
    • puruṣa — pure consciousness; the inactive witness, the subject underlying all objects of awareness; shown to be many, one per person.
    • prakṛti — nature / primordial matter; productive but unintelligent and unmanifest; transforms step-by-step into all things while puruṣa watches.
    • the blind man and the lame man — image of puruṣa and prakṛti's cooperation: nature is blind (no consciousness), consciousness is lame (inert without nature).
    • effects pre-exist in causes — the doctrine justifying evolution; pots are "already in the clay," so things come from being, not nothing.
    • three sources of knowledge — perception, inference, valid testimony; prakṛti is known only by inference (from the order and limit in things).
    • guṇa — "cord/thread"; the functional mode or tendency in prakṛti driving change; three intertwined strands.
    • sattva / rajas / tamas — the three guṇas: lightness/pleasure (and liberation); change/upheaval/suffering; heaviness/resistance/despondency — each both cosmological and psychological.
    • buddhi — intellect, "the Great One"; first evolute, impersonal undirected thought.
    • ahaṃkāra ("I-making") — self-awareness, the first-person stance; second evolute.
    • manas (mind) — actual thinking and perceiving; with buddhi and ahaṃkāra forms the interior organ.
    • ākāśa — empty space or pervading ether; fifth element alongside air, earth, fire, water.
    • liberation — identifying with puruṣa, the audience not the performer; an escape from the ignorance that binds us to the evolved world.

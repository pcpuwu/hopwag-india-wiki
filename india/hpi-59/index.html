    HoPWaG India · Ep 59 — "Looking East: Indian Influence on Greek Thought"¶

    ← Ep 58

    Ep 60 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:22:35 In brief: The series' one episode on India's possible influence on Greece — surveying the real channels of contact (trade, the Persian court, Alexander), Thomas McEvilley's catalogue of parallels, the formidable obstacles to transmission, and the two best test cases (Pyrrho and Plotinus), reaching the measured verdict that some Indian ideas probably did filter west, but only intermittently and incidentally, never as the detailed, well-informed engagement seen in the Greek-Arabic-Latin translation movements. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit/Greek terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gannery"; Pyrrho of Elis ≈ "Pyro of Ellis"; Sextus Empiricus ≈ "Sexus Empiricus"; Thomas McEvilley ≈ "McEvely/McEvilly"; Megasthenes ≈ "Magasthenes/Megastanis"; Seleucus ≈ "Seljukos"; Strabo ≈ "Strebo"; Diogenes Laertius ≈ "Diogenes Laersius"; gymnosophists ≈ "naked sawfists"; Taxila ≈ "Tuxina"; ataraxia ≈ "Atadaxia"; Everard Flintoff ≈ "Flintov"; Anaximenes ≈ "anacymines"; Parmenides ≈ "Permanides"; Madhyamaka ≈ "Madyamika"; Iamblichus ≈ "Iamblicus"; adiaphora ≈ "a-diaphora"; anātman ≈ "un-Otmahn"; svabhāva ≈ "Svavava"; Porphyry ≈ "Porfrey"; Hecataeus ≈ "Hecateus").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Thomas McEvilley — author of the massive The Shape of Ancient Greek Thought, which detects echoes of Indian ideas in nearly every Greek philosopher [00:05:31].
    • Megasthenes — Hellenistic ambassador to the Mauryan court; our best eyewitness on Indian culture, surviving only in quotations [00:04:21].
    • Pyrrho of Elis — founder of Hellenistic skepticism; said to have travelled with Alexander to India and met its sages [00:11:35].
    • Plotinus — 3rd-c. AD founder of Neoplatonism; joined an expedition east hoping to learn Persian and Indian philosophy [00:16:54].

    The most common question — and why it's the wrong reason to care¶

    • [00:00:31] If you give public lectures on ancient philosophy, you are "almost guaranteed" to be asked whether Greek thought was influenced by ancient Indian culture — perhaps the single most common question from general audiences. It's understandable: what could be more intriguing than that Plato, Aristotle, or Plotinus drew on the Upaniṣads, early Buddhism, or Vedānta?
    • [00:01:07] Taking the possibility seriously threatens to undermine the very contrast between "Eastern" and "Western" philosophy — which is fine by the hosts, a contrast that deserves undermining anyway.
    • [00:01:15] What they find more problematic is the unspoken assumption behind the question: that if Indian thought really did influence Greece, that would secure India a rightful place in the history of philosophy. If this series has been even halfway successful, you should already be convinced Indian thought deserves that place in its own right.
    • [00:01:35] The same distortion afflicts philosophy in the Islamic world: Avicenna and Averroes have drawn attention chiefly because they influenced Latin Christendom, not as thinkers worth studying for themselves — and the vast number of Muslim philosophers after the 12th century are omitted from most histories simply because they came too late for the Arabic-Latin translation movement and were unknown in Europe.
    • [00:02:08] So as we examine influence, remember that the real importance of Indian philosophy is its intrinsic fascination. And if we were going to justify interest by influence, we'd do far better to stress India's massive impact on China and other parts of Asia, which on any reckoning dwarfs whatever it contributed to ancient Europe.

    Was there any contact at all? — trade and the Persian meeting place¶

    • [00:02:41] The obvious first question: was there any contact and cultural exchange between ancient Greek and Indian cultures at all? The answer "can only be a resounding yes." Trade routes between Europe and India were open well before Presocratic philosophy even emerged.
    • [00:02:58] Scattered literary testimonies survive: the Hippocratic corpus mentions pepper, Sophocles Indian gold, Xenophon Indian hunting dogs. The Persian Empire would have offered a meeting place — the army Xerxes led against the Greeks apparently included Indian soldiers, and Greek and Indian scholars would have met at the Persian court.
    • [00:03:28] Still, during classical antiquity — the era of the Presocratics, Plato, and Aristotle — knowledge of India was limited and indirect. The most valuable information sat in ethnographic writings now lost, notably by the "fabulously named" Skylax, who in the 6th century BC joined a military expedition to India under the Persian king Darius. Herodotus mentions that expedition and draws on another ethnographer, Hecataeus.

    Alexander, Megasthenes, and the śramaṇa movements¶

    • [00:04:02] The key moment comes around the time of Aristotle, with the conquests of Alexander the Great. Reaching Bactria and India in about 330 BC, he brought Greek culture, founded Hellenistic cities, and created the conditions for deeper engagement with Indian society.
    • [00:04:21] That engagement is most palpable in Megasthenes, an ambassador to the Mauryan court sent by Seleucus, the Hellenistic king who succeeded Alexander in the conquered eastern lands. Megasthenes dealt with the mighty emperor Chandragupta and may have visited the great city of Pāṭaliputra, so he could describe Indian culture as a direct witness — though using the tropes of Greek ethnography and making "perplexingly basic errors," for instance denying that slavery existed in ancient India.
    • [00:04:51] Megasthenes' work too is lost, except in quotations by later authors, notably the historian Strabo. Thanks to him we know Megasthenes was aware of the śramaṇa ascetic movements, even contrasting two kinds of renouncers: those who stayed in the city working as healers, and those who withdrew to the forest.
    • [00:05:14] All of this makes it "eminently plausible" that Indian philosophical ideas could have reached Greek intellectuals — and anyone who reads in both traditions will hit on striking parallels.

    McEvilley's catalogue of resonances¶

    • [00:05:25] Someone who has done far more than read around a bit is Thomas McEvilley, whose massive The Shape of Ancient Greek Thought detects echoes of Indian ideas in pretty much every Greek philosopher, from the Presocratics down to the late Neoplatonists. A sampling:
    • Matching lists of fundamental elements — air, earth, fire, water — in both cultures.
    • Both Heraclitus and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa saying divine forces play "like a child."
    • The Ṛg Veda making ocean the source of all things, as Thales made water his primary principle; the Upaniṣads identifying air as primary, agreeing with Anaximenes.
    • The Pythagorean doctrines of vegetarianism and reincarnation sounding "rather Indian."
    • The monist trend in Vedic thought culminating in Advaita Vedānta echoed in Parmenides — and the Chāndogya Upaniṣad sounding like Parmenides when it denies anything can come to be from nothing.
    • [00:06:33] For later philosophy, McEvilley remarks it is hard to identify any significant difference between the methods or stated purposes of Pyrrhonist and Madhyamaka dialectic — a strong parallel between ancient skepticism and Buddhism — and compares the philosophically motivated rituals of Tantra to theurgy in Neoplatonists like Iamblichus.

    Three explanations: influence, coincidence, deep shared roots¶

    • [00:06:56] McEvilley is largely content to flag the echoes without committing to one explanation. Alongside more-or-less direct influence from India, he admits two other possibilities.
    • [00:07:08] Coincidence. We have two enormous bodies of writing; if we trawled the thousands of pages of Pāli and Sanskrit philosophy and found nothing reminiscent of anything Greek, that would be extraordinary — especially since philosophical reflection is bound to throw up certain fundamental ideas independently (atomism in physics, skepticism in epistemology, monism in metaphysics). A skeptic could see McEvilley's resume of resonances as just similar ideas reached independently.
    • [00:07:49] Deep shared roots. McEvilley seems attracted to the idea of obscure common origins behind both traditions — shared mythic tendencies also found in Near Eastern and Egyptian religion, pointing to cultural connections from before historical records. That might explain the urge to trace all things back to one physical element. But it is "unsatisfyingly vague," and not really enough to account for the more exact parallels, where the resonance is so strong one suspects direct textual appropriation.
    • [00:08:25] Tellingly, in his many hundreds of pages McEvilley offers few parallels of that exact sort. The one he treats as something close to genuine proof: a testimony that Heraclitus explained night and day by two exhalations, one bright and one dark — the same idea found in the Chāndogya and Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("Great Forest") Upaniṣads.
    • [00:09:03] Anticipating readers who think a shared element-list is just obvious, McEvilley notes that China had a different list — water, fire, wood, metal, earth. (Adamson adds that 1970s disco culture got it down to three: "Earth, Wind & Fire.")

    The obstacles in the way of transmission¶

    • [00:09:24] To decide whether there was real historical influence, it helps to think harder about how it could have happened — and there were formidable obstacles.
    • [00:09:40] Distance. The two civilizations were separated by thousands of miles; it is not obvious that complex philosophical ideas could travel that far alongside pepper and luxury goods.
    • [00:09:53] Language. Profound cultural exchange in the history of philosophy typically involved a deliberate translation effort — rich Muslims commissioning Arabic versions of Greek science in the 8th–9th centuries AD, Latin Christians translating from Arabic and Greek in the 12th. Nothing like that happened here: there was no Sanskrit-Greek translation movement, indeed not a single case of a philosophical work from India being translated into Greek or Latin in antiquity.
    • [00:10:29] Elite exclusivity. The ancient Indian elite excluded almost all other Indians from their intellectual activities, never mind distrusted foreign "barbarians." It is more plausible that the less-exclusivist śramaṇa philosophers — Buddhists, Jains, and Ājīvikas — were open to sharing ideas with outsiders. And any ideas that did fight past these obstacles would likely have been greatly oversimplified or distorted in transmission.

    What would convince a skeptic — and enter Pyrrho¶

    • [00:11:00] To persuade cautious historians of profound, direct engagement, they'll want an "intrepid traveler" — in practice a Greek who reached India, since no Indian thinker seems to have gone to Europe. They'll need not just evocative parallels but historical evidence that the thinker was genuinely inspired by Indian ideas — and, given the exclusivity point, the ideas should likely be śramaṇa, not Brahminical.
    • [00:11:35] "Step forward, Pyrrho of Elis" — putative founder of Hellenistic skepticism and namesake of the radical Pyrrhonian skepticism later adopted by the 2nd-c. AD author Sextus Empiricus. Excitingly, Diogenes Laertius gives a detailed ancient life reporting that Pyrrho accompanied Alexander's expedition to India, where he met "naked sophists" — the gymnosophists, ascetic philosophers said elsewhere to have been encountered by Alexander's entourage near Taxila.
    • [00:12:11] Diogenes says it was a direct result of meeting these sages that Pyrrho adopted his signature policy of suspending judgment — since our beliefs are only matters of custom, and in themselves things are "no more one thing than another." On this basis scholars like Everard Flintoff and, more recently, Christopher Beckwith have argued Pyrrho's introduction of skepticism to Greek philosophy was a more-or-less direct borrowing from early Indian skepticism. Flintoff leaves open which ascetic movement Pyrrho met; Beckwith wrote an entire book arguing the Pyrrho stories are crucial evidence for the nature of early Buddhism.
    • [00:12:58] The hypothesis is attractive because Pyrrho was renowned for achieving ataraxia — freedom from disturbance, impervious to physical and mental pain, just as a liberated Buddhist is free from all suffering. And he reached it precisely by suspending judgment about all things, using techniques to puncture the pretensions of "dogmatists." Among them was an argument resembling the tetralemma, the fourfold menu of options used so effectively by Nāgārjuna — found in early reports of the Buddha himself, in a testimony about Pyrrho, and across Sextus (even though such forms appear already in Plato and Aristotle).

    Why the Buddha is not a Pyrrhonian¶

    • [00:13:53] There is "one small problem": Buddhists are very far from being Pyrrhonian skeptics. The Buddha did sometimes refuse to answer philosophical questions, but typically because the questions made misleading presuppositions — e.g. assuming the existence of the self — not because he suspended judgment.
    • [00:14:15] Indeed Buddhism's most signature commitment, the doctrine of no-self, would count as a negative dogmatic belief to any self-respecting (or "no-self-respecting") Pyrrhonian, who would suspend judgment about whether there is a self. Likewise each of the four noble truths is a straightforward assertion a Pyrrhonist would decline to endorse.
    • [00:14:46] Beckwith "rather cheats" by trying to make the Buddha a universal skeptic like Pyrrho — suggesting that when Pyrrho called all things adiaphora (undifferentiated, not distinct) he meant the same as the Buddha's anātman (lack of self or independent reality). But the notions are very different: Pyrrho meant we can never judge anything to be more one thing than another (no action more good than bad), whereas the Buddha was rejecting a specific metaphysical idea — far from suspending judgment about ātman, he forthrightly denied it, and the skeptical consequences were never meant to undermine Buddhism's positive doctrines like the four noble truths.

    Sextus versus Nāgārjuna¶

    • [00:15:34] Jumping ahead to the 2nd-c. AD successors — Sextus and Nāgārjuna — yields the same conclusion. Nāgārjuna's dialectical subtlety in undermining rivals is comparable to Sextus's, but Sextus would unhesitatingly classify him as a negative dogmatist, not a true skeptic, because Nāgārjuna was committed to the principle that all things are empty and dependently arising.
    • [00:16:04] Whatever exactly he meant by that (much disputed), Sextus would want to suspend judgment about whether all things are empty: there are, he'd say, good arguments on both sides of the debate between upholders of svabhāva (intrinsic reality) and adherents of emptiness.
    • [00:16:23] The upshot is not that we can rule out Buddhist influence on Pyrrho. The likelier conclusion satisfies "neither the skeptic nor the enthusiast": Pyrrho may have encountered and been inspired by Indian philosophers on campaign with Alexander, but — given the cultural and linguistic barriers — he failed to understand what they were saying, or at best borrowed from them very selectively.

    Plotinus and the lure of Vedānta¶

    • [00:16:54] Several centuries on, another, even more influential Greek has often been tied to Indian thought: Plotinus, the 3rd-c. AD founder of Neoplatonism. As with Pyrrho, we have evidence of both interest and eastern travel: his student, editor, and biographer Porphyry reports that in 242 AD Plotinus joined the expedition of the emperor Gordian III precisely hoping to learn about the philosophy of the Persians and Indians.
    • [00:17:28] Unfortunately the expedition was an abortive one, so it can't really prove Plotinus had the encounter he hoped for. He never mentions India in his own writings — but Porphyry does: his work on vegetarianism has a reasonably well-informed section on Indian practices, and Augustine tells us that in another (now lost) work Porphyry set out to compare Neoplatonic philosophy with "the practices and teachings of the Indians." So Plotinus had both the motive and the means to access Indian ideas, if only indirectly.
    • [00:18:08] Are there signs of influence? Positive answers compare his Neoplatonism with Vedānta, especially the Advaita variety. This is chronologically awkward — Śaṅkara lived about half a millennium after Plotinus — but perhaps Plotinus was picking up the same older Indian ideas that would later inspire Advaita, notably the Upaniṣadic concept of brahman. In apparent agreement with that strand, Plotinus identifies the paradigmatic source of being as a single universal mind and urges us to realize our unity with it, making the physical universe nothing but an image or outward manifestation of intellect — the seat of knowledge and (we might venture) consciousness or selfhood. He also carries forward earlier Greek themes that recall India, such as reincarnation.

    The case against — and a methodological conundrum¶

    • [00:19:08] The main argument against Indian inspiration is that Plotinus "could, and quite evidently did," develop all these ideas through engagement with earlier Platonism. His universal mind is also the realm of Platonic Forms — not a new idea so much as a modification of Middle Platonism, the tradition of reflection on Plato's dialogues in the generations before him. Some contemporaries even accused Plotinus of stealing his whole system from the Middle Platonist Numenius — surely exaggerated, but "with a grain of truth."
    • [00:19:45] Hence a methodological conundrum: should we accept claims of intercultural influence only when developments cannot be explained as internal to a tradition? That seems unduly demanding — nothing prevents Plotinus from being inspired both by his Platonist predecessors and by Indian ideas, especially if he saw them as agreeing.
    • [00:20:13] The Plotinus-Vedānta comparison is further complicated by the fact that Plotinus posits a higher principle above the universal mind — a pure unity that is explicitly not a mind and does not think about anything, even itself. And if we wanted to locate a self or seat of consciousness in Plotinus, it fits the individual soul better than the universal mind. As with Pyrrho, initially striking parallels look less exact once we attend to the details.
    • [00:20:43] Then again, that is just what we should expect: where access to Indian thought was bound to be very imperfect, why expect perfect parallels? In that spirit even sympathetic scholars reach half-hearted conclusions — McEvilley writes that although it was "virtually certain" Plotinus had some contact with Indian ideas, that contact "probably didn't amount to much," and the similarities may stem from the longer-standing presence of Indian themes already in Greek thought. Another recent assessment guesses Greco-Roman authors had more information than sources survive, "though probably in a very general and distorted way."

    The overall verdict¶

    • [00:21:36] Such moderate conclusions are salutary, because by the standards of the history of philosophy this question evokes unusually strong feelings: many want to believe the influence was real and deeply significant; others — not least scholars of Greek philosophy who'd rather not have to learn Indian philosophy — are equally determined to exclude it entirely.
    • [00:21:57] The honest verdict: we should almost certainly admit that ideas did filter into the ancient European world from Indian culture. But in stark contrast to the tremendous, determinative impact of Greek ideas on the Islamic world, and of Islamic-world works on Latin Christendom, the Indian contribution to ancient Greek thought was intermittent and rather incidental — rarely if ever a detailed, well-informed engagement with Indian philosophy by Greek or Roman intellectuals.
    • [00:22:27] For that kind of engagement we must wait for later moments of cultural exchange — the topic of the next episode of the history of philosophy in India.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Thomas McEvilley — author of The Shape of Ancient Greek Thought, cataloguing echoes of Indian ideas across Greek philosophy; flags three explanations (influence, coincidence, deep shared roots) without firmly choosing.
    • Megasthenes — Seleucus's ambassador to Chandragupta's Mauryan court; eyewitness on India (and on the śramaṇa renouncers), surviving only in quotations by Strabo.
    • śramaṇa — the non-Brahminical ascetic/renouncer movements (Buddhists, Jains, Ājīvikas); less exclusivist, so likelier than Brahmins to share ideas with foreigners.
    • gymnosophists — the "naked sophists" / ascetic philosophers Alexander's entourage met near Taxila; the sages Pyrrho is said to have encountered.
    • Pyrrho of Elis — founder of Hellenistic (Pyrrhonian) skepticism; reportedly took his policy of suspending judgment from meeting Indian sages on Alexander's campaign.
    • ataraxia — Greek "freedom from disturbance"; Pyrrho's goal, compared to a liberated Buddhist's freedom from suffering.
    • tetralemma — the fourfold argument-menu used by Nāgārjuna, with parallels in reports of the Buddha, Pyrrho, and Sextus.
    • Sextus Empiricus — 2nd-c. AD Pyrrhonist who would class both the Buddha and Nāgārjuna as negative dogmatists, not true skeptics.
    • Nāgārjuna — 2nd-c. AD Madhyamaka Buddhist committed to the thesis that all things are empty and dependently arising (hence dogmatic to a Pyrrhonist).
    • anātman / svabhāva — Buddhist denial of a self vs. the intrinsic, independent reality (svabhāva) that emptiness denies; the Buddha rejects the self rather than suspending judgment about it.
    • four noble truths — straightforward Buddhist assertions a Pyrrhonist would refuse to endorse — proof the Buddha was no skeptic.
    • Plotinus — 3rd-c. AD founder of Neoplatonism; joined Gordian III's expedition (242 AD) hoping to learn Indian/Persian philosophy; his "universal mind" invites comparison with Advaita Vedānta's brahman.
    • Porphyry — Plotinus's biographer; wrote on Indian practices and (per Augustine) compared Neoplatonism with Indian teachings.
    • Advaita Vedānta / brahman — the monist Upaniṣadic strand (later systematized by Śaṅkara, half a millennium after Plotinus) whose ultimate reality is likened to Plotinus's universal mind.
    • Middle Platonism / Numenius — the internal Greek tradition that can fully explain Plotinus's system, the chief argument against needing Indian influence to account for it.

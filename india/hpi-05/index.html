    HoPWaG India · Ep 5 — "Indra's Search: The Self in the Upaniṣads"¶

    ← Ep 4

    Ep 6 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:44 In brief: The gods are everywhere in the Upaniṣads yet do surprisingly little philosophical work; the episode reads the Chāndogya story of the god Indra's century-long apprenticeship under Prajāpati as a parable about the self (ātman) — something so close to us that it can never be an object of perception, knowable only obliquely in the very act of perceiving and thinking. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Canary"; Prajāpati ≈ "Prachapati / Prachabati / Prajabati"; Virocana ≈ "Virochana"; Yājñavalkya ≈ "Yajnavakya / Yajnyalvakya / Yajnial Valkya"; Maitreyī ≈ "Maitra Yi"; Chāndogya ≈ "Chandogya"; Bṛhadāraṇyaka ≈ "great forest Upanishad"; Bimal Krishna Matilal ≈ "Bimal Krishna Matilal"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 5 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019). Its sources & a reading list are under Further reading below.

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • The Chāndogya Upaniṣad — source of the Indra/Prajāpati "search for the self" story that anchors the episode [00:05:21].
    • Indra — the most prominent god of the Ṛgveda, here cast not as creator-king but as a student searching for his true self [00:04:38].
    • Prajāpati — the reluctant teacher-creator who feeds Indra one false account of the self after another [00:06:04].
    • Yājñavalkya — Upaniṣadic sage of the Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("great forest") Upaniṣad, whose dialogue with his wife Maitreyī supplies the "not, not" and salt-mass images [00:13:20].
    • Bimal Krishna Matilal — modern scholar quoted to the effect that, in Indian philosophy, the gods are "window dressing" [00:03:48].

    The gods in the texts — and the contrast with Europe¶

    • [00:00:38] One way to mark Indian philosophy off from European: it "first emerged and was always pursued in a religious context." We think of Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism primarily as religions, not schools of philosophy — even though they produced rich philosophical argument.
    • [00:00:59] But the contrast can be challenged from both sides. Religious ideas were rarely far from European thinkers either — from ancient pagans to medieval theologians and beyond. And it is "notoriously difficult" to say whether, or in what sense, Buddhism is even a religion. Conveniently, this is a podcast on the history of philosophy, not of religion, so the issue can be set aside [00:01:23].
    • [00:01:28] Still, the religious elements in these ancient Indian texts have to be noticed. The most striking is the sheer presence of gods, who appear as characters in the Upaniṣads and the Mahābhārata. Even the Buddhists, who rejected much of Brahmanic ritual and doctrine, kept recognizing and talking about the gods [00:01:46].

    What philosophical work do the gods do? — natural theology and creation¶

    • [00:01:56] The live question: what philosophical relevance do the gods carry? Can we speak of a "natural theology" in Buddhism or in Hindu sacred texts? Only, Adamson says, if we drop some of the expectations that phrase usually brings.
    • [00:02:11] A striking absence matches the striking presence of gods: there is little interest in creation of the world in the strong sense — bringing the universe into being out of nothing, at a distinct point in time. Instead the standard picture is one of world cycles, each lasting a vast period and followed by another equally long one.
    • [00:02:35] There are partial exceptions, flagged back in episode 3: in the Ṛgveda the gods extract the species of animals from Puruṣa, and one god, Prajāpati, is briefly a creator. He returns in the Mahābhārata, first fashioning a body for himself, then the underlying matter of the whole universe, from which he makes everything else [00:02:47].

    From the book — ch. 5

    The book gives the Sanskrit term for that "underlying matter" Prajāpati fashions before all else: pradhāna — the primordial stuff from which the rest of the universe is then made.

    • [00:02:58] The Upaniṣads too sometimes convey that all things are caused by or depend on something — there brahman is the fundamental support of all reality. Indeed the word brahman means "that which bears up all things" [00:03:11].
    • [00:03:18] Yet if brahman is divine, it is a markedly impersonal divinity: rarely said to create, reward or punish. Already in the earlier Vedas the universe has a moral order independent of any theological force. That continues with karma, where a god's role is at most a kind of "cosmic bookkeeping" — invoked only to guarantee that our good and bad deeds actually shape our future fates [00:03:31].

    Matilal: the gods as "window dressing"¶

    • [00:03:48] For these reasons the great scholar Bimal Krishna Matilal wrote that in Indian philosophy the gods are essentially "window dressing" — more a part of India's cultural heritage than anything load-bearing.
    • [00:04:01] And yet the gods are still there. We may find no creation from nothing and no divine-command theory of ethics, but gods populate the philosophical texts — and even take part in the philosophical discussions [00:04:07].
    • [00:04:14] Examples: the teacher at the centre of the Bhagavad-Gītā is Kṛṣṇa, an avatar of Viṣṇu; and gods appear as sage-like interlocutors in the Upaniṣads, perhaps as a strategy for lending authority to the teachings [00:04:27].

    Indra, the searching god (Chāndogya Upaniṣad)¶

    • [00:04:38] One of the most famous of all Upaniṣadic stories has Indra as its lead. He is instantly familiar to anyone who has paged through the Ṛgveda — though hardly its only god. Natural phenomena (wind, sun, heaven, dawn) count as deities, as do ritual elements like Agni (the ritual fire) and Soma (the sacrificial plant) [00:04:50].
    • [00:05:04] Because the Ṛgveda centres on the sacrifice of Soma, and Soma is offered up to Indra, Indra is its most prominent god — far more so than Viṣṇu, who plays only a minor part there.
    • [00:05:21] All the more noteworthy, then, that in the Chāndogya Upaniṣad Indra appears not as almighty creator or priestly king but as a figure searching after wisdom — taking to heart the ubiquitous Upaniṣadic exhortation to go in search of your true self.
    • [00:05:46] As the previous episode showed, that search ends in realizing a hidden correspondence between oneself and the cosmos, and between both and the space of the Vedic ritual. The twist: you'd expect a god to be explaining this to a human — instead Indra is the one on the journey of self-discovery [00:05:57].
    • [00:06:04] The gods and demons have heard that Prajāpati speaks of "a self by discovering which one obtains all the worlds, and all one's desires are fulfilled." This fits the Ṛgveda's Prajāpati, exalted as creator and credited with knowledge surpassing the other gods [00:06:18].

    The apprenticeship: 101 years of being fobbed off¶

    • [00:06:29] Indra, together with a demon named Virocana, goes to live as a celibate in Prajāpati's āśrama to learn the secret of the self. The first lesson is patience: Prajāpati says nothing for 32 years, then only asks them what they want [00:06:36].
    • [00:06:49] Up to here, Adamson jokes, the story resembles trying to renew your driver's license — but no government bureaucrat would deliver the conversation that follows. When Prajāpati hears their quest, he "fobs them off with an answer he knows to be false."
    • [00:07:12] First false answer — the mirror-image self. Look into a pan of water, he says; what you see is the self. They dress up, look again, and report seeing themselves "beautifully adorned, well dressed, all spruced up." "That is the self, that is the immortal, that is the one free from fear, that is brahman," Prajāpati tells them — and the two leave with contented hearts [00:07:59].
    • [00:08:05] Watching them go, Prajāpati remarks: "there they go, without learning about the self." Virocana is satisfied; Indra is not. He reasons that the reflected self cannot be the true self, for if the body can be made beautiful it can equally be made lame and crippled. He turns back for more instruction [00:08:19].
    • [00:08:32] Another 32-year wait (Indra now has the patience of "a major city hospital"), and a second fobbing-off. Second false answer — the dreaming self: "the one who goes happily about in a dream, that is the self… that is brahman." Indra falls for it, then on reflection sees that the dreaming self still suffers anxiety and disappointment [00:08:49].
    • [00:09:01] Back again, another 32 years, and a third false answer — the dreamless-sleep self: "when one is fast asleep, totally collected and serene, and sees no dreams, that is the self." Again Indra leaves happy, again the doubts return.
    • [00:09:26] This time he waits only five more years — a total of 101 years, which (Adamson notes) "by an amazing coincidence" is roughly how long it'll take to cover the whole history of philosophy in podcast form. Now Indra is finally given the true answer — and we, mercifully, only wait a few minutes for it [00:09:38].

    How knowledge works: the do-it-yourself self¶

    • [00:09:51] Before the payoff, dwell on the manner of the story. Prajāpati shows astonishing reluctance and a willingness to deceive — not just the demon Virocana but a fellow god — and insists on long stretches of harsh living as the price of instruction [00:09:57].
    • [00:10:10] Crucially, Indra only improves by going away content with an answer and then beginning to question it. The ever-more-sophisticated accounts come as the grudging concessions of a teacher reluctant to share his wisdom even with another divinity [00:10:22].
    • [00:10:34] There's an implied theory of learning here, at least for the central Upaniṣadic topic of the self: one cannot leap straight to an adequate grasp. Before each new stage, Indra must first understand what was inadequate about the previous one. One must see why each idea of the self is false even to speculate on the truth of the next [00:11:06]. Had Indra not seen that the self is no reflection, he couldn't appreciate the subtler doctrine that the true self is met in dreams.
    • [00:11:23] And Prajāpati lets Indra discover each flaw for himself. Indra must not merely be told he is not a reflection — he must come to see the flaw "as the result of his own personal investigation." So Prajāpati could neither hand over the final doctrine nor announce that the earlier ones were false; he could only feed Indra each doctrine and wait to see if Indra would find it wanting [00:11:40].

    Why the self is so hard to find¶

    • [00:11:53] Why does discovering the self demand this do-it-yourself approach — taking even a god over a century? The answer: the self is, ironically, "so close that it is nearly impossible to find" [00:12:12].
    • [00:12:20] Things outside us are possible objects of sensation: to perceive the Eiffel Tower you fly to Paris. But "no plane ticket" brings you to a vantage point from which to behold the self. Nor can it be reached by deduction or by another person's testimony — as later Indian philosophers would put it, there is no pramāṇa (means of knowledge) that leads to knowledge of the self [00:12:36].
    • [00:12:50] As the last episode suggested, brahman and the self make knowledge possible without themselves being knowable. Our natural instinct is to hunt the self among the objects of consciousness, because that strategy works for everything else — but in this one case it is exactly wrong [00:13:01].

    Yājñavalkya: "not, not" — the self that can't be objectified¶

    • [00:13:09] Other Upaniṣadic passages confirm it: the self is not within reach of the senses and mind precisely because it is what makes sensing and thinking possible — "too close to be seen." This is voiced in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("great forest") Upaniṣad by the sage Yājñavalkya in dialogue with his wife Maitreyī [00:13:20].
    • [00:13:34] His argument: where there is a duality, one thing can smell, see, hear, greet, think of and perceive another. But when "the whole has become one's very self," there is no longer any other to smell or see, and nothing by which to do so. "By what means can one perceive the perceiver?" [00:14:04].
    • [00:14:12] Hence of the self one can only say "not, not" (neti neti): it is ungraspable (cannot be grasped), undecaying (not subject to decay), with nothing sticking to it; not bound, neither trembling in fear nor suffering injury. The self is "not even a possible target of language" [00:14:42]. As another passage puts it: you can't see the seer who sees, hear the hearer who hears, think the thinker who thinks, perceive the perceiver who perceives — "the self within all is this self of yours" [00:15:03].

    Re-reading Indra's three false answers¶

    • [00:15:10] The framework now makes sense of Indra's education. The first two bogus answers tried to make the self a possible object of consciousness — first an object of sensory awareness (the reflected image), then an object of dream consciousness. Each fails because the self is the subject of sensation, not something sensed [00:15:24].
    • [00:15:34] The third answer moves to the idea that the self is no object of consciousness at all, but simply whatever undergoes dreamless, contentless sleep. Indra rejects even this — because that self "is in fact nothing at all." As he observes, in dreamless sleep the self "does not even know any of these beings here, it has become completely annihilated" [00:15:52].

    The true answer: the self as that-which-is-aware¶

    • [00:16:03] Having diagnosed every false proposal — and shown himself, Adamson quips, "more expert" a waiter than any restaurant employs — Indra is ready for the truth [00:16:05].
    • [00:16:11] The answer: "the one who is aware, 'let me smell this' — that is the self," the faculty of smell merely enabling him to smell; likewise for speech, hearing and thought. The self is that which underlies thought, speech and sensation [00:16:44].
    • [00:16:55] The chariot analogy (from another Upaniṣad): intellect is the charioteer, the mind the reins, the body the chariot, the senses the horses. But that risks suggesting too active a self. Strictly, Prajāpati's self "is the source of neither action nor will." It stands still, observing itself as it sees, hears and thinks — not merely the one who sees, nor the one who decides to look, but the one aware of seeing and looking [00:17:19].

    From the book — ch. 5

    The book names the source the spoken version leaves as "another Upaniṣad": the chariot analogy is from the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (3.3–4).

    • [00:17:31] Even so it is not a detached, impassive self disengaged from its own desires and actions, because it is aware of itself even as it pursues them. The self is "that in virtue of which the subject of consciousness is self-conscious," while never itself being an object of consciousness [00:17:44].

    Self-discovery without an object: catching the self in its activity¶

    • [00:17:50] The remaining puzzle: in what can self-discovery consist if the self is not something we can discover? A hint from Yājñavalkya in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka [00:17:57]: when a drum is beaten, you cannot catch the external sounds on their own — you catch them only "by getting hold of the drum or the men beating the drum."
    • [00:18:13] So too: we catch the sensing self only by getting hold of the sensing; we catch the thinker only by getting hold of the thinking. We reach the self "not directly, but by catching it in its activity" — found "in the shadowy edges of experience" [00:18:33].
    • [00:18:38] A modern gloss: the self is known only through the phenomenal quality of thinking — "the flavor of the experience of what it is like to think." There is something it feels like, from within, to be thinking; focusing on that, rather than on the thought one is thinking, makes one obliquely aware of the self [00:18:49].
    • [00:18:55] So we should abandon the instinctive assumption that the self is a core or centre of consciousness. Rather it "pervades all of consciousness" and can be extracted from it [00:19:00].
    • [00:19:07] Yājñavalkya's image (preferred even over Prajāpati's): "as a mass of salt has no distinctive core and surface, the whole thing is a single mass of flavor, so… this self has no distinctive core and surface — the whole thing is a compact mass of cognition" [00:19:20]. The self is hard to find precisely because it is always present:

    From the book — ch. 5

    The book supplies the Sanskrit beneath the image, and a small pun the spoken version drops: the self is "extracted" from consciousness — pra-jñāna — and the salt-mass is a "compact mass (ghana) of cognition (prajñāna)," so the very word for the extraction echoes the word for the cognition being extracted. the phenomenal character of experience is barely noticeable, hidden behind the false desires of our worldly concerns [00:19:34].

    Is this religion? — and where we go next¶

    • [00:19:44] Though the message passes from one god to another, it is "not necessarily religious in character." The self-knowledge here does resemble a mystic's awareness of God — graspable only indirectly, barely sayable [00:19:57]. But identifying that experience with a mystical awareness of the divine is a further step, taken only in the more theistic, later Upaniṣads [00:20:02].
    • [00:20:15] Besides, when most people think "religion and the Upaniṣads," it isn't self-knowledge that comes to mind — it's the cycle of rebirth, and above all the law of karma governing each individual's fate in the next life [00:20:20]. How distinctive of the Upaniṣads is that idea really, and how can karma be understood philosophically? "You won't have to wait 101 years to find out" — it's the next topic [00:20:39].

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Chāndogya Upaniṣad — Upaniṣad containing the central story of Indra's 101-year search for the self under Prajāpati.
    • Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("great forest") Upaniṣad — source of Yājñavalkya's "not, not" teaching and the salt-mass and drum images.
    • Indra — the Ṛgveda's most prominent god (recipient of the Soma sacrifice); cast here as a student of the self rather than creator-king.
    • Prajāpati — creator-figure and reluctant teacher who offers Indra three deliberately false accounts of the self before the true one.
    • Virocana — the demon who studies alongside Indra but leaves satisfied with the first (mirror-image) answer.
    • Yājñavalkya — Upaniṣadic sage; in dialogue with his wife Maitreyī he denies the self can be an object of perception or language.
    • Bimal Krishna Matilal — modern scholar who called the gods "window dressing" in Indian philosophy.
    • ātman / the self — that which underlies and is aware of all thinking, speech and sensation; the subject, never an object, of consciousness; "so close it is nearly impossible to find."
    • brahman — "that which bears up all things"; the impersonal divine support of reality, which makes knowledge possible without itself being knowable.
    • pramāṇa — a means/source of knowledge; the point being that no pramāṇa yields knowledge of the self.
    • karma — the law governing each individual's fate in the next life; the god's role in it is mere "cosmic bookkeeping."
    • neti neti ("not, not") — Yājñavalkya's formula: the self can only be characterized by negation, being ungraspable and beyond language.
    • Puruṣa / Prajāpati / world-cycles — the limited creation motifs of Indian texts, set against the absence of creation-from-nothing.
    • Ṛgveda · Agni · Soma · Viṣṇu · Kṛṣṇa — the Vedic ritual backdrop: ritual fire, sacrificial plant, and gods who appear (Kṛṣṇa as Viṣṇu's avatar) as philosophical interlocutors.

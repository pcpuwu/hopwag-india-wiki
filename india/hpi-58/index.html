    HoPWaG India · Ep 58 — "Amber Carpenter on Animals in Indian Philosophy"¶

    ← Ep 57

    Ep 59 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson with Amber Carpenter 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson with Amber Carpenter · Runtime: 00:25:43 In brief: An interview on animals in classical Indian thought: they saturate the literature (fables, epics, even the divine) yet are rarely thematized as a philosophical question; Sanskrit has no single word for "animal," reincarnation folds animals into the moral economy without individuating a fixed self, and the ethic of ahiṃsā rests on a prior, unrestricted commitment to non-violence rather than on "that pig might be my uncle." Narration order by topic; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janarn Gennary"; Yale-NUS College ≈ "Yale and U.S. College"; Pañcatantra ≈ "Panchatantra"; Hitopadeśa ≈ "Hittipadasha"; Dharmaśāstra ≈ "Dharma Shasta"; Arthaśāstra ≈ "Atta/artist Shasta"; Ṛg Veda ≈ "Rig Veda"; Rāmāyaṇa ≈ "Maramayana"; Hanumān ≈ "Hanaman"; Gaṇeśa ≈ "Ganesha"; Śaṅkara ≈ "Shankra"; Mahāyāna ≈ "Mahayana"; Brahmin/Brahminical ≈ "Romanical/reprimanical"; Jains ≈ "giants/Jane").

    The guest and the topic¶

    • [00:00:25] Adamson introduces Amber Carpenter, associate professor at Yale-NUS College, for an interview on animals in Indian philosophy — a topic that "leaps to mind" when people think of the Indian tradition, given vegetarianism, reincarnation and so on.
    • [00:00:51] His opening question: what kinds of texts can we look to in order to learn about ancient Indian attitudes towards animals?

    Where the animals are: a literature saturated with beasts¶

    • [00:01:00] Carpenter: "The astonishing thing about classical Indian literature is that it's absolutely full of animals." Animals are everywhere.
    • [00:01:07] First source — the vast corpus of animal fables: tales of talking animals in social situations, used for didactic purposes. These stories are extremely old, possibly already circulating in the time of the Ṛg Veda, and were reworked constantly across centuries by different religions and circumstances, collected into compilations like the Pañcatantra and later the Hitopadeśa. They depict animals as social, interacting beings — a "background hum" of thinking about animals.
    • [00:02:15] Second source — the works of conduct: the Dharmaśāstras and Arthaśāstras (already covered earlier in the series). As part of right conduct, these give a great deal of information about how one should behave towards other animals.
    • [00:02:34] Third source — epic literature. Carpenter draws a deliberate contrast with the Greek epics, which show little concern with our interaction with animals; the Indian epics are quite different. The Mahābhārata and the Rāmāyaṇa are full of subplots involving animals — e.g. Hanumān, the talking monkey, who is "no ordinary monkey."
    • [00:03:03] A further feature: animals are even incorporated into the divine realm — e.g. Gaṇeśa, the elephant-headed god. Everywhere you look there is some awareness or incorporation of animals.

    Pervasive in literature, but not a philosophical theme¶

    • [00:03:25] The interesting twist: against that rich background, there is comparatively little active engagement with the question of animals in explicitly philosophical literature. Looking at classical Indian thought, "you might wonder what's the question about animals." Animals are pervasive but not thematized as a specific philosophical issue.
    • [00:03:55] Why? Carpenter argues it is a feature of Sanskrit that there is no word that really corresponds to "animal" the way English uses it to pick out the non-human mobile living things. The Dharmaśāstra and Arthaśāstra have many ways of grouping animals (wild vs. domesticated, etc.), but no single term meaning "all the non-human animals together." So the very question — what makes the non-human animal as a category? — does not arise in the same way.

    Rational vs. non-rational — a Greek obsession the Indians don't share¶

    • [00:04:42] Adamson contrasts the Greek tradition, where the rational/non-rational divide is pervasive: rational animals (humans) vs. non-rational animals, with debates among Stoics and Aristotelians, and figures like Plutarch and Porphyry arguing non-human animals can think or even use language. Are there debates like that in India — or even the distinction itself?
    • [00:05:14] Carpenter: it is "definitely not as much of an obsessive concern." Where some thinkers do try to say what is distinctively human, they tend to associate it not with rationality but with dharma — the ability to act for the sake of a larger or more distant good, for the sake of liberation or of what is right.
    • [00:05:49] One could connect this to a kind of rationality (and the Greeks do end up linking the notions closely), but in India that is not the keynote. The real question is not "is it rational?" but "are non-human animals different from human animals, and if so how?"
    • [00:06:18] The answer: humans are much better at acting for the sake of a distant goal — at attaining mokṣa and the broadly ethical things. This is where the reincarnation question starts to bite: humans are more likely to achieve these things, but the tradition does not very often draw sharp category distinctions between humans and animals.

    Animals inside the moral economy of reincarnation¶

    • [00:06:50] Animals are caught up in reincarnation in a way that implies they too fall under dharma: if one can be punished by rebirth as an animal, or an animal rewarded by rebirth as a human — just as men can be reborn as women and vice versa — then animals are part of the same moral economy. (Adamson notes in passing that the idea men are reborn as women, etc., is the texts' view, not his own.)
    • [00:07:37] Carpenter agrees — but stresses what "involvement" means: reincarnation introduces a completely different sense of individuation. You don't get a person simply replaced by an animal. Rather there is a single being whose actions generate consequences that play out in various ways — possibly through the acquisition of an animal-type body with animal-type desires.
    • [00:08:11] A central question: can animals behave morally — act for the sake of a higher good rather than just being driven by desire? Across the board the Buddhists, Jains and Hindus are "not sentimental about animals." An animal life is conceived as disagreeable, a punishment, a life driven by desire and fear, unable to step back and revise one's desires according to what is best. So animal existence is worse off — and arguably less able to be morally responsible. This is acknowledged.

    The "serve your time as an elephant" problem¶

    • [00:09:03] The worry: if becoming an animal makes you unable to act morally, how do you ever get out? It looks like a story of pure degeneration with no exit.
    • [00:09:15] Two solutions appear in the texts. (1) Just accept it: you arrive as an animal with a stock of karma already built up; you acquire no new karma during the animal life (you can't act morally), but other karma will come into action once the punishment is paid. "Basically you just have to serve your time as an elephant." (2) Alternatively — and this too is in the texts — animals can behave morally, in the sense of acting for a purpose that then acquires good or bad consequences.

    Is the reincarnating self human, animal, or neither?¶

    • [00:09:48] Adamson presses the metaphysics: if the same being is human, then animal, then human again, that suggests the self being reborn is neither human nor animal. Compare the episode on women in Indian thought, where sometimes the self seems really male (even when reborn as a woman) and other times genderless. Is it a human trapped in an animal body, or a self that is neither?
    • [00:10:35] Carpenter: there is no unanimity — different texts and philosophers disagree. Śaṅkara is famous (or infamous) for holding that something special about the male Brahmin body is necessary before one achieves mokṣa; but plenty of others denied this, and there are even stories of animals going straight to liberation without an intervening human rebirth. The dominant view, she thinks, is of a being that is in itself neither human nor any other animal.
    • [00:11:20] And of course, when you come to the Buddhists, they challenge the very idea that we should be looking for something "in itself" at all — so they are well placed to say there is no real answer to "but what is it really?"

    Vegetarianism and ahiṃsā — the line doesn't run the way you'd think¶

    • [00:11:41] Adamson raises vegetarianism and the broader ethic of ahiṃsā (non-violence). The natural thought: I shouldn't eat animals because one might be a reincarnated human — maybe even my grandfather. Is that the moral logic the tradition actually uses?
    • [00:12:05] Carpenter: "Not as often as you would expect" — and in fact it works the other way around. "I mustn't eat it because it's my uncle" can only piggyback on a prior conviction that one doesn't eat one's uncle." And even then it doesn't go through: if the pig's life is so disagreeable, why not liberate your poor uncle and do him a favour?
    • [00:12:44] So there is no straight line from reincarnation to non-violent behaviour. What you actually need is a prior commitment to non-violence. Combine that with a metaphysics of reincarnation that doesn't individuate beings, and the principle of non-violence comes out unrestricted in scope: there is no presumption that it applies only to humans and then gets extended to animals. One is simply to be non-violent — towards whatever could be a recipient of violence or harm.
    • [00:13:27] Adamson reframes it: animals and humans have in common that they are potential victims of violence, and that is why they fall under the rule.

    How far does the boundary stretch — plants, even stones?¶

    • [00:13:33] Adamson notes that non-violence literature sometimes extends to plants, and even to stones. Is there a global ethic of avoiding violence to anything, or does it matter whether the victim is an animal versus a plant or rock?
    • [00:14:08] Carpenter: the answer is both. There are two distinct things ahiṃsā means, held together with different emphases:
    • (1) Don't harm that which is capable of being harmed. How far this extends depends on the school: for a Jain it extends very far; for a Buddhist fairly far, but not quite to plants.
    • (2) Violence is a mindset — an ill-will — and that is simply bad in itself. Even toward a stone you don't think can be harmed, adopting a violent, ill-willed attitude is an expression of confusion that is "not good for you or for anybody around you."

    For the animal's sake, or for mine?¶

    • [00:15:18] This connects to Adamson's next question: is benevolence toward animals for the animal's sake (it has a right not to be harmed) or for the moral agent's sake (I don't want a violent mindset; it might block my own liberation)? It sounds like Carpenter thinks both are at work.
    • [00:16:07] Carpenter: to an extent, yes — both, in different emphases in different places. The Buddhists are keen on exercises that recognize every living being suffers and wants not to suffer and wants to be happy; this is crucial to developing compassion, and enlightenment just is the perfection of compassion together with the perfection of wisdom.
    • [00:16:43] One caveat: Indian moral thought is not rights-based. The notion of an animal having a right I must respect is "not the kind of language you ever see." So it is not framed as just vs. unjust, but as appropriate/inappropriate, or as dharma — what is or isn't according to dharma. Making that case, one might appeal to a rule, or to what conduces to one's own liberation, or to the nature of the animal and its suffering.

    Different schools, different proportions of the same arguments¶

    • [00:17:35] Adamson asks whether this holds across Jains, Buddhists and Vedic thinkers, or is truer of one group. Carpenter: you find strands of all of these in all of them, but in different proportions.
    • [00:17:48] Within the Brahminical tradition — with the Vedas and the Arthaśāstra-type texts to refer to — you are more likely to get appeals to rules ("here's what you do and don't do"). With the Buddhists you find a preponderance of texts emphasizing the suffering of the creature.
    • [00:18:16] Part of the reason: the Buddhists have a polemic against Brahminical animal sacrifice. Even after the Brahminical tradition largely stopped sacrificing animals, it remained committed to the idea that it's okay to do — and the Buddhists and Jains came back hard at that.

    Two fun facts about the Buddhists¶

    • [00:18:49] Carpenter offers two: (1) Buddhists aren't always vegetarian, and (2) Buddhists hold that plants aren't alive (they're not unique in this).
    • [00:19:03] On plants: if you mustn't be violent toward anything capable of harm, and you think plants can be harmed, then you can't eat plants. The Jains were strict and clear about exactly this — you couldn't eat plants in any way that damages or kills them; for Jains to eat is to engage in violence, which is why being alive at all is to be compromised by the necessity of violence.
    • [00:19:38] The Buddhists, following the middle way, are more pragmatic: one can eat plants, and this is not a bad thing. But because they too hold the no-harm principle, they are forced to conclude that plants aren't capable of being harmed — they can't feel pleasure and pain, which in the Indian context is the very criterion for being alive. So, in effect, plants aren't alive.
    • [00:20:21] Nobody is very explicit about this; it grows up gradually as a worried sideline. And the Buddhists aren't alone — to the objection "plants grow, so they must be alive," others answered: crystals grow and reproduce too, yet you wouldn't call them alive. A genuine philosophical question lurking around the edges.
    • [00:20:53] On meat: the Buddhists agree there are degrees of harmfulness — harming a mosquito isn't as bad as harming a human. The original monastic criterion for what you could eat: you couldn't kill it yourself, couldn't have it killed for you, couldn't hear it being killed, couldn't know it was killed for you — but if someone's leftovers are placed in your bowl, you eat it. If those leftovers are meat, then whether that person ought to have been eating it "is their affair" — you eat what you're given.
    • [00:21:47] Only with the Mahāyāna movement, several centuries later, do arguments arise that if you are striving for enlightenment and really trying to be compassionate, you don't eat animals at all — even leftovers.
    • [00:22:03] This becomes a point of Jain–Buddhist polemic: the Jains taunt the Buddhists as "meat-eating supposed ascetics," insisting they are the hardcore ones.
    • [00:22:13] A favourite Buddhist argument against eating meat (once they do oppose it): when you eat meat you smell different — you stink — not because humans will be put off (our sense of smell is poor), but because prey animals (rabbits, mice) have an acute sense of smell. Walking around stinking like a carnivore strikes fear into every little furry creature you pass — and inducing that fear is a cruel, alarming thing to do.

    Ending with the fables: no distance between human and non-human¶

    • [00:22:55] Adamson proposes closing on the fables. Beyond Hanumān, the literature teems with "precocious" animals; like Aesop, India has talking-animal tales, but it is, if anything, an even more striking feature. What do the fables tell us about philosophical attitudes toward animals?
    • [00:23:33] Carpenter: the most striking thing is what they express — "the lack of distance between the human and the non-human." Animals appear in a wide variety of guises: they can be wicked or good, crafty, tricky, lazy — but also charitable, generous, thoughtful. They express the full range of moral qualities.
    • [00:24:11] Crucially, they are social. One might think sociability is distinctive of the human realm, but the fables depict animals as social — and not merely as a convenient proxy for humans (though they can function that way too). They show a receptivity and perceptiveness about genuine non-human animal interaction: animals take stances toward one another and engage with each other. The world is not a human one with a colourful animal backdrop, but "an extremely rich one in which all kinds of creatures are going about living their lives."
    • [00:25:00] This is reinforced by the fact that the fables are constantly shared, reworked and spread as part of everyday discourse.
    • [00:25:09] Adamson's closing thought: the fables both express and reinforce the idea of a continuum of living, conscious beings from humans to animals — because a child raised on stories of talking animals is "less likely to think what the Stoics thought about animals." Carpenter: "Exactly — and that can only be a good thing."
    • [00:25:38] Adamson thanks Carpenter and previews the next episode: whether Greek philosophy was influenced by Indian philosophy.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Amber Carpenter — the guest; associate professor at Yale-NUS College, who has worked extensively on Buddhism.
    • animal fables — ancient, constantly-reworked didactic tales of talking, social animals; a "background hum" of Indian thought about animals.
    • Pañcatantra / Hitopadeśa — major collections into which the fable tradition was gathered.
    • Dharmaśāstra / Arthaśāstra — works of conduct that prescribe right behaviour, including toward animals.
    • Mahābhārata / Rāmāyaṇa — the epics, full of animal subplots (e.g. Hanumān the talking monkey).
    • Hanumān — the talking monkey of the Rāmāyaṇa; "no ordinary monkey."
    • Gaṇeśa — the elephant-headed god; example of animals incorporated into the divine.
    • dharma — duty / what is right; for the Indians, what distinguishes humans is the capacity to act for dharma and a distant good, more than "rationality."
    • karma — accumulated moral consequence of action; explains how one can serve out an animal rebirth yet later exit it.
    • mokṣa — liberation; humans are far better placed to attain it, though some texts allow animals to reach it directly.
    • reincarnation / individuation — rebirth across human, animal and divine; introduces a self that is in itself neither human nor animal, and doesn't individuate beings.
    • Śaṅkara — held (infamously) that a male Brahmin body is needed before achieving mokṣa.
    • ahiṃsā — non-violence; rests on a prior, unrestricted commitment, not on "that animal might be my relative."
    • two senses of non-violence — (1) don't harm what can be harmed; (2) violence as an ill-willed mindset that is bad in itself, even toward a stone.
    • Jains — strictest on harm: to eat is to do violence, so all life is compromised; the "hardcore" ascetics who taunt the Buddhists.
    • Buddhists — middle-way pragmatists: not always vegetarian (original rule allowed leftover meat); hold plants aren't alive because they can't feel pleasure and pain (the Indian criterion for being alive).
    • Mahāyāna — later Buddhist movement that argued the truly compassionate eat no animals at all, even leftovers.
    • compassion / suffering — for Buddhists, recognizing that every being suffers and wants happiness is central; enlightenment is the perfection of compassion plus wisdom.
    • not rights-based — Indian ethics frames the issue as appropriate/inappropriate or according to dharma, never as the animal "having a right."

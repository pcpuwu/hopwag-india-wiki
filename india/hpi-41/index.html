    HoPWaG India · Ep 41 — "Monima Chadha on Indian Philosophy of Mind"¶

    ← Ep 40

    Ep 42 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson with Monima Chadha 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson with Monima Chadha · Runtime: 00:29:30 In brief: An interview on ancient Indian philosophy of mind, centred on the great self/no-self debate between the Vedic ("Hindu") schools and the Buddhists — why "mind" (manas) is not "self" (ātman), how the Buddhists argue that impermanent mental faculties can do all the work the Vedic self was meant to do (memory, the unity of consciousness, agency), and how these debates speak to contemporary, eliminativist philosophy of mind. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Monima Chadha ≈ "Monima Chalha / Moni Machada"; Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gennary"; Vasubandhu ≈ "Basu Bandhu / Vazupandu"; Cārvāka ≈ "Chharvaka / Chavaka / Chalaka"; Nyāya ≈ "Niyya / Niyahi"; Abhidharma ≈ "Abidharma"; ālayavijñāna ≈ "Aleh Vidyan"; ahaṃkāra ≈ "ahamqar"; citta ≈ "chita"; buddhi ≈ "budi"; Philosophy East and West ≈ "philosophy Eastern West").

    Is there a Sanskrit word for "mind"?¶

    • [00:00:28] The guest is Monima Chadha, senior lecturer in philosophy at Monash University; the episode is an interview on ancient Indian philosophy of mind.
    • [00:00:56] Adamson opens with terminology: is there a single Sanskrit word that means just what English means by "mind"? Chadha's answer is "yes and no, as you'd expect from a philosopher."
    • [00:01:04] There is manas, standardly translated "mind." But there are several other relevant terms naming distinct mental faculties — citta, ahaṃkāra (the "I-maker"/ego), buddhi (intellect). So when we say "mind" in Indian philosophy we are really talking about a cluster of mental faculties and conscious states. In Buddhist philosophy especially, mind is often spoken of as citta.
    • [00:01:50] So there's no clean one-to-one mapping: you can't pick one Sanskrit word and call it the equivalent of the English "mind."

    Mind belongs to the category of "not-self"¶

    • [00:01:57] The crucial point for the whole episode: in the Indian tradition "mind" falls under the category of the not-self. The fundamental divide is not mind versus body (as in the West) but self versus not-self.
    • [00:02:24] What separates the two categories, in Chadha's view, is permanence. Mind — together with body, the sense organs, and the rest of the not-self — is impermanent. The self (ātman) is by contrast permanent, even eternal for some Vedic schools. So whenever we think about mind in this tradition we must think of it as an impermanent entity.
    • [00:02:53] She recalls a riddle from the mythological tradition: "what is swifter than the wind?" — the answer being the mind. But the deeper warning is terminological: in Western philosophy we freely use "mind," "self," and "person" interchangeably; in Indian philosophy that is a mistake, because mind belongs to an altogether different category from self.

    Is the self/no-self debate really about mind?¶

    • [00:03:29] This sets up the central ancient Indian debate: the Buddhists deny a persistent self, the Vedic schools insist on one. Adamson presses a worry: since the Vedic tradition itself distinguishes mind from self, is this really a debate in the philosophy of mind at all?
    • [00:03:53] Chadha reframes it. The debate is officially about whether there is a self. But you can read it as a debate about mind, because the Buddhist move is to argue that the mind can do all the work of the self — so we don't need a self at all. Whatever the Hindu schools claim the self does, the Buddhist says the mind can do.
    • [00:04:36] Adamson adds the complication: the Buddhists deny that the mind persists over time either — indeed they deny that anything persists. Chadha agrees; this is precisely the challenge. If the mind is impermanent, how can it explain phenomena like memory? The Hindus postulate a self largely because otherwise memory has no good explanation. The Buddhist reply is: no, we can account for memory and the other mental functions the Hindus point to, using impermanent mental faculties.

    The argument from memory — and what the Nyāya-Sūtra actually says¶

    • [00:05:32] The most prominent Vedic argument for a persisting self, from the Nyāya school: "I must persist over time because I can remember things I experienced yesterday." A powerful argument, Adamson grants.
    • [00:05:56] Chadha corrects a common assumption: the original argument in the Nyāya-Sūtra does not mention memory. The original argument is about the need to explain desire — how can I desire something unless I've had the relevant experiences before? Therefore there must be a persisting self. Memory enters only in the commentaries.
    • [00:06:29] And in the commentaries it's specifically episodic memory — not just remembering facts about the past, but remembering episodes: e.g. "I was the one singing at my 40th birthday," a vivid first-person recollection. That kind of memory, the Nyāya argue, cannot be explained without a permanent self.
    • [00:07:13] Adamson probes the timescale: is it only about long-ago events, or also ongoing memory (e.g. remembering you've been talking for several minutes)? Chadha finds the question interesting — how much time must pass before something counts as a memory? Even comprehending a single sentence requires holding the earlier words, but they treat sentence comprehension as happening "all at once," so that wouldn't count. Something from a few minutes ago, though, probably should count.
    • [00:08:03] And no, she says, they don't draw a short-term / long-term memory distinction in framing this argument for the self.

    The unity of consciousness¶

    • [00:08:13] Adamson raises a second Vedic argument — not about persistence over time but about the unity of consciousness at a single moment, the first-person perspective. Right now he hears Chadha's voice and sees the room; there seems to need to be some mental principle (the mind? the self?) that gathers these impressions into a single "gestalt awareness."
    • [00:08:58] Chadha: the Nyāya and Hindu schools will indeed say only the self can unite experiences at a time. But the Buddhists answer that a mental faculty — the eighth consciousness or storehouse consciousness (ālayavijñāna) — can account for this synchronic unity of experiences, without postulating a self.
    • [00:09:51] Adamson tries to locate the mind/self difference here: perhaps the Vedic side says the mind does certain jobs, but I also have sense perception, so I need a self to unify what's in the mind with what's in sense perception into one consciousness (e.g. recalling yesterday while looking at a painting). The Buddhists, on this reading, say you can get by with just whatever mind and sense perception do.
    • [00:10:30] Chadha refines it: the Buddhist doesn't actually distinguish sense perception from mind in that way. The Buddhist treats the various consciousnesses as all being kinds of minds. There is no single thing called "the mind"; mind just is a series of states, changing and causally connected over time. Sense perception is one kind of conscious awareness, self-awareness another, bodily awareness another — multiple simultaneous series that share a time-frame and causal interactions, and together they can do the work the Hindus assign to a self.

    How the Buddhist critique differs from the Cārvāka¶

    • [00:11:38] Adamson links back to earlier episodes on the Cārvāka (materialist) position. The Cārvākas seem to accept at least a mind and maybe a self — though the Cārvāka-Sūtra avoids the word ātman, speaking of mental phenomena instead. The Buddhists are saying something very different.
    • [00:12:19] Chadha: the Cārvākas treat consciousness as an emergent property of the material elements. The Buddhists are not materialists — they distinguish mental atoms/elements from physical elements, speak of psychophysical complexes, and refuse to reduce mind to body.
    • [00:12:58] But the deeper difference is impermanence: for the Buddhist, mind is not a persisting thing but a series of conscious awarenesses (indeed many series). Their critique of persistence would apply to the emergent Cārvāka mind just as much as to Vedānta theories of self — because the Cārvākas do take the material elements to persist over time, combining to give rise to the new property of consciousness.
    • [00:13:36] So the debate between Cārvākas and Hindus is not about persistence; that's a different debate. The Buddhist/Hindu debate is specifically about whether we need persisting entities in our ontology to explain everything there is.

    From Occam's Razor to an argument against existence¶

    • [00:13:53] Adamson notes that as described, the Buddhist argument sounds like Occam's Razor — we can explain the phenomena without a persisting self or mind, so we shouldn't posit one. (He's covering Ockham in the parallel medieval episodes.) Is it merely that, or do they also argue the self is incoherent?
    • [00:14:45] Different schools, different arguments. The economy argument Chadha has been describing belongs to the Abhidharma school. Vasubandhu sharpens it: for the Abhidharma, the criterion of reality is causal efficacy — "to be is to be causally efficacious." His claim is that the self is causally inefficacious; there's nothing for the self to do; therefore there is no self.
    • [00:17:36] Adamson observes this is how you turn a merely economical Occam-style argument into an aggressive one that shows something doesn't exist: you add the premise that to exist just is to be causally efficacious, and so bootstrap from "we don't need it" to "it doesn't exist."

    The "it will make you unhappy" argument — and aggression on both sides¶

    • [00:15:42] A second, very different Buddhist argument: if you postulate a self, it will make you unhappy — because with a self comes self-interest. The self is not only doing no work, it is an illusion that causes misery, so we should be rid of it. (There is also a separate incoherence argument in the tradition.) All these are competing interpretations of what the Buddha meant by the denial of self.
    • [00:16:35] Adamson finds the "it'll make you unhappy" argument strange: philosophy of mind is supposed to be an abstract, theoretical branch, yet here is an argument from misery. Chadha pushes back firmly: the argument is not "don't believe in the mind," it's "don't believe in the self" — or more carefully, "don't believe in persisting things." Mind, as momentary mental awareness, is not a persisting thing; it exists only momentarily before giving rise to the next conscious state. So the unhappiness argument targets the persisting self, not mind.
    • [00:18:14] Aggression, she adds, runs on both sides. The Hindus are no gentler when debating Buddhists. She recalls an exchange in the journal Philosophy East and West: a Buddhist philosopher disagreed with her, and the Nyāya scholar Stephen Phillips (a professor in Texas) quipped, "Chadha might not be right, but against the Buddhists, everything is fair."

    Storehouse consciousness and the unity of consciousness, again¶

    • [00:18:51] Adamson concedes memory is easy for the Buddhists — it just falls under their general account of apparent persistence (causal connections between fleeting momentary phenomena). Harder, he thinks, is the unity of consciousness: without a self, what centre of awareness directs attention across all the cognitions happening at one moment?
    • [00:19:55] Chadha (specifying the Abhidharma school and the philosopher Vasubandhu, a tradition of northern India) explains via the storehouse consciousness — which the later Vasubandhu takes over from the Yogācāra school. The storehouse consciousness acts as a base: there is a two-way interaction between it and the sensory consciousnesses (eye-consciousness, olfactory, taste, etc.). It helps the other consciousnesses, and they in turn feed their objects back into it. All the sensory consciousnesses thus share a common base.
    • [00:21:19] Crucially, this base is not a persisting thing: it is itself a changing consciousness like every other. So whatever a transcendent single self overseeing everything was supposed to do, the Buddhist does instead by appealing to the interrelationships between the components of the momentary person.

    "Am I my mind?" — eliminating the "I"¶

    • [00:21:54] Since the Buddhists accept mind as momentary cognition, and (in Chadha's published work on Vasubandhu) this momentary mind does the work of a self or person — would Vasubandhu say "I am my mind"?
    • [00:22:25] No, she thinks the Buddhists would not be happy with that. They dislike the notion of "I" altogether: the "I" is a fiction, a conceptual imposition on what is really real, and this imposition causes great suffering — because with "I" comes "me" and "mine," ego and self-interest.
    • [00:22:54] So it's a reductionist / eliminativist account. Adamson contrasts the Cārvākas again: they say mental life emerges from the elements but is nonetheless real. The Buddhists, by contrast, want us to abandon all thought of the self / the "I" — because they think it brings nothing but pain.

    Contribution to contemporary philosophy of mind¶

    • [00:23:38] Adamson closes by asking how all this bears on contemporary philosophy of mind — noting he's never been to a contemporary talk that opens with "liberation from suffering," so the motivation is clearly different.
    • [00:24:20] Chadha's first answer: moral responsibility and agency. If you give up persons and the self, you also seem to give up the agent of action — and the Buddhists embrace that. She gives Vasubandhu's worked example: you see a mango → a desire for the mango arises → that causes an intention → that causes bodily actions → you procure the mango and satisfy the desire. Where is the agent in all this? There is just an interaction of mental and physical states explaining the action; no agent is needed. Some Buddhists say the very sense of agency is an illusion: responsibility can be handled by the causal relations without positing agents as the bearers of moral responsibility.
    • [00:26:22] Adamson notes the fit: much contemporary philosophy of mind tends eliminativist or toward supervenience theories, trying to get away from Cartesian pictures of self and mind and reduce them to the material. So even though the Buddhists started from very different motivations, their drive to eliminate apparent mental phenomena slots into the modern debate neatly.
    • [00:27:04] A further contribution: degrees / levels of conscious awareness. For the Buddhists it's not simply "conscious state vs. unconscious"; there are degrees of awareness. Her example: one is not directly conscious of one's body, but the moment it's mentioned it comes into awareness. Contemporary philosophy and the sciences of mind could attend to this more carefully rather than treating consciousness as binary.
    • [00:28:02] And it isn't only the Buddhists: the Hindu / Vedic tradition offers a fine-grained taxonomy of conscious states — waking, dream states, and deep dreamless sleep (what happens to consciousness there?). These are exactly the questions entering contemporary philosophy and the science of mind — evidence suggesting degrees of consciousness even in sleep. So this is not merely history: it has potential to contribute to live debates.
    • [00:28:54] Adamson thanks Chadha; this whets the appetite for the forthcoming Buddhist episodes. But first, a change of tack: the next episode turns to ancient theories of aesthetics — drama, poetry, and music — before the series moves on to the Buddhists and Jains, alongside Jonardon Ganeri.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • manas — the term standardly translated "mind"; but only one of several Indian words for mental faculties.
    • citta — "mind" in the sense of conscious states/awareness, especially in Buddhist philosophy.
    • ahaṃkāra — the "I-maker," the ego-faculty; a mental faculty distinct from manas.
    • buddhi — the intellect; another of the mental faculties grouped under "mind."
    • ātman — the self; for the Vedic schools permanent, even eternal — the thing the Buddhists deny.
    • self vs. not-self — the fundamental Indian divide (not mind vs. body); mind, body, and sense organs all belong to the impermanent not-self.
    • Nyāya — Vedic school; argues for a persisting self — originally from the need to explain desire (the Nyāya-Sūtra), and, in the commentaries, episodic memory.
    • Cārvāka — materialist school; treats consciousness as an emergent property of persisting material elements; avoids the word ātman in its sūtra.
    • Buddhists (Abhidharma) — deny any persisting self; hold that impermanent mental faculties, a series of momentary conscious awarenesses, can do all the self's work.
    • Vasubandhu — Abhidharma philosopher of northern India; argues the self is causally inefficacious (and so unreal), and develops the storehouse-consciousness account of the unity of experience.
    • causal efficacy — the Abhidharma criterion of reality: "to be is to be causally efficacious"; since the self does nothing, it does not exist.
    • ālayavijñāna (storehouse / eighth consciousness) — a non-persisting "base" consciousness, taken from Yogācāra, that interacts two-way with the sensory consciousnesses to explain their synchronic unity without a self.
    • Yogācāra — Buddhist school from which the later Vasubandhu adopts the storehouse-consciousness idea.
    • episodic memory — vivid first-person recollection of past episodes; the Nyāya commentators argue it requires a permanent self.
    • unity of consciousness — the gathering of simultaneous cognitions into one awareness; the Vedic argument says only a self can do it, the Buddhists answer with the storehouse consciousness.
    • the "I" as fiction — for the Buddhists the "I" is a conceptual imposition that breeds "me," "mine," ego, and suffering, and should be abandoned (an eliminativist account).
    • agency / moral responsibility — Vasubandhu's mango example shows action explained by interacting mental and physical states with no agent; the sense of agency is, for some Buddhists, itself an illusion.
    • degrees of consciousness — both Buddhist and Vedic traditions offer fine-grained taxonomies of awareness (waking, dream, deep dreamless sleep), a contribution to contemporary philosophy and science of mind.

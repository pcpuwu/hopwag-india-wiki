    HoPWaG India · Ep 48 — "Taking Perspective: The Jain Theory of Standpoints"¶

    ← Ep 47

    Ep 49 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:13 In brief: The Jains, the "great syncretists" of ancient India, respond to every philosophical question by accepting all the available viewpoints as partial truths — the doctrine of anekāntavāda ("non-one-sidedness"). The episode walks through Umāsvāti's seven nayas or standpoints, shows how each maps onto a rival school, and presents the Jains not as conflict-avoiding pacifists but as relativists who think one-sided theories are flatly false. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gannery"; anekāntavāda ≈ "Anacantavada"; naya ≈ "night"; Umāsvāti ≈ "Umazvati"; Kundakunda ≈ "Kunda Kunda"; Siddhasena Divākara ≈ "Siddhasaena/Siddhasena Divakara"; Hemacandra ≈ "Hemachandra"; Śvetāmbara ≈ "Shvetambara"; Āgamas ≈ "Agamas"; pramāṇa ≈ "Pramana"; Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika ≈ "Niaya Vaishika"; Cārvāka ≈ "Chavaka/Charvaca"; Prabhācandra ≈ "Prabachandra"; Advaita Vedānta ≈ "Advaita Vedanta"; Madhyamaka ≈ "Madyamaka/Madyamika"; Nāgārjuna ≈ "Nagarjuna/Nagajuna"; Candrakīrti ≈ "Chandra Kirti/Chandar Kirti"; Bhartṛhari ≈ "Batriari"; Śakra ≈ "chakra"; Frege ≈ "Frege"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 38 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019). Its sources & a reading list are under Further reading below.

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Hemacandra — 12th-c. Śvetāmbara Jain thinker; source of the merchant's-wife parable (relayed by modern scholar John Cort) [00:01:25].
    • Mahāvīra — the last of the Fordmakers; his answers to the Buddha's unanswered questions seed the theory of standpoints [00:02:54].
    • Umāsvāti — author of a pioneering sūtra, the first to set out Jain thought in Sanskrit (late 4th c.); systematizer of the seven nayas [00:06:11].
    • Kundakunda and Siddhasena Divākara — the other two early architects of the standpoint theory (Kundakunda probably earliest; Siddhasena around 700 AD) [00:06:24].

    The merchant's wife and the bull¶

    • [00:00:33] The episode opens with a parable. A merchant's wife, deserted for another woman, obtains a spell guaranteed to put her husband in a state where she could "lead him about with a string." The promise turns out literal, not figurative: he is transformed into a bull. Leading him to pasture, she overhears Śiva mention that an herb growing in the shade of a nearby tree would restore him. Not knowing which plant it is, she gathers every plant beneath the tree and forces the bull to eat them all — and he is restored to human form.
    • [00:01:25] The story comes via the modern Jain-philosophy scholar John Cort, who reports it from the 12th-century Śvetāmbara thinker Hemacandra. The moral: if we want salvation, we should not embrace only one teaching or tradition — we should embrace them all.

    The Jains as syncretists — anekāntavāda¶

    • [00:01:44] The Jains are "the great syncretists of ancient Indian thought." Where the Vedic schools each defend a single account of the soul and its liberation, and where Buddhists like Nāgārjuna reject all such theories as incoherent, the Jains accept all the available points of view.
    • [00:02:00] Hemacandra's tale might suggest the motive is merely hedging bets — since we cannot know which teaching is right, adopt all at once and trust the correct one is in the pile. But the Jain approach is more sophisticated: a genuine epistemological theory called anekāntavāda, "the teaching of non-one-sidedness," also called the theory of standpoints.
    • [00:02:38] The core conviction: any particular, one-sided answer merely expresses a limited point of view and cannot grasp the manifold nature of things as they really are. To grasp the world aright would be to grasp it from an unlimited number of perspectives — an achievement reserved for the omniscient Fordmakers, the saviors who showed the way to liberation, Mahāvīra being the last of them. The Jain philosopher's job is to approximate that omniscience: appreciate the contribution of each intellectual tradition while seeing that each offers only partial truths.

    Origins: Mahāvīra versus the Buddha's unanswered questions¶

    • [00:03:20] The theory traces back to Mahāvīra's reaction to early Buddhist doctrine. Early Buddhist tradition lists ten unanswered questions the Buddha refused to resolve: Is the world eternal, or not? Finite, or not? Is the soul identical with the body, or different? And four about the Buddha after death — does he exist, not exist, both, or neither?
    • [00:04:05] The Buddha declined to answer, comparing them to asking "where does a flame go after it is extinguished?" For the Buddhist, the right response is often to expose the faulty presuppositions behind the question.
    • [00:04:16] Mahāvīra responds completely differently. Asked whether the world is infinite, he neither stays silent nor picks one side — he embraces both: the world is finite in size but infinite in time (it never ends), and infinite in properties (limitless color, smell, taste, touch). Asked whether the soul is eternal, he says in a sense yes, in a sense no: eternal because it never ends, non-eternal because it takes different forms across the cycle of reincarnation — first animal, then human, then god.
    • [00:05:05] This is the central illustration of the Jain method. Against Buddhists who say there is no enduring self and Vedic thinkers who make the self eternal and unchanging, the Jains argue both sides are partly right and therefore partly wrong: the Buddhists err because the soul does persist; the Vedic thinkers err in making it unchanging. Just as Madhyamaka systematized themes already in the Buddhist scriptures, the standpoint theory generalizes these reports about Mahāvīra.

    The early Jain philosophers¶

    • [00:05:53] It has been proposed that Jainism passed through a phase of commentary on its scriptural texts, the Āgamas, with a more logical approach emerging only afterward. In fact things are messier: the two genres overlapped chronologically, and exegesis animated Jain philosophy much as it did the Brahmanical schools.
    • [00:06:07] Chronology is bedevilled by uncertainty over key dates — notably Umāsvāti, whose sūtra "on what is" was the pioneering work, the first to set out Jain thought in Sanskrit; he probably wrote in the late 4th century. Other important early figures: Kundakunda (probably earlier than Umāsvāti) and Siddhasena Divākara (around 700 AD). These three, above all, developed the theory of standpoints as an expression of Mahāvīra's method.

    From the book — ch. 38

    The book names Umāsvāti's pioneering work: the Tattvārthādhigamasūtra ("Sūtra on What Is"), translated by Nathmal Tatia as That Which Is. It is the text the chapter cites throughout by section number.

    Direct versus indirect knowing¶

    • [00:06:40] Mahāvīra himself had not so much a special method as a special insight. The Jains draw a fundamental distinction between indirect and direct ways of knowing, using the standard term for a means of knowledge, pramāṇa. Most of us must make do with the indirect access of perception and scriptural tradition; Mahāvīra enjoyed a direct omniscience of all things, which is why he could see from all points of view at once.
    • [00:07:15] Worse than having a limited perspective is having one without realizing it is limited — the predicament of the Jains' rivals, Vedic and Buddhist alike, who "fetishize a single standpoint as the only valid view."
    • [00:07:32] Though there are infinitely many ways to look at anything, Umāsvāti breaks the possibilities into seven types of standpoint, or naya: (1) the non-distinguished, (2) the general, (3) the practical, (4) the straight thread, (5) the verbal, (6) the subtle, (7) the thus-happened. The first three affirm substance; the remaining four concern the modifications or predicates of substance — already echoing Mahāvīra's two perspectives on the soul (that it endures, and that it changes).

    From the book — ch. 38

    The book records the Sanskrit name of each standpoint: naigama (non-distinguished), saṃgraha (general), vyavahāra (practical), ṛjusūtra (straight-thread), śabda (verbal), samabhirūḍha (subtle), and evambhūta (thus-happened). It also labels the substance-affirming trio dravyāstika and the modification-concerned four paryāyāstika.

    Substance versus modification¶

    • [00:08:18] That contrast between the substance point of view and the modification point of view is fundamental to Jainism. Kundakunda holds that substance — sentient and non-sentient alike — is "that which undergoes and endures through all change," and so can be seen from two angles depending on whether we stress its endurance or its change.
    • [00:08:41] Set Advaita Vedānta (which makes a single unchanging brahman the only true reality) against the Buddhist rejection of any enduring independent substance, and the Jain move comes into focus: it convicts both schools of being "nothing but points of view," one-sided because each manages only a partial perspective. But Umāsvāti has something more complex in mind than just two perspectives — hence the full seven.

    Working through the standpoints — the three substance views¶

    • [00:09:23] (1) The non-distinguished. The least clear of the seven — Siddhasena omitted it from his overview. It seems almost to be the standpoint of refusing to take any standpoint: thinking of a thing without bothering to distinguish whether it is a substance or a property — the attitude of someone merely cataloguing what there is without worrying about kinds. Puzzlingly, the Jains ascribe it to the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika philosophers, presumably because those schools treated properties as if they were objects in their own right, thus failing to distinguish substance from property. The Jains do not call this illegitimate — all viewpoints are legitimate, "but only so long as they are not taken to have a monopoly on validity."
    • [00:10:28] (2) The general. (And no, Adamson notes, dating makes it unlikely this is a tribute to Buster Keaton's greatest film.) It attends only to the most general commonalities — e.g., that everything shares the property of "being an existent" — ignoring distinguishing features. Adopted dogmatically, it leads to monism, with Advaita Vedānta "unsurprisingly fingered as the leading culprit."
    • [00:11:05] (3) The practical. The complement of the general: it stresses the special distinguishing features of things and how they sort under various headings. From this view a philosopher says every existent is either a substance or a property. Some Jains place the Cārvāka materialists here, probably because they classify both the material body and the spiritual self as substances (rather than properties) and so treat them as the same kind of entity.

    Naming and shaming — and the non-violence question¶

    • [00:11:42] (4) The straight thread. This attends only to what is presently perceived — the pleasure or pain felt right now. Taken as the uniquely correct standpoint, it reduces enduring substances and persons to the ephemeral deliverances of the senses — the mistake of the Buddhists, or at least some of them.
    • [00:12:05] In explaining these first four standpoints, the Jain writers effectively supply a survey of Indian metaphysics — which is why the Jains are so useful as a source on non-Jain thinkers (it was the Jain Prabhācandra who informed us about the otherwise obscure Cārvāka tradition). But these schools are not merely named — they are shamed, exposed as one-sided.
    • [00:12:35] A tempting reading: the theory of standpoints is an abstract embodiment of the Jain commitment to non-violence — disagreement being a kind of intellectual aggression, so the Jains find a way of agreeing with everyone. But this overlooks that the Jains do not simply congratulate rivals on being partly right; they stress that rivals are partly wrong. Like everyone else, the Jains hold that knowledge leads to liberation — but their knowledge requires seeing things from many viewpoints, whereas the Brahminical or Buddhist thinker adopts just one. The Jains cheerfully assert such one-sided theories are false precisely insofar as they are one-sided, and they saw no breach of non-violence in vigorous debate — hence the story of a Jain who held forth against a Buddhist at such length that "his opponent finally died from exhaustion."

    The three linguistic standpoints¶

    • [00:13:46] The remaining three show the Jains joining "that most typical of ancient Indian philosophical pursuits," the analysis of language.
    • [00:14:03] (5) The verbal. It takes grammar as a guide to reality — e.g., inferring from past, present, and future verb forms that their subjects differ. On this view, "the mountain existed" and "the mountain will exist" refer to two different mountains. Odd, but akin to a Madhyamaka point: Nāgārjuna (per his commentator Candrakīrti) held we should distinguish "the axe that is seen" from "the axe used for chopping," the distinction marked by grammatical case — object of action versus instrument. Useful, the Jains agree, so long as not applied indiscriminately: it is plausible that the grammatical contrast of subject and predicate often tracks a real contrast of thing and property, but one must not infer metaphysics from every grammatical distinction — else a German would conclude from noun gender that doors are female, houses neuter, and tables masculine.
    • [00:15:21] (6) The subtle. Here we distinguish different words that refer to the same thing but have different etymologies. The lord of the gods is called both Indra (from a verb "to reign") and Śakra (from a verb "to be able"); the names pick out the same god but differ in meaning because of their derivation. Striking, because — etymology aside — it anticipates Frege's sense/reference distinction: "morning star" and "evening star" both refer to Venus yet differ in meaning, as shown by the fact that one might not know they are the same body.
    • [00:16:19] (7) The thus-happened. Many words have functional senses, referring to objects by a capacity they can perform — "cook," "swimmer." On this standpoint such terms should be used only while the object is actually exercising the function: someone is a "swimmer" only while actually swimming. It is a linguistic version of the straight-thread view, reducing everything to the currently present. Taken dogmatically it yields the fallacy of refusing to call a sleeping swimmer a swimmer — echoing Nāgārjuna and Candrakīrti's argument that "the running giraffe is distinct from the seeing giraffe" because of their different actions.

    The standpoints in practice¶

    • [00:17:18] Siddhasena uses the standpoints to handle the problem of substance. Setting aside the non-distinguished, he starts with the general perspective that sees all substance as one — Advaita monism. The practical standpoint distinguishes substance into types — the attitude of the pluralists among Vedic schools. The straight thread, ignoring all but present reality, yields a theory of constant flux — the Buddhists. Grammarians cannot see past the verbal perspective — think of Bhartṛhari. Other language enthusiasts use the subtle standpoint to classify substances by the etymology of their names. And the thus-happened reduces a substance to its currently performed action.
    • [00:18:17] The summary: broadly, only the general and practical standpoints do justice to the stability and independence of substances, while the rest capture the modifications substances undergo. To adopt one standpoint is to get things partially right; to adopt it narrowly and exclusively is to get them wrong.

    The Jains as relativists — and what's next¶

    • [00:18:39] The Jains hold an idiosyncratic view of truth. They are not skeptics who deny truth is accessible or who suspend judgment about ever having it. Rather, they are relativists in a special sense: truth is always relative to a point of view or context in which it is uttered or believed. Yet — unlike most modern relativists — they think ultimate truth is possible: the truth attained by embracing an infinity of valid viewpoints all at once.
    • [00:19:13] The Jains realized this theory could not work within the logical system of the Nyāya school, and Nāgārjuna's destructive tetralemma would hardly suit their non-one-sided aims. So they developed a remarkable logic with seven kinds of predication — the topic of the next episode, which will also ask whether, by endorsing the yes-and-no side of every issue, the Jains fall into self-contradiction (the very problem that threatened Madhyamaka).
    • [00:19:46] A closing Jain joke about the podcast's month-long summer break: from a listener's perspective a month may seem long; from the producers' perspective it will be over before they know it. "Taking that point of view, we'll see you next time."

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • anekāntavāda — "the teaching of non-one-sidedness"; the Jain theory of standpoints, which holds that every one-sided view captures only a partial truth.
    • naya — a standpoint or perspective; Umāsvāti enumerates seven, the first three affirming substance, the last four its modifications.
    • the seven nayas — non-distinguished, general, practical, straight thread, verbal, subtle, thus-happened — each mapped onto a rival school's one-sided dogma.
    • Mahāvīra — the last Fordmaker (omniscient savior); his "in a sense yes, in a sense no" answers to the Buddha's unanswered questions seeded the standpoint theory.
    • Fordmakers — Jainism's omniscient saviors who showed the way to liberation; only they grasp all perspectives at once.
    • pramāṇa — a means of knowledge; the Jains distinguish indirect knowing (perception, scripture) from Mahāvīra's direct omniscience.
    • substance vs. modification — the fundamental Jain contrast: substance endures through change (Kundakunda), while modifications are its changing predicates.
    • Hemacandra — 12th-c. Śvetāmbara thinker; source of the merchant's-wife parable urging us to embrace all teachings.
    • Umāsvāti — late-4th-c.(?) author of the first Jain sūtra in Sanskrit; systematized the seven standpoints.
    • Kundakunda — early Jain philosopher (probably pre-Umāsvāti); defined substance as that which endures through all change.
    • Siddhasena Divākara — Jain philosopher (~700 AD) who applied the standpoints to the problem of substance and omitted the non-distinguished naya.
    • Āgamas — the Jain scriptural texts, on which a phase of commentary overlapped with the rise of philosophical literature.
    • anekāntavāda vs. non-violence — a tempting but rejected reading: the Jains do not merely agree with everyone; they call one-sided theories false and debated vigorously.
    • relativism (Jain) — truth is relative to a standpoint, yet ultimate truth is attainable by embracing infinitely many valid viewpoints at once.
    • rival schools placed by standpoint — Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika (non-distinguished), Advaita Vedānta (general/monism), Cārvāka (practical), Buddhists (straight thread/flux), Bhartṛhari & grammarians (verbal).

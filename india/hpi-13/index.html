    HoPWaG India · Ep 13 — "Grand Illusion: Dharma and Deception in the Mahābhārata"¶

    ← Ep 12

    Ep 14 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:25 In brief: The Mahābhārata as a great work of ethics done through storytelling: an epic of trickery and "grand illusion" that uses moral dilemmas about lying — above all the truth-teller Yudhiṣṭhira's fatal half-lie that kills Droṇa — to show that dharma operates on different levels, that genuine moral dilemmas can be tragic, and that the best thing to do may sometimes be something wrong. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Ganari"; Kṛṣṇa ≈ "Krishna"; Yudhiṣṭhira ≈ "Yutistira/Yudistira/Yudishtira/Udistora"; Duryodhana ≈ "Doriodana"; Droṇa ≈ "Drona"; Bhīma ≈ "Bima"; Pāṇḍavas ≈ "Pandavas"; Vyāsa ≈ "Vyasa"; kāma ≈ "karma"/"pleasure"; mokṣa ≈ "moxha"; svadharma ≈ "swadharma"; kṣatriya ≈ "Kashatria"; Nicomachean ≈ "Nikamakian"; Arthaśāstra ≈ "Atta Shastra"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 12 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019).     Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • The Mahābhārata — the vast Indian epic, "great tale of the Bharata"; eight times the size of both Homeric epics combined, and a monument of Indian literature with religious meaning [00:00:33].
    • Kṛṣṇa — God incarnate as Arjuna's charioteer; teller of the truth-telling parable and instigator of the war's key deceptions [00:01:38].
    • Vyāsa — the single bard to whom the epic is ascribed (cf. Homer), who is also a character inside his own narrative [00:03:07].
    • Yudhiṣṭhira — eldest of the Pāṇḍavas, famed for never lying; his floating chariot and his fall are the episode's moral test case [00:06:35].
    • dharma — duty / law / morality / religion; the moral code whose ambiguity drives the whole discussion [00:07:35].
    • Bimal Matilal — modern interpreter who reads Kṛṣṇa as a "moral innovator" [00:17:05].

    A dilemma older than Kant: the truth-teller and the murderers¶

    • [00:00:33] Today's episode: "Grand Illusion — Dharma and Deception in the Mahābhārata." It opens with a thought-experiment. A group of strangers rushes up begging for help: they're being pursued by murderous thieves who want to rob and kill them — don't tell them which way we ran. A minute later the cruel-looking pursuers arrive and ask which way the first group went.
    • [00:01:00] You're now in a dilemma. Stay silent and the robbers may torture the information out of you; lie and send them the wrong way; or tell the truth and send murderers after their victims.
    • [00:01:16] Philosophy students will recognize the puzzle: Immanuel Kant raised it as a challenge to his own teaching that one should never lie. But the example is much, much older than Kant — it features in the eighth of the 18 books of the Mahābhārata, told by none other than God himself, incarnated as the charioteer Kṛṣṇa.
    • [00:01:50] In Kṛṣṇa's version the dilemma falls to a priest who has vowed always to tell the truth. He duly tells the murderers which way to find their victims. Good though his intentions are, the priest is later sent to hell for the decision.

    A great work of ethics — done through stories¶

    • [00:02:01] The Mahābhārata has plenty to teach about ethics, and it is certainly great — so it can be considered a great work of ethics. But it does not pursue the subject abstractly, the way Kant's Critique of Practical Reason does. This is ethics performed through the telling of stories.
    • [00:02:24] The product of generations of orally transmitted narratives, it is a monument in the landscape of Indian literature — a text that "holds within it a whole culture" and has, since antiquity, carried religious meaning for its readers. The epic even refers to itself as a fifth Veda.
    • [00:02:44] It is one of a pair of ancient epics, the other being the Rāmāyaṇa — which makes the comparison to the Greek Iliad and Odyssey almost irresistible, and to some extent genuinely illuminating. All four epics feature superhuman heroes who endure warfare while being manipulated by the gods; and like Homer's poems, the Mahābhārata is ascribed to a single bard, named Vyāsa.
    • [00:03:14] But "great" is meant in the most literal sense too: the epic is enormously long — eight times the size of both Homeric epics combined. And it is of more direct importance to the historian of philosophy, because embedded within it is a section called the Song of the Lord, the Bhagavad-Gītā, which (like the Iliad) centres on a great warrior's reluctance to fight but is a classic philosophical text. The Gītā is for next time.
    • [00:03:44] This episode looks at a few scenes from the rest of the epic that bear on one question: can deception ever be morally justified? It is just one of countless themes the text contains — the Mahābhārata boasts of its own comprehensiveness: "what is not here does not exist anywhere." But it is an especially apt theme, because the epic also flaunts its own status as a made narrative, a myth whose making is constantly thrust before the reader.

    Grand illusion: a self-aware myth of tricksters¶

    • [00:04:21] The epic is presented through multiple frames. The whole is purportedly Vyāsa's work, but we hear it at several removes: he recited it to his son and disciples; one of them recited it again from memory; another bard repeated that, and his recitation is supposedly the basis of the 18-book version we have.
    • [00:04:48] This version is not just full but "stuffed to bursting," often with seemingly extraneous sub-tales — stories and digressions. They likely show that the epic narrative was treated as a framework, with generations of storytellers adding material relevant to their own times. The result is a text "invented and reinvented" that keeps making us aware of its own inventedness.
    • [00:05:15] Vyāsa, Kṛṣṇa and others both act within the narrative and tell stories inside it. This device — the myth-maker entering the plot of his own myth — reveals what the Mahābhārata is about: the elusiveness of truth and the ambiguity of moral rightness. Within the narrative too, the heroes deal constantly in deception, the plot driven forward by lies, tricks and subterfuge. This is "epic as grand illusion, featuring characters who are themselves illusionists."

    The central story: Pāṇḍavas vs. Kauravas¶

    • [00:05:53] A brief summary makes the point. Two families within the same lineage, the Bharata line — which is also the name of the geographical territory of India (hence Mahā-bhārata, "Great Tale of the Bharata," with "great" again pointing to its scope).
    • [00:06:17] A first story tells how the text was literally weighed in scales against the four Vedas and found heavier. Pitted against each other are two sets of brothers.

    From the book — ch. 12

    The book frames the weighing-in-the-scales story with a textual detail the talk skips: the epic says of itself that it circulates in both longer and shorter recensions (Mahābhārata §1.1), and the version that has come down to us is "very much the long form" — which the weighing against the four Vedas is meant to underscore. The Pāṇḍavas are the heroes, led — with some reluctance — by their eldest brother Yudhiṣṭhira, famed always for telling the truth; their number includes the great archer Arjuna, whose reluctance to fight his own kin will prompt Kṛṣṇa to preach the Gītā to him.

    • [00:06:59] On the other side, the Kauravas are a hundred brothers led by the anti-hero Duryodhana, who cheats the Pāṇḍavas of their birthright by duping Yudhiṣṭhira in two games of dice. The rest of the story tells of the Pāṇḍavas' wanderings, their return, and their victorious 18-day war against the Kauravas, at the cost of massive bloodshed on both sides. As in Homer, set-pieces detail the deaths of central heroes — and the most prominent deaths again involve deception and lies.

    What is dharma? From cosmic support to universal code¶

    • [00:07:35] To grasp what moral code is being violated in such treachery, we need to think about dharma. We've met the word (and its Pali equivalent dhamma) before: it was used in early Buddhism for the Buddha's teaching and appears repeatedly in the moralizing inscriptions of the Buddhist king Aśoka.
    • [00:07:59] Translators render it across a "disconcertingly wide range" — from law to morality to religion. In modern India it can simply mean religion (philosophy of religion becomes dharma-darśana). But in the ancient context, dharma captures only the part of religion that bears on moral conduct. Living in accordance with dharma was sometimes said to be necessary but not sufficient for liberation from the cycle of reincarnation. In general, one's dharma is all one's various duties — as a particular individual, as a member of a family and society, and simply as a human being.
    • [00:08:50] But in the earliest surviving literature, dharma did not mean a general moral code. It hewed closer to its etymological root, a verb meaning "preserve" or "support." The first Vedas use dharma for supporting the cosmos — as when Indra holds the rivers apart from the plants. The same usage appears in ancient Iran: Zoroastrian texts use a linguistically cognate word for God holding up the heavens so they don't fall.
    • [00:09:27] Dharma then plays little role in later Vedic literature; where it appears, it is in social and legal contexts — the king's role of upholding order and justice, contrasted with the "law of the fish," where, absent royal protection, the bigger devour the smaller.
    • [00:09:55] This suggests a down-to-earth test for which actions accord with dharma: just look at existing social practice. Praiseworthy behaviour is dharma; behaviour that earns rebuke is its opposite, adharma. There's a parallel with linguistic correctness as the Sanskrit grammarians saw it: Patañjali remarked that if you want words, don't go to a grammarian but to a competent language-user — likewise, if you want ethics, don't ask a philosopher but find people agreed to be ethical. This socially-embedded picture recalls the idea of virtue in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics.
    • [00:10:40] But dharma ultimately took on the more ambitious sense of an abstract, general set of moral requirements — and this seems to have happened especially, perhaps specifically, with the advent of Buddhism. The Buddha's dhamma was aimed at everyone regardless of social role or caste, the same universalism voiced in Aśoka's inscriptions.
    • [00:11:08] This sense then pervades later Indian literature, not least the Dharma-Sūtras (contrasted with Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra back in episode 11). At this stage dharma is named one of the main goals of life, alongside artha (practical advantage) and kāma (pleasure); movements like the Buddhists and Jains add a fourth, greater goal — mokṣa, liberation.
    • [00:11:29] The catch: this grander, absolute conception of dharma makes it much harder to know how to obey it. As one Dharma-Sūtra puts it, "Dharma and adharma do not go about saying, 'Here we are.'"

    Yudhiṣṭhira's fall: the floating chariot and the death of Droṇa¶

    • [00:11:49] And this returns us to the Mahābhārata. The parable of the truth-telling priest shows it is no easy matter to say where dharma leads — even those who seek to live well may get it wrong, especially in moral dilemmas where no choice seems right.
    • [00:12:13] It's no accident that Kṛṣṇa's tale is about truth-telling, a recurrent theme of the epic. The Pāṇḍavas' leader Yudhiṣṭhira is so committed to honesty that his chariot floats a little above the ground as he rides. He comes crashing back to earth — literally — when he finally tells a falsehood.
    • [00:12:31] He does so out of desperation: there is no other way to slay the Kaurava hero Droṇa. Astonishingly, it is the divine Kṛṣṇa himself who proposes the base stratagem: Droṇa cannot be beaten by force, so "casting aside virtue," the Pāṇḍavas should resort to a winning method — Droṇa will stop fighting if he believes his son has been killed; "let some man say that he has been slain in battle."
    • [00:13:09] Reactions are mixed: Arjuna disagrees at once; the warlike Bhīma thinks it splendid. Yudhiṣṭhira is torn — he absolutely wants to win (it's said he is addicted to victory) but is loath to spoil his spotless moral record.
    • [00:13:33] The subterfuge they hit on: kill an elephant that happens to share the name of Droṇa's son, then report "the death" while thinking of the elephant but letting Droṇa assume the son is meant. As Arjuna puts it, this "covers a lie with a truth, as if with armour."
    • [00:13:51] Bhīma, untroubled, delivers the misleading news, but the suspicious Droṇa demands confirmation from Yudhiṣṭhira — who confirms the fateful lie, muttering under his breath that it is only an elephant who has died, too quietly for Droṇa to hear. The ruse works: Droṇa drops his weapons, ready to die in grief, and is killed.
    • [00:14:16] But success is short-lived. Droṇa's son will wreak great destruction on the Pāṇḍavas, and Yudhiṣṭhira has lost his spotless reputation — later forced to witness his brothers' suffering in hell, a vision then revealed to be an illusion, payback for his part in deceiving Droṇa.

    The epic's ambivalence: do the ends justify the means?¶

    • [00:14:34] Despite the bitter fruit Yudhiṣṭhira reaps, the Mahābhārata's attitude to such deceptions is ambivalent. Elsewhere Kṛṣṇa warns that the Kaurava leader Duryodhana can only be killed by trickery — and here Kṛṣṇa is given a speech justifying (or perhaps just rationalizing) it: when enemies grow too numerous and powerful, "they should be slain by deceit and stratagems. This is the path formerly trodden by the gods to slay the demons, and a path trodden by the virtuous may be trodden by all."
    • [00:15:12] This reverses a slogan found elsewhere — "where dharma is, there is Kṛṣṇa" — into, roughly, if it's good enough for the gods, it's good enough for us. The idea seems to be that the end justifies the means: if the end is right, the necessary means are themselves right.
    • [00:15:35] Kṛṣṇa insists that only by deception can the Pāṇḍavas win, and remarks, "where dharma is, there is victory." On this reading the Pāṇḍavas' tricks are excused because they bring about the correct result — defeating enemies who are themselves tricksters. (Recall the whole war traces back to Duryodhana's double-dealing in the dice game that stripped the Pāṇḍavas of their kingdom.)

    Why the disapproving passages? Multiple imperatives and the moral innovator¶

    • [00:16:05] But if the end justifies the means, why do other passages disapprove of the Pāṇḍavas — and especially Yudhiṣṭhira — for their ignoble tactics? A banal answer: the Mahābhārata was composed over generations by many hands, so the occasional self-contradiction is no surprise (as was said of the Arthaśāstra).
    • [00:16:36] But perhaps the epic doesn't want things to be so simple — that the way of dharma is either tell the truth and lose or win by lying, and we just need to pick the right one. It has been aptly said that "the Mahābhārata never wants to resolve itself." There are multiple ethical imperatives at work: it's good to tell the truth, but it's also good to defeat wicked enemies and secure victory for your clan — especially when a god urges you to.
    • [00:17:05] As Bimal Matilal argued, Kṛṣṇa's role in these passages could be that of a moral innovator, teaching the Pāṇḍavas to shake off the rigid code Yudhiṣṭhira had cherished — that one must tell the truth in all circumstances, the very code followed by the priest who led murderers to their victims. On this reading Kṛṣṇa is a moral agent who gave up moral integrity to avoid a total miscarriage of justice.
    • [00:17:37] Yet even if the duty to tell the truth is trumped by other concerns, it may remain a duty nonetheless. That is why it makes sense for the epic to show the consequences of Yudhiṣṭhira's act — the grounded chariot, the vision of hell. For him, and for all of us at times, the best thing to do may involve doing something wrong, something against dharma.

    Svadharma: the dharma of one person is not another's¶

    • [00:18:00] To this the epic adds that the dharma of one group or person may differ from another's. Truth-telling is Yudhiṣṭhira's calling card; it is, in the deepest sense, out of character for him to collude in a lie. He thereby violates what may be called his svadharma — the duty or code relevant to him specifically.
    • [00:18:25] For a man like Bhīma, whose character revolves wholly around the kṣatriya (warrior) code of victory, the dilemma would hardly arise at all. No wonder Yudhiṣṭhira tried to have it both ways, muttering under his breath: his dharma as a truth-teller clashed with his dharma as a kṣatriya leader.
    • [00:18:48] In other passages Yudhiṣṭhira tries to escape by questioning the very validity of the warrior's dharma — the kṣatriya code, he suggests, is no better than the "law of the fish" where the strong prevail; better to give it up, even if that means retreating into the life of an ascetic: "the peaceful man sleeps happily, as he has given up both victory and defeat."

    The central insight: tragic dilemmas, but not "anything goes"¶

    • [00:19:14] Here is the central insight emerging from the epic's moral dilemmas: it recognizes that such dilemmas may be genuinely tragic — that not all duties can be satisfied at once. This does not mean there's no right response at all: in Kṛṣṇa's story it seems clear the priest should not have told the murderers the truth.
    • [00:19:36] What it means is that dharma may operate on different levels, if only because we as moral agents are complex beings with different sorts of duty and responsibility. Yudhiṣṭhira is a truth-teller by character and inclination, a warrior by circumstance — a combination that leads to a conflict resolvable only by betraying himself or his responsibilities.
    • [00:19:55] The same kind of dilemma is the catalyst for the epic's most famous section: it begins when Yudhiṣṭhira's younger brother Arjuna hesitates over whether to act as a warrior and ride out against his enemies, or as a kinsman who should not slay members of his own family.
    • [00:20:14] "Fortunately, you face no such conflict" — joining us next time, as we turn to the Bhagavad-Gītā, should be an easy decision.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Mahābhārata — the vast Indian epic, "Great Tale of the Bharata"; eight times the length of both Homeric epics, calls itself a fifth Veda, and does ethics through storytelling rather than abstract argument.
    • Bhagavad-Gītā — the Song of the Lord, a classic philosophical text embedded in the epic; subject of the next episode (Kṛṣṇa persuading Arjuna to fight).
    • Rāmāyaṇa — the other of the two ancient Indian epics, paired with the Mahābhārata as Greece's Iliad and Odyssey are paired.
    • Vyāsa — the single bard to whom the epic is ascribed; also appears as a character inside the multiply-framed narrative.
    • Kṛṣṇa — God incarnate as Arjuna's charioteer; tells the truth-teller parable, yet proposes the deceptions that kill Droṇa and Duryodhana; "where dharma is, there is Kṛṣṇa / victory."
    • Yudhiṣṭhira — eldest Pāṇḍava, never lies; his chariot floats above the ground until his half-truth about Droṇa's "death" brings it crashing down.
    • Arjuna — great archer among the Pāṇḍavas; objects to the deception of Droṇa; his refusal to fight kin sets up the Gītā.
    • Bhīma — warlike Pāṇḍava for whom only the kṣatriya code of victory matters; happily delivers the lie that fells Droṇa.
    • Duryodhana — anti-hero leading the hundred Kaurava brothers; cheats the Pāṇḍavas of their kingdom in the rigged dice game.
    • Droṇa — Kaurava hero, undefeatable by force, killed once tricked into believing his son is dead (an identically-named elephant is slain instead).
    • dharma — duty / law / morality / religion; from a root meaning "support/preserve" (cosmic order) it grows into a universal moral code, especially via Buddhism. Hard to apply: "Dharma and adharma do not go about saying, 'Here we are.'"
    • adharma — the opposite of dharma; behaviour that earns rebuke.
    • svadharma — the dharma specific to one's own role/character; Yudhiṣṭhira violates his (truth-telling) when forced into a warrior's lie.
    • artha / kāma / mokṣa — the goals of life named alongside dharma: practical advantage, pleasure, and (added by Buddhists and Jains) liberation.
    • "law of the fish" — the state of nature without royal protection, where the bigger devour the smaller; the foil to dharma as order/justice.
    • Bimal Matilal — modern interpreter who reads Kṛṣṇa as a moral innovator, sacrificing integrity to prevent a miscarriage of justice.
    • Kant — raised the truth-teller-and-murderer dilemma to challenge his own absolute ban on lying; the Mahābhārata posed it millennia earlier.

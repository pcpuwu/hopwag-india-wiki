    HoPWaG India · Ep 36 — "Fine Grained Analysis: Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra"¶

    ← Ep 35

    Ep 37 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:19:05 In brief: Against the cliché that all Indian thought derives everything from one source, Vaiśeṣika is a thoroughgoing realist pluralism: Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra carves the world into six categories (substance, quality, motion, universals, individuators, inherence), builds bodies up from eternal atoms, and — allied with Nyāya — promises that knowing this structure of reality leads to the highest good, against the anti-realist "therapy" of the Buddhists. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Canary"; Vaiśeṣika ≈ "Vaichesika / Vysheeshika / Vichita"; Kaṇāda ≈ "Kananda"; al-Kindī ≈ "Akindi / Okindi"; Praśastapāda ≈ "Prasashtapada"; Wilhelm Halbfass ≈ "Wilhelm Halbfas"; Ulūka ≈ "Ulika / Uluca"; padārtha ≈ "padarta"; Ākāśa ≈ "Akasha"; aṇu ≈ "anu"; bhūta ≈ "Bhuta"; samavāya ≈ "Samavaya"; Śaṅkara ≈ "Shankara"; Nyāya ≈ "Niyaya").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Vaiśeṣika — the realist, pluralist "partner school" of Nyāya; heroes of metaphysical pluralism [00:01:44].
    • Kaṇāda (Ulūka, "the owl") — author of the Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra, the founding document of the school [00:02:43].
    • Praśastapāda — 6th-c. CE author whose freestanding treatise (not a commentary) became the school's real touchstone [00:03:31].
    • al-Kindī — 9th-c. Muslim philosopher, used as the framing foil with his theory about why Indians play one-stringed instruments [00:00:37].

    A one-stringed instrument? The cliché of the single cause¶

    • [00:00:37] The 9th-century Muslim philosopher al-Kindī had strange ideas about music: he reportedly once cured a young man's apoplexy by having tunes played on a lute, and held that music can affect the soul and expresses the character of the people who play it.
    • [00:00:57] On this theory, different cultures use different numbers of strings. The Greeks, who invented the theory of four elements, played four-stringed lutes; the Indians, al-Kindī claims, make do with a single-stringed instrument to represent their view that the whole world arises from only one cause.
    • [00:01:14] "But we know better." If the survey of the Vedic philosophical schools has taught anything, it is that Indian thinkers were not all singing from the same hymn sheet. True, Vedānta emphasized the derivation of all things from the one brahman — or even, in Advaita Vedānta, the identity of things with that source. But ancient India also had its fair share of pluralists.

    Vaiśeṣika: pluralist, realist, and accused of "semi-nihilism"¶

    • [00:01:44] The heroes of metaphysical pluralism were the partner school of Nyāya, who went by the name Vaiśeṣika.
    • [00:01:50] Śaṅkara, founder of Advaita Vedānta, scornfully dismissed the Vaiśeṣikas as semi-nihilists, because they failed to ground all things in the one true reality of brahman.
    • [00:02:02] But Vaiśeṣika is in fact committed to thoroughgoing realism. Unlike Advaita, it assumes there is a structured world out there, independent of human conceptions and thought — yet a world we can understand, in the first instance by offering a classification or division of reality into its various types.

    The founding text and its owlish author¶

    • [00:02:25] This classifying task is undertaken in the school's founding document, the Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra, thought to have been compiled around the first century CE — though Vaiśeṣika ideas were certainly circulating much earlier.
    • [00:02:43] Its author is Kaṇāda, also known as Ulūka, "the owl." Several explanations are offered for the nickname: perhaps it just plays on "Kaṇāda," which means grain-eater (like an owl), though it can also be read as atom-eater, since Vaiśeṣika is committed to atomism. A more fabulous story has it that the whole system was revealed to Kaṇāda by a god who had assumed the form of an owl.
    • [00:03:14] Like other works in sūtra form, the Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra is compressed and hard to understand. Since "no further explanation was forthcoming from divine owls," the tradition turned for clarification to a later work by Praśastapāda, who lived in the 6th century CE.
    • [00:03:35] In a departure from the usual procedure, Praśastapāda's work is not a formal commentary (bhāṣya) but a freestanding treatise, albeit one clearly inspired by Kaṇāda. It became the touchstone for later authors even more than the sūtra itself — modern scholar Wilhelm Halbfass calls Praśastapāda "the most authoritative representative, if not the creator, of classical Vaiśeṣika."
    • [00:04:02] Though both men are known above all for metaphysics, they keep a keen interest in ethics and liberation. The ethically loaded term dharma even opens the Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra — though here it may carry the more metaphysical meaning it has in Buddhism rather than the ethical sense familiar from Mīmāṃsā, which fits better with the metaphysical analysis that immediately follows.

    The six categories (padārtha) — the world structured like language¶

    • [00:04:36] Experience with Sāṃkhya would lead us to expect this analysis to come as a list, with items subdivided into further lists — and Kaṇāda does not disappoint.
    • [00:04:52] The initial analysis itemizes six categories, some broken down further: the first category, substance, has nine types; the second, quality, no fewer than 24 types; and so on.
    • [00:05:10] A clue to the project lies in the very word "category," in Sanskrit padārtha. Commentators tell us this combines pada and artha — "word" and "meaning." A category is thus a type of thing that can be signified by language: the noun "owl" refers to a substance (the first category), the adjective "white" to a quality (the second), and so on.
    • [00:05:40] In other words, the system of categories says the world is structured so as to correspond to our language — once again Indian philosophers take their cue from grammar.

    Substance, quality, motion — and the unobservable substances¶

    • [00:05:53] The first three categories are substance, quality, and motion. An owl is a substance; it has qualities (the color of its feathers) and motions (its swoop down on an unsuspecting mouse).
    • [00:06:09] Crucially, for Vaiśeṣika a quality or motion is just as much a particular individual entity as the owl itself. The white of this owl belongs only to it, as does the particular swoop it performs to land on the shoulder of its wizard owner.
    • [00:06:24] Although the theory accommodates such familiar examples, the substances in Kaṇāda's first category are not necessarily observable. You can perceive an owl, but from the Vaiśeṣika point of view there are also substances unobservable in principle — and these are the most fundamental: atoms (paramāṇu), space, time, and a pervasive substance called Ākāśa, sometimes translated "ether."
    • [00:06:57] Observable beings like owls are composed from atoms, which (unlike owls) are indestructible and eternal. So too are ether and the more familiar elements — earth, water, fire, air. These five are called bhūta, or "physical substance," meaning they have sensible qualities.
    • [00:07:15] Originally the school may have correlated each of the five substances to one of the five sensible qualities — odor residing in earth, color in fire, and so on — so that a complex substance like an owl, combining all five elements, is perceptible to the full range of our senses. Perhaps the wish to match our five senses even motivated positing five substances. But the simple version is hard to credit (is earth really invisible until mixed with fire to give it color?), and by the time of the Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra it had been abandoned; Kaṇāda still draws correlations, but makes them more complex.

    Why atoms? The argument from divisibility¶

    • [00:08:15] Our most basic bodily substances, then, are atoms of the basic elements — atoms of earth, of water, and so on. They are too tiny to perceive and have a special minimal size simply called aṇu ("small"): the smallest any existing thing can be.
    • [00:08:33] But how do we know bodies are at bottom made of indivisible particles? The standard argument: it is an empirically established truth that whatever is perceived is composite. Even the smallest perceptible thing — a fleck of dust in a sunbeam — has parts; and those parts, being smaller than the smallest visible thing, are themselves invisible. Yet (says Vaiśeṣika) those invisible parts have further parts, just as visible cloth is made of threads and threads of smaller strands.
    • [00:09:08] More precisely: the invisible particle is a triad made of three subparts, each of which is a dyad of two parts, and those sub-sub-subparts are the atoms. Going the other way — small to large — pairs of atoms join, three pairs combine into an invisible particle, and these combine into things we can actually see.
    • [00:09:40] The obvious objection: how do we know division can't go on forever? Why not say the six bits of the invisible particle are each still divisible? One Vaiśeṣika answer: if indefinite division were possible, then a mountain and a mustard seed would be the same size, since each would have the same number of constituent parts — namely an infinite number.
    • [00:10:16] The implicit premise (made explicit by other Vaiśeṣikas) is that the size of a whole is a function of the size, number, and spatial arrangement of its parts. The argument looks question-begging: that premise is only true if atomism is already accepted. A non-atomist will say an object's size is fixed not by its constituents but by the spatial boundaries of the continuous stuff it is made of.

    Realism extended: even absences are real¶

    • [00:10:39] Elsewhere in the history of philosophy, atomists have been tempted to think non-atomic substances and their qualities are somehow unreal — as in Democritus's line, "by convention sweet, by convention color, but in reality, atoms and void."
    • [00:11:05] Not so for Vaiśeṣika, with its strong commitment to realism: for Kaṇāda and his successors, the owl, the owl's color, and the atoms are all real.
    • [00:11:18] Later in the tradition, from about the 12th century, this realism was extended even to the absences of things, added as a seventh category. This is the metaphysical version of the thesis met in the sister school Nyāya — that you can perceive an absence, like Ferris Bueller missing from behind his desk. (Getting ahead of the list and of history.)

    Categories four and five: universals and individuators¶

    • [00:11:48] The fourth category is universals — the general classes under which substances, qualities, and motions fall. There are not just individual owls like Hedwig but the whole species (snowy owl), which belongs to the more general class of owls, which belongs to the still-more-general class of animals.
    • [00:12:19] The chain does not go on indefinitely: in Vaiśeṣika, being is the highest, most general universal, since everything that exists is a being. There are universals for qualities and motions too — Hedwig's white belongs to her alone, but whiteness is found in many substances, so whiteness is a general class just as "owl" is.
    • [00:12:47] The fifth category answers: how does an individual owl become that individual? This was a major issue for medieval Europe too — Peter Abelard, Gilbert of Poitiers, Duns Scotus. Vaiśeṣika's answer strikingly anticipates Scotus (or rather Scotus echoes Vaiśeṣika, since Kaṇāda proposed it more than a millennium earlier): there must be a unique distinguisher, a principle of individuation for each particular.
    • [00:13:29] Kaṇāda's real need for this is less about owls than atoms. One snowy owl can be told from another (whiter feathers, more tired from delivering the post), but one fire atom is liable to be utterly indistinguishable from every other. The distinguishing principle explains why each individual atom is the atom it is. This matters all the more because owls and all bodies are atomic compounds, so the individuality of complex bodies ultimately stands or falls with the individuality of their atoms.

    Category six: inherence (samavāya)¶

    • [00:14:14] The sixth and final category is inherence (samavāya) — again Kaṇāda anticipates a problem and solves it before anyone can complain (or perhaps someone had complained).
    • [00:14:33] The problem: on one side we have substances; on the other the qualities, motions, universals, and individuating distinguishers that belong to them. What connects these features to the substances that display them? What explains that this white belongs to Hedwig? Simply the fact that it inheres in her — and this connection of inherence is itself something real, deserving its place on the list.
    • [00:15:03] Inherence even explains why substances have a special place: they do not inhere in anything else, but rather have other things inhering in them. Our owl does not belong to anything the way her white color belongs to her.
    • [00:15:22] But that isn't quite right. The Vaiśeṣikas are intensely interested in how wholes relate to their parts, because Kaṇāda and his followers want to refute the Buddhists, who deny the reality and unity of wholes by decomposing them into parts — the chariot that is "nothing more than wheels, axle, and so on."
    • [00:15:52] The realist Vaiśeṣika instead recognizes wholes as perfectly respectable entities: Hedwig is real, even made of atoms. But a composite being differs from her atoms, and this difference is expressed via inherence: the whole inheres in its parts, much as white color inheres in Hedwig. So strictly, an owl has two kinds of inherence relations — white color inheres in her, and she inheres in her own parts (ultimately the atoms). Atoms alone inherit in nothing: they belong to nothing the way a quality or motion does, and they have no parts.

    Knowledge of the categories and the highest good¶

    • [00:16:39] All this may seem far from the moral duty and liberation stressed by the other Vedic schools. What could ethical goals have to do with a list of six categories that merely "enumerates everything in this world that has the character of being"?
    • [00:17:03] Plenty, says Praśastapāda: knowing the truth about the six categories of being does help lead us to the highest good. No wonder Vaiśeṣika joins forces with Nyāya, since both promise that knowledge leads to this lofty goal — Nyāya supplying an account of what knowledge consists in, Vaiśeṣika the analysis of the world we grasp when knowledge is achieved. "Like the United States, Gautama has a friendly neighbor in the shape of Kaṇāda."

    The real rival: Buddhist anti-realism¶

    • [00:17:33] But Gautama and Kaṇāda, and their followers in Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika, have a serious rival: the anti-realism of the Buddhists, who also promise to bring us to liberation and the highest good — but by unmasking the unreality of what seems real.
    • [00:17:47] For the Buddhist, the only therapy for human suffering is to undermine commitment to a world of enduring subjects and objects, because the belief that we and other things are real and endure through time inevitably provokes needs, cravings, and attachments.
    • [00:18:04] Some Hindu thinkers, inspired rather than appalled by this strategy, also embraced anti-realism — arguably the Advaita Vedānta thinkers, who keep the authority of the Upaniṣads but read them as pointing toward brahman, a single reality hidden behind appearances.
    • [00:18:29] For the Vaiśeṣikas, a better response is to demonstrate that realism is true — for if it is, then Buddhist therapy rests on a mistake. Nor is it their only mistake: the Vaiśeṣikas insist on the reality of wholes made of parts as well as partless atoms.
    • [00:18:50] What arguments could defeat the Buddhist view, and what more can be said about the whole–part relation beyond a "rather mysterious appeal to the idea of inherence"? That is the subject of the next part, devoted entirely to the Vaiśeṣika theory of material composition.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Vaiśeṣika — realist, pluralist school, partner of Nyāya; classifies reality into six (later seven) categories; dismissed by Śaṅkara as "semi-nihilist" for not grounding all things in brahman.
    • Kaṇāda (Ulūka, "the owl") — author of the Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra; the name means "grain-eater" or "atom-eater," and one legend has the system revealed to him by an owl-shaped god.
    • Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra — the school's founding document, compiled c. 1st c. CE; compressed sūtra form, opening with the term dharma.
    • Praśastapāda — 6th-c. CE author of a freestanding treatise (not a bhāṣya) that became the school's true touchstone; called by Halbfass "the creator of classical Vaiśeṣika."
    • padārtha — "category"; etymologized as pada + artha ("word" + "meaning"), i.e. a type of thing that language can signify — so the categories mirror grammar.
    • the six categories — substance, quality, motion, universals, individuators (principle of individuation), and inherence (a seventh, absence, added c. 12th c.).
    • dravya (substance) — first category, nine types; includes both observable things (owls) and unobservable fundamentals (atoms, space, time, ether).
    • paramāṇu (atom) / aṇu — eternal, indestructible, indivisible particle of minimal size (aṇu = "small"); bodies are built from atom-pairs → dyads → triads (the smallest invisible particle).
    • bhūta — "physical substance"; the five elements (earth, water, fire, air, ether) that bear sensible qualities, perhaps matched to the five senses.
    • Ākāśa — ether; a pervasive, unobservable, eternal substance.
    • universals — general classes (snowy owl → owl → animal); being is the highest, since everything that exists is a being.
    • principle of individuation — the unique distinguisher that makes each particular itself; chiefly needed for otherwise-identical atoms; anticipates Duns Scotus.
    • samavāya (inherence) — the real connection binding qualities/motions/etc. to substances, and wholes to their parts; substances are special because they inhere in nothing else.
    • realism vs. Buddhist anti-realism — Vaiśeṣika defends the reality of owls, qualities, atoms, and wholes against Buddhists who dissolve wholes into parts (the chariot) as therapy against craving; allied with Nyāya, it holds that knowing reality's structure leads to the highest good.

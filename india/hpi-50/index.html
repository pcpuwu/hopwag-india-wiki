    HoPWaG India · Ep 50 — "Marie-Hélène Gorisse on Jain Epistemology"¶

    ← Ep 49

    Ep 51 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson with Marie-Hélène Gorisse 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson with Marie-Hélène Gorisse · Runtime: 00:31:50 In brief: An interview on Jaina logic and epistemology: how the Jainas, working inside India's "debating hall," ground inference in epistemology and ultimately in soteriology, defend a dualist soul against Buddhist no-self, and build their signature doctrine of non-one-sidedness (anekāntavāda) — a perspectivism that says rival schools each grasp one true aspect of an irreducibly complex reality, capped by a distinctive theory of inference set against Dignāga and Dharmakīrti. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Marie-Hélène Gorisse ≈ "Marie-Elein-Len-Gorice"; Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gannery"; Jaina ≈ "giant/Jain"; anekāntavāda ≈ "Anikauntavada"; nayavāda ≈ "Naya Vada"; Mahāvīra ≈ "Maharevira"; Dignāga ≈ "Dignaga"; Dharmakīrti ≈ "Darmakirti/Dada Makhiote/Dama Kioti"; Tarka; avinābhāva ≈ "Aniata"; Saṅghadāsa ≈ "Sangadasha"; Digambara ≈ "Degombara").

    Who the Jaina thinkers are, and where they sit¶

    • [00:00:43] Adamson opens by asking Gorisse to place the Jaina logicians: who they are, roughly when they lived (he bets she'll say "we don't know"), and where they sit in India's "broader debating network of schools."
    • [00:01:04] Gorisse first sets the stage. The thinkers she works on contributed to a highly regulated debating hall that was "center stage" in Indian philosophy from roughly the end of the 2nd century CE to the 11th century.
    • [00:01:35] That centrality comes from method: alongside perceptual knowledge and the correct interpretation of sacred texts, the best way to secure a genuine piece of knowledge is inferential reasoning — and her own work characterizes that inference.
    • [00:01:54] A key structural point: logical considerations (necessity, the properties of relations) are directed toward epistemological purposes — clearly defining the means by which we acquire new knowledge. And those epistemological considerations are in turn directed toward soteriological purposes — defining how one should behave in order to "fully realize my own nature." Logic serves epistemology serves liberation.
    • [00:02:39] Three main parties debated in this hall: the Buddhists, the Hindu philosophers of Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, and Mīmāṃsā, and the Jainas — her focus.
    • [00:02:56] Jainism, she notes, is pervasively important in Indian culture yet "largely unknown in the West." It is a religious and philosophical tradition whose last great spiritual leader is Mahāvīra, who lived in the 5th century BCE. Like Buddhism, it arose as a reaction against the ritualistic focus of the Vedic corpus, offering instead an "inner fight" grounded in ascetic practices. Jainas today live worldwide but are concentrated in west-central India.

    The soul as the subject of knowledge¶

    • [00:03:44] Adamson sums up the picture: the Jainas are one school among several debating now-familiar issues, especially with Nyāya — they too have a theory of inference, even "scientific reasoning," as do the Buddhists. But before the epistemology, he asks about the subject of knowledge: the Jainas believe in a soul, so what is it like and how does it relate to matter?
    • [00:04:33] Gorisse: because Jainism is first of all a soteriology, its metaphysics is subordinated to ethics — it must secure human responsibility, which requires a persisting self-identity and an "influential active self."
    • [00:04:58] They get this through a dualism. On one side are souls — single, independent, isolated subjective entities whose very essence is consciousness. On the other is non-soul stuff: objectivity consisting in matter and its organizing principles.
    • [00:05:38] Within the material realm the focus is karma, understood as a kind of matter whose specific property is to develop the consequences of our virtuous or unethical acts. Because our acts witness the self's desire toward external objects, karmic matter takes the form of a given limited mental state.
    • [00:06:06] So the core problem is not to explain but to get rid of the interaction between karmic matter and souls, in order to reach a non-limited state of consciousness. In principle we are all omniscient; only by shedding karmic matter do we realize that omniscient nature.
    • [00:06:35] This matters epistemologically: the Jainas hold their spiritual leaders reached omniscience, so their corpus counts as authoritative. Mahāvīra, for instance, achieved liberation even in this life, while still embodied, by divesting himself of bodily concerns so thoroughly that he recovered the original omniscience we all share.
    • [00:07:12] Adamson confirms; Gorisse adds that the very nature of his omniscience was such that he spontaneously taught what he knew to everyone — "it just flows out of him," Adamson says — by emitting, for some of the Digambara Jainas, a kind of divine sound.

    Jainism vs. the Buddhists — and a likeness to Sāṃkhya¶

    • [00:07:32] Adamson finds it striking that the subsisting independent soul is so crucial, because people often assume Jaina theories resemble Buddhist ones, since both reject Vedic orthodoxy. Yet on this point they are diametrically opposed to the Buddhists.
    • [00:08:02] Gorisse agrees emphatically. Adamson notes the Jaina view actually sounds more like Sāṃkhya — an independent immaterial soul set against a material subject in which the soul resides. Gorisse: yes, closer to Sāṃkhya, and also to some Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika considerations.

    Non-one-sidedness (anekāntavāda): everybody is partly right¶

    • [00:08:30] That very feature — Jainism echoing several rival schools at once — goes along with its epistemology, which "in a way seems to be a way of saying that everybody is right." This is the famous theory of non-one-sidedness, a kind of perspectivism; they speak of naya or viewpoints. The thought: whatever anyone knows is always known from a perspective, so I attain truth only from my own standpoint.
    • [00:09:20] Gorisse confirms the doctrine's name — anekāntavāda, the theory of non-one-sidedness, which yields that perspectivism. She grounds it in the claim that Jaina doctrine is a philosophy of synthesis of ancient India's metaphysical theories.
    • [00:09:40] Crucially, the Jainas are realists. They explain the plurality of coexisting yet apparently conflicting valid epistemic stances by the complexity of the knowable itself — the complexity is in the object, not merely in us. This drove them to original semantic approaches: the theory of viewpoints (naya), plus a parallel theory of angles of analysis used in hermeneutics.
    • [00:10:21] That hermeneutic application is especially for interpreting texts — above all monastic rules. Adamson's example: if some monastic orders say you should be naked and others say you shouldn't, you can contextualize both by noting that the recipients differ, so different practices serve the same purpose. Gorisse notes this is one of the main divergences between Mahāvīra and the previous spiritual teacher (a reference to Pārśva, whose name the audio drops).

    What it means for a claim to be only contextually valid¶

    • [00:11:01] Adamson asks what it means for a claim to be only contextually valid. Gorisse takes her examples from rival epistemological traditions. The Buddhist insistence on momentariness represents a perspective that fixes on the most specific aspect of the knowable — it says something deep and true about the transient character of phenomena.
    • [00:11:44] Human knowledge requires recognizing common features, but we can halt our focus at different levels, from the most specific to the most general. Buddhists stop at the most specific. That is a partially correct but incomplete account — it cannot, for instance, think a persisting self and ethical responsibility.
    • [00:12:12] The Jainas go further: even perspectives that recognize different aspects can fail. They are keen to argue that Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika are wrong to treat a substance (e.g. a table) and its qualities (e.g. its touchable quality) as two distinct entities linked by a relation of inherence.
    • [00:12:39] On the Jaina theory of identity-in-difference, there is one single complex entity that can be grasped either under its substantial or its qualitative aspect — a "fluidity" also manifest in their atomist physics: atoms are all alike, take on specific characteristics, and evolve into the five elements.
    • [00:13:14] Adamson checks his understanding: the Buddhist momentariness thesis is "not completely wrong" because there is something momentary about our existence, but it is not the whole story (Gorisse: exactly). Yet the Jainas don't simply say all theories are correct — when Vaiśeṣika splits an underlying substance from its property, the Jainas say that's wrong, because you're really thinking of the same object two ways. The upshot: there are complex entities understandable only from many perspectives, and rival schools usually manage only one perspective at best. Gorisse agrees.

    Does non-one-sidedness apply beyond philosophy?¶

    • [00:14:14] Adamson wonders whether the theory is only about philosophical theories or applies more broadly. If we're both sitting in chairs, would they apply non-one-sidedness to that belief — "yeah, you're sitting in a chair, but that's only one perspective on a more complex situation"?
    • [00:14:49] Gorisse: their focus is philosophical theories, especially the permanence or impermanence of metaphysical entities like the soul. But if you can find practical value, there's no problem applying the perspectivism to everyday life.
    • [00:15:08] She cites Saṅghadāsa, a 6th-century commentator on monastic rules, who argued that under-specified statements can be quite useful for adapting rules that are too strict, via a "wise contextual interpretation."
    • [00:15:40] For the chair, she can find no practical use; but in court one might say "I have not been acting as a citizen but as an ethical agent" with another set of underlying rules. Adamson extends it: one set of responsibilities as a father, another as a philosophy teacher — and probably to politics too. He concludes it's "a very powerful idea."

    The self-refutation objection¶

    • [00:16:18] Adamson raises an objection: the Jainas seem to claim that the theory of non-one-sidedness is correct — not just one perspective among others, but the right epistemology. So aren't they inconsistent, failing to apply their own theory to itself?
    • [00:16:57] Gorisse's reply: non-one-sidedness functions as meta-epistemology. Just as the rules governing the movement of spatial objects don't apply to space itself (as the condition of possibility of motion), the rules of epistemological theories needn't apply to a theory of what makes a good epistemological theory.
    • [00:17:26] She concedes Adamson is right that they do develop a substantive epistemology and conception of the world, and defend it against rivals. Her way to make sense of it: the complex object they aim to know is complex in such a way that the only way to know it is to disregard any particular focus and become aware of the manifold particular-and-universal aspects. So they are "practicing non-one-sidedness even in developing their conception of the world."

    How they defend non-one-sidedness — by refutation, like Nāgārjuna¶

    • [00:18:12] Adamson adds that they defend non-one-sidedness against one-sided theories by attacking those theories, not by giving an a-priori argument for non-one-sidedness.
    • [00:18:37] Gorisse: yes — and not only on the ground that the rivals are one-sided. They also attack them as incoherent theories that don't hold up.
    • [00:18:54] Adamson draws the parallel: like Nāgārjuna attacking Vedic positions with skeptical arguments, the Jaina skeptical arguments expose the limitations of one-sidedness. Gorisse: there's no positive way to establish non-one-sidedness, but one can refute every one-sided claim — close to Nāgārjuna in that way.
    • [00:19:25] But it's not a skeptical theory, Adamson stresses: they don't say knowledge is impossible; they say knowledge is possible, but only if you adopt a variety of viewpoints. Gorisse: indeed, and humans can even draw up an extensive, exhaustive classification of the types of stance on the knowable — a "deep faith in knowledge."

    The mango: three theories of inference¶

    • [00:19:54] One sign of that faith is inference, where the Jainas wage a polemic against the Buddhists. Adamson's favorite example (from her papers): from a mango's color I infer its taste — if it looks ripe, it will taste ripe (sweet). Buddhists and Jainas account for this differently.
    • [00:20:37] Gorisse: I'd like to know whether a mango will taste ripe before I taste it (and pay for it). A good way is to reason from the evidence of its ripe color, then ask whether there is a necessary relationship between color and taste.
    • [00:21:11] Dignāga (5th–6th c.) sets three conditions for the color to be good evidence: (1) this mango actually has the ripe color; (2) every ripe mango I've tasted had that color; (3) I've never tasted an unripe mango with that color.
    • [00:21:47] The Jainas — and others — object that these conditions are neither sufficient nor necessary, because such a state of affairs potentially describes an accidental, not a necessary, relation.
    • [00:22:08] For the Jainas, evidence is good only if known to be "impossible otherwise" — avinābhāva — and this is grasped by a separate type of knowledge called Tarka, functioning as a direct discernment of the universal.
    • [00:22:24] The Buddhist Dharmakīrti (6th c.) refutes the need for any such extra knowledge. Evidence is good if one can show an essential or causal relationship between taste and color — giving an ontic foundation to valid reasoning. In the mango case it's a combination of two causal relations: the ripeness causes the color (and is the only explanation of that specific color), and by the same reasoning the ripeness causes the taste.
    • [00:23:20] Here the Jainas disagree with Dharmakīrti. They argue some inferences rest not on essential/causal relations but on relations of co-presence (color and taste) and of succession (e.g. the rising of stars) — these too can ground sound inferences. Accepting such inferences shows the Jainas grant a deeper regularity of worldly phenomena than the Buddhists do.
    • [00:24:03] Adamson reconstructs the three positions: (1) Dignāga — as long as color and taste are always found together, infer one from the other; (2) Dharmakīrti — you need an underlying cause (ripeness) producing both, which guarantees the inference; (3) Jainas — Dharmakīrti is right that Dignāga's mere constant conjunction isn't enough, but you needn't appeal to an underlying nature: past experience suffices, so it's more like inference based on induction. Gorisse: "Yes, it's exactly this."

    Logical analysis, and Adamson's own verdict¶

    • [00:25:26] This, Gorisse says, is one of many examples of logical analysis in the classical Indian debating hall. After Dharmakīrti it chiefly asks: what are the exact properties between which a necessary relation holds? It's mainly concerned with metaphysical topics — e.g. does "being an effect" necessarily presuppose having a conscious producer? If so, one can build an argument for the pervasive action of a god in the world. "When we speak about logic in India, this is all about the pervasion of one property by another."
    • [00:26:13] Adamson confesses that if we're really talking about necessary inferences, he's "totally on Dharmakīrti's side." Dignāga's critics are right that two properties merely always being found together doesn't make them necessarily connected — they might come apart later, and induction famously doesn't license necessary inference. Dharmakīrti can appeal to an underlying nature or cause that necessitates: whatever has the ripe color has the ripe taste.
    • [00:27:00] Gorisse: so Dharmakīrti goes from universal relations to necessary ones — "if I know there's an oak, I know there's a tree," and even if every tree were destroyed this stays true. Whereas the Jaina who says "one star rises, so the other will rise" faces the worry: what if the second star dies?
    • [00:27:31] Her way to make sense of the Jainas wanting necessity and not merely universality: they grant such a deep regularity of worldly phenomena that, the next time the universe manifests, the nature of the stars is such that one will succeed the other as before. Adamson: so they appeal to the theory of world cycles to explain how an inductive inference could license necessary claims. Gorisse: "Exactly."

    Tying it back to ethics and liberation¶

    • [00:28:12] To close, Adamson returns to the ethical/soteriological frame: like everyone in ancient India, the Jainas care about liberation and freedom from suffering — but how do these abstract epistemological ideas connect?
    • [00:28:43] Gorisse first names the standard answer, then rejects it. Non-one-sidedness is often called intellectual nonviolence — the intellectual version of not harming other beings, at the core of Jainism and an inspiration to Gandhi. But this is unconvincing: rival theories aren't all merely partially good (Jaina theories are completely good), they're usually dismissed entirely, not just partially — at best assimilated into Jaina theory. That cross-sectarian assimilation, she notes, lets Jainas borrow from rivals to an "impressive" degree.
    • [00:30:01] Adamson: right — if the point were just "I'm non-violent, so I won't argue," why spend so much time refuting other theories? Gorisse: exactly, that's not what they're doing.
    • [00:30:11] Her better account: the three ethical principles of Jainism are non-violence, non-attachment, and non-absolutism. In practicing non-absolutism one acts with special care and realizes that everything is interconnected — and the interpretive techniques tied to non-one-sidedness yield the same recognition of interconnection.
    • [00:30:46] This helps stop the inflow of karmic matter: once we see the interconnected paradigm, we cease desiring external objects. So the practice of perspectivism is genuinely part of ethical behavior.
    • [00:31:08] Adamson restates it: the maxim isn't "don't be violent" but "don't have a limited set of concerns — extend your concern to everything, because everything is interconnected," which goes with seeing the world's complexity from a maximally well-rounded set of viewpoints. Gorisse: "Yes, this is what I meant."
    • [00:31:32] Adamson finds Jaina epistemology "quite attractive" — convinced by the epistemology if not the theory of inference — and thanks Gorisse before signing off to next time.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Marie-Hélène Gorisse — the guest; Senior Teaching Fellow at SOAS (London) and at Ghent University; works on Jaina logic and inferential reasoning.
    • Jainas / Jainism — religious-philosophical tradition reacting against Vedic ritualism via ascetic practice; one of the three main parties (with Buddhists and Hindu Nyāya/Vaiśeṣika/Mīmāṃsā) in India's debating hall.
    • Mahāvīra — last great Jaina spiritual leader (5th c. BCE); attained omniscience and liberation while still embodied, then spontaneously taught what he knew.
    • soteriology — Jaina metaphysics is subordinated to liberation; logic serves epistemology, which serves the realization of one's true (omniscient) nature.
    • soul / non-soul dualism — souls are isolated, independent subjects whose essence is consciousness; non-soul is matter and its organizing principles.
    • karma — for the Jainas a kind of matter that develops the consequences of our acts and produces limited mental states; liberation = shedding it to recover innate omniscience.
    • anekāntavāda (non-one-sidedness) — the signature doctrine: a realist perspectivism holding that rival schools each grasp one true aspect of an irreducibly complex reality.
    • naya / nayavāda (viewpoints) — the semantic theory of perspectives; all knowledge is from a standpoint, fixed at some level from most specific to most general.
    • identity-in-difference — the Jaina view that substance and quality are one complex entity grasped two ways, against the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika split joined by inherence.
    • Saṅghadāsa — 6th-c. commentator on monastic rules; argued under-specified statements help adapt over-strict rules by contextual interpretation.
    • Digambara — Jaina group for whom the omniscient teacher's teaching flows out as a divine sound.
    • Dignāga — Buddhist logician (5th–6th c.); good evidence = three conditions of constant conjunction between mark and target (color/taste).
    • Dharmakīrti — Buddhist logician (6th c.); rejects extra knowledge-types, grounding good evidence in an essential/causal relation, moving from universality to necessity.
    • Tarka / avinābhāva — Jaina "direct discernment of the universal" (Tarka) by which evidence is known to be "impossible otherwise" (avinābhāva), licensing inference from co-presence and succession.
    • non-violence / non-attachment / non-absolutism — the three Jaina ethical principles; practicing non-absolutism realizes universal interconnection and stems the inflow of karma.

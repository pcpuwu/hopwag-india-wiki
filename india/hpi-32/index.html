    HoPWaG India · Ep 32 — "What You See Is What You Get: Nyāya on Perception"¶

    ← Ep 31

    Ep 33 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:21:39 In brief: A close reading of Gautama's four-part definition of perception (pratyakṣa) in the Nyāya-Sūtra — sensory contact, non-verbal awareness, non-deviation, and definiteness — and how each clause, elaborated by the commentators, is aimed at defending a common-sense realism against the Buddhist drive to reduce experience to bare, conceptless sense-data. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Ganari"; Nyāya ≈ "Niaya/Niyaya"; Naiyāyikas ≈ "Nayaikas/Nayayikas"; pratyakṣa ≈ "Pratyaksha"; pramāṇa ≈ "pramana"; Vātsyāyana ≈ "Vatsyayana/Batsyayana/Vatsya Yenna"; Uddyotakara ≈ "Ujjotakara/Ujotakara/Udiotakara"; Dharmakīrti ≈ "Damakirti"; Nāgasena ≈ "Naga Sena"; Milindapañha ≈ "Melinda Panya"; skandhas ≈ "scandas"; dewlap ≈ "due lap/doolap").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Gautama — author of the Nyāya-Sūtra; supplies the definition of perception under examination [00:00:45].
    • Vātsyāyana — the foundational commentator, who classifies the sūtras into naming, defining and critical kinds [00:00:52].
    • Uddyotakara — later super-commentator on Vātsyāyana; distinguishes six types of perceptual connection and defends the perception of absences [00:06:12].
    • Dharmakīrti — later Buddhist; counters that "perceiving an absence" is really an inference from non-perception [00:08:49].
    • The Buddhists — Gautama's standing opponents; reduce experience to immediate, concept-free sense-data and deny that wholes exist [00:03:50].

    Defining your terms — Vātsyāyana on the structure of the sūtras¶

    • [00:00:13] The title sets the theme: What You See Is What You Get: Nyāya on Perception. Good philosophers should always define their terms, and the authors of the foundational text of Nyāya "were nothing if not good philosophers." So Gautama, in his Nyāya-Sūtra, pauses to explain the key terms of his theory.
    • [00:00:52] This feature was highlighted by the commentator Vātsyāyana, who — like other commentators — wants to bring out the underlying logic and structure of sūtras that "at first glance may look like nothing more than a list of unconnected or vaguely connected aphorisms." He introduces a distinction between three kinds of remark Gautama makes: naming sūtras (which introduce a topic or concept for analysis), defining sūtras (which offer a definition of the concept), and critical sūtras (which examine and evaluate the adequacy of the proposed definition).
    • [00:01:27] And if the Naiyāyikas were really good philosophers, they would also tell us what they mean by giving a definition — and they do. A sound definition provides "some property that is coextensive with the concept to be defined." We should steer a middle course between overly narrow and overly wide definitions; showing that a proposed definition suffers from neither flaw is the job of the critical sūtras.

    The cow, the dewlap, and a modest ambition¶

    • [00:01:58] Their favourite example is the cow. We shouldn't define a cow as something tawny-coloured, because not all cows are tawny — some are black and white — so this definition fails through undercoverage. But we shouldn't overreact by naming some broad property: define a cow as something with horns and we commit the opposite error of overcoverage, since other things have horns too, "like reindeer and the James Brown band."
    • [00:02:33] A better definition: a cow is something with a dewlap — the loose skin that hangs from the cow's neck — "since all cows and only cows have a dewlap."
    • [00:02:40] Note how modest the achievement is. The definition does not reveal the essence or nature of cows; it just gives a reliable way of picking cows out from everything else. This same modest aim governs Gautama's definition of perception.

    Gautama's four-part definition of perception (pratyakṣa)¶

    • [00:02:52] Perception — pratyakṣa — is defined early in the Nyāya-Sūtra, in one of the aphorisms Vātsyāyana designates a defining sūtra. A perceptual experience is that which (1) is produced from contact between sense faculty and object, (2) is a non-verbal awareness, (3) does not deviate or err from its object, and (4) is definite in character.
    • [00:03:28] This is impressive. Ask your friends over dinner what perception is and they'd say "seeing stuff"; Gautama gives a group of features that must all be satisfied before an experience counts as perception.

    The Buddhist target behind the definition¶

    • [00:03:50] Gautama's historical influences are speculative, but he is fairly certainly responding to early Buddhist discussions of perception. Early Buddhists treated perception as a kind of direct sensory acquaintance with sensible data — colours, sounds, smells. That sounds no more advanced than the dinner-party answer, but "appearances can be deceiving."
    • [00:04:14] The Buddhists gave perception a special place: for them suffering is caused by attachment to false ideas and mental constructs — the idea of a permanent self, or of essences belonging to everyday objects. Freedom from suffering means letting go of such attachments, which leaves "nothing but simple perceptions, the remaining residue of mental life." That is why the Buddhist definition stresses the immediacy of sense experience — perception untainted by false theoretical postulates.
    • [00:04:51] The Nyāya definition, for all its complexity, has a humbler objective: it just seeks to articulate "our ordinary common-sense notion of what constitutes a perceptual awareness."

    First clause — sensory contact, and the trouble with mirrors¶

    • [00:05:09] The first condition: there must be a direct sensory connection or causal link between object and perceiver. This works for seeing a cow in a field. But what about seeing something in a mirror, through binoculars, or through a video camera? We'd normally call these perception — but do they bring us into contact with the object, or only with an image of it?
    • [00:05:39] One might save the definition by allowing indirect contact through images, but it's hard to believe that perceiving a representation is the same as perceiving the thing. "Do I really see my sister when I look at a picture of her? And do you really hear her when I tell you what she said?" Gautama, on his own, has not said enough.

    Uddyotakara's six kinds of perceptual connection¶

    • [00:06:12] The commentators rise to the challenge. In his super-commentary on Vātsyāyana, Uddyotakara distinguishes six types of perceptual connection:
    • Direct conjunction — as between me and a pot when I see it on the stove top.
    • Perceiving what inheres in an object — seeing the blue colour of the pot.
    • Perceiving what inheres in what inheres — the universal blueness that resides in the pot's blue colour.
    • & 5. Perceiving that such a property, and that such a universal, inheres in something — the subtle difference between seeing the blue of the pot and seeing that the property blue (or the universal blueness) is present in it.
    • The connection between an object and its absence — as when I see that there is no pot in the kitchen. ("Probably the giraffe took it when we threw her out after the last episode.")
    • [00:07:10] For Uddyotakara we perceive not just objects but also their qualities, properties, and motions — crucial for Nyāya. Unlike the Buddhists, they insist that blueness is objectively out there in the world, so I should be able to perceive it. But abstract blueness can't make me see blue on its own; for that it must inhere in some particular object like the pot. Universals are by nature located in particulars; I can't perceive them directly, only by perceiving the particulars in which they occur.

    Perceiving absence — negative entities and Ferris Bueller¶

    • [00:07:58] The most provocative of the six is the last. As the school evolves, Nyāya becomes known for a wholesale commitment to the reality of negative entities. Consider "the classic cinematic study of not showing up," Ferris Bueller's Day Off: when the teacher sweeps his gaze across the room, he doesn't merely see an empty chair where Ferris would sit — he sees that Ferris is not there, i.e. perceives the absence of Ferris.
    • [00:08:39] Is that plausible? If the teacher said "I see that Ferris isn't here," isn't that just a figure of speech? The later Buddhist Dharmakīrti would say that in such a case we do not perceive absence but make an inference from a lack of perception: the teacher does not perceive Ferris; if Ferris were here, he would see him; therefore Ferris is not here (and is skipping school again).
    • [00:09:09] Uddyotakara, however, holds that we really do sometimes perceive an absence — absences can inhere in objects just as colours do. The absence of Ferris is a qualifying property residing in the chair where he normally sits.
    • [00:09:27] Gautama had already offered a helpful example: told to fetch the cloths from a pile of fabric that are not marked, what I look for is "the absence of a mark on the cloth"; when I see that absence, I know to take it.
    • [00:09:47] The issue looks pedantic but exposes a deep disagreement. The Naiyāyikas are "determined to resist Buddhist attempts to expose the experienced world as one of conceptual fabrication." For Nyāya, "what you see is, for the most part, what you get — experience really does put the world in view."

    Does vision reach out? — the knife and the flower petals¶

    • [00:10:05] A further strand: do the senses connect with their objects in the literal sense of being physically connected? Nyāya claims that when I see something, my vision reaches out to the visible object.
    • [00:10:31] The Buddhists object: if vision really reached out, it would contact closer things before further ones — you'd be aware of seeing a giraffe silhouetted against the full moon before seeing the moon behind it.
    • [00:10:47] The Nyāya reply: vision does contact distant things slightly later, but so fast that we're unaware of the lapse. Their lovely analogy: when I thrust a knife through a pile of flower petals, I'm not aware of cutting each petal separately, even though the knife must pass through them one after another.

    Second clause — non-verbal awareness (conception-free vs conception-loaded)¶

    • [00:11:11] The second clause: perceptual experience is non-verbal. This may look like a concession to the Buddhist demand to strip away conceptual generalizations — but the point is the opposite. Gautama says that although I can perceive something without classifying it under a concept, I can also deploy concepts to enrich my perceptual experience.
    • [00:11:48] So one can have a mere sensory connection with an object without being able to recognize what it is. The ancient Indian would be very familiar with cows but unfamiliar with giraffes: put a giraffe in front of her and she'd see it, but have no concept or word for it. The Buddhist account makes all perception sound like that. Gautama disagrees — such "unvarnished encounters" are not even the typical case.
    • [00:12:19] Those of us familiar with giraffes can perceive them as giraffes, and perceive that a given object is a giraffe. Later Naiyāyikas call the first kind conception-free and the second conception-loaded. The clause admits that conception-free perception is possible, but refuses to concede that all perception is non-verbal. Gautama's view of experience is expansive; the Buddhist's is reductive.

    Third clause — non-deviation, and the theory of error¶

    • [00:12:49] The third clause is the most philosophically problematic: perception is non-deviating. Gautama includes this to mark off genuine perception from illusions and hallucinations: a genuine perceptual experience must convey the truth of things. This fits, since he dignifies perception as a pramāṇa — a means of knowing — and how could a means of knowing sometimes be in error?
    • [00:13:25] Yet the claim seems bizarre: surely we frequently have erroneous perceptions. The classic case is seeing a coiled rope as a snake in a dim corner. There is a sensory connection — with the rope — but we wouldn't say I perceive a snake, since (thankfully) no snake is present. Illusions fail to give knowledge of the world, so Gautama concludes they are not instances of genuine perception.
    • [00:14:03] This drives later Nyāya to a whole theory of cognitive error. In every perceptual experience, genuine or erroneous, something is in contact with the senses — here the rope. Error occurs when I misapply a property to that thing: I wrongly apply the concept snake to the rope. Snakes are real and the rope is real; it's just that no snakehood is present in the rope.
    • [00:14:42] A bonus of this analysis: it explains how I can see that ice is cold or that a flower is fragrant. Having once touched ice and felt its cold, I now see that cold feature without touching — what's "technically called presentation through revived memory," vision drawing on the deliverances of touch or smell. These count as legitimate perceptions, but can also give rise to illusion — e.g. seeing glass as cold because it looks like ice.
    • [00:15:23] All of this presupposes the earlier distinction between concept-laden and concept-free perception. As with seeing the giraffe as a giraffe versus as "an inexplicable object taking up the lion's share of our field of vision," in the illusion I see the rope as a snake when it lacks snakehood — and that difference undermines my claim to a genuine perception. Error creeps in at the application of a concept; the brute perception of the object itself is always immune to error.
    • [00:16:13] On this theory there are no outright misidentifications in which you think you see one object but actually see another — only misdescriptions of the things you do see, since you always have at least a brute, concept-free perception of the object in front of you.
    • [00:16:30] The theory struggles, though, with illusory cases where there is no object at all — hallucinations, and even the blue sky. The problem isn't that the sky isn't blue; it's that we aren't seeing the sky at all — the blue is just light refracted from the sun. For Gautama's theory to work there must be a real external object the viewer contacts and onto which she superimposes blueness. The Naiyāyika might "slither out of this" (taking a cue from the snake) by saying she superimposes blueness onto empty space — but that's not terribly plausible.

    Fourth clause — definite character¶

    • [00:17:37] The fourth condition: perceptual experience must be of definite character. By Vātsyāyana's reading, Gautama has in mind cases where, though I'm in sensory contact with an object, I cannot tell what sort of object it is — like the earlier example of seeing something at a distance and being unsure whether it's a wooden post or a person.
    • [00:18:01] Suppose it really is a person. Can I then say I'm perceiving the person? Gautama says no: genuine perception "must leave no room for doubt." The mere possibility that what I'm seeing is a wooden post prevents the experience from being a real perception — and from being a pramāṇa, a source of knowledge.
    • [00:18:25] This stays consistent with the non-verbal clause: rudimentary, concept-free perception is not infected by doubt, since it involves seeing the object out there "neither as a person nor as a wooden post, nor as anything else."

    Closing debate — perception vs inference, and the whole giraffe¶

    • [00:18:44] Nyāya is "a tradition devoted to the art of debate as much as to the sources of knowledge," and debate is never far below the surface even in a straightforward-looking definition. Gautama's treatment of perception is aimed at the Buddhists, made explicit by the commentators.
    • [00:19:14] Sometimes Gautama engages openly — as with a probably Buddhist argument that all perceptual claims are really disguised inferences: looking at an object I never see the whole thing, only the part facing me (the near surface of a giraffe's body); so "I perceive the giraffe" really means I've seen the facing part and inferred a whole giraffe.
    • [00:19:43] This looks like pedantry — there aren't front-halves of giraffes running around without whole giraffes attached. And, Gautama notes, the argument concedes that I can perceive something without inference: I can perceive the part facing me.
    • [00:19:59] But more is going on. The Buddhists deny that whole entities exist — there are only collections of parts — the point of the famous passage in the Milindapañha (the Questions of Menander) where the sage Nāgasena argues that a chariot is really just a collection of parts. Ultimately the Buddhists drive toward their view of persons as mere collections of mental and physical processes, the skandhas.
    • [00:20:27] So when Gautama insists that we do perceive a whole giraffe, as common sense has it, he's defending a controversial thesis — and he has the metaphysics to back it: just as a universal like blueness is present in "a giraffe that has fallen into a very large pot of blue paint," so the whole giraffe inheres in each of its parts.
    • [00:20:55] This means we can perceive wholes just as we perceive universals: we experience blue by perceiving the object that has blueness, and we perceive a whole by perceiving whichever part makes itself available to our senses. So Gautama fends off the claim that all perception of objects involves inference.
    • [00:21:17] None of which means Gautama underrates inference — on the contrary, he makes inference another pramāṇa alongside perception, with a whole theory of how inferences work and what happens when they go wrong. That is the subject of the next episode.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Nyāya / Naiyāyikas — the realist school of "good philosophers" devoted to debate and the sources of knowledge; insists the experienced world is real, not a conceptual fabrication.
    • Gautama — author of the Nyāya-Sūtra; defines perception by four jointly necessary conditions.
    • Nyāya-Sūtra — the foundational Nyāya text; its aphorisms include the defining sūtra on perception.
    • Vātsyāyana — the foundational commentator; sorts the sūtras into naming, defining, and critical kinds, and reads the definiteness clause as covering cases of uncertain identification.
    • Uddyotakara — super-commentator on Vātsyāyana; lists six types of perceptual connection and defends the perception of absences as properties inhering in objects.
    • Dharmakīrti — later Buddhist; argues that apparent "perception of absence" is really an inference from non-perception.
    • pratyakṣa — perception; defined as awareness produced by sense–object contact, non-verbal, non-deviating, and definite in character.
    • pramāṇa — a means of knowing; perception is one, inference another. Because it yields knowledge, genuine perception cannot err.
    • definition (coextensive property) — a good definition supplies a property holding of all and only the instances, avoiding undercoverage (too narrow) and overcoverage (too broad); the cow's dewlap is the model, not its essence.
    • conception-free vs conception-loaded perception — bare sensory awareness of an object without classifying it, vs perceiving the object as a so-and-so; both are admitted by Nyāya, against the Buddhist reduction of all perception to the bare kind.
    • inherence / universals — properties like blueness, and even wholes, exist in the world by inhering in particulars; we perceive them by perceiving the particulars that bear them.
    • absence (negative entities) — Nyāya's commitment to the reality of absences, which can inhere in objects and be perceived directly (the empty chair, the unmarked cloth).
    • theory of error — perceptual error is misapplying a property (e.g. snakehood to a rope), not seeing a wrong object; brute perception is always veridical, so there are misdescriptions but no misidentifications.
    • presentation through revived memory — seeing that ice is cold or a flower fragrant, vision drawing on remembered touch or smell.
    • skandhas — the bundles of mental and physical processes into which the Buddhists analyse persons; tied to their denial that wholes exist.
    • Milindapañha (Questions of Menander) / Nāgasena — the Buddhist source where Nāgasena argues a chariot is merely a collection of parts — the wholes-don't-exist thesis Gautama resists.

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:22:59 In brief: Indian philosophy splits into realist movements that presuppose knowledge can be had and skeptical ones that doubt it — and the systematic schools meet the skeptic head-on, from the ancient "paradox of inquiry" (how can you investigate what you don't yet know?) to the regress of the pramāṇas (how can a means of knowing certify itself?). Śabara, Kumārila, and Śaṅkara answer the first; Nāgārjuna, Jayarāśi, and Śrīharṣa press the second. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gannery"; Śabara ≈ "Shabara"; Kumārila Bhaṭṭa ≈ "Kumaraara/Kumarira Bata"; Jayarāśi ≈ "Jayaashi"; Śrīharṣa ≈ "Shriharsha/Shri Hasha"; Madhyamaka ≈ "Madiamaka"; jijñāsā ≈ "Jijnasa"; vyutpatti ≈ "Vyutpati"; nirvacana ≈ "Nirvachana"; Sañjaya ≈ "Sanjaya"; Kena Upaniṣad ≈ "Kena Upanishad"; Cārvāka ≈ "Chattvaka").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Śabara — Mīmāṃsā commentator who raises (and answers) the paradox of inquiry on the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra [00:03:56].
    • Kumārila Bhaṭṭa — later Mīmāṃsā writer who sharpens the same paradox [00:04:40].
    • Śaṅkara — 8th-century Vedānta thinker whose Brahma-Sūtra commentary fields the skeptical challenge with three replies [00:05:10].
    • Nāgārjuna — 2nd-century CE Buddhist, founder of the Madhyamaka school; presses the regress of the means of knowing [00:15:19].
    • Jayarāśi — 9th-century skeptic who denies knowledge can even be defined [00:15:27].
    • Śrīharṣa — 12th-century skeptic who turns the regress against counterfactual reasoning [00:15:27].
    • Udayana — 11th-century Vaiśeṣika who proposed counterfactual reasoning as a route to knowledge [00:20:31].

    Recap: realist systems versus skeptical movements¶

    • [00:00:35] Last time began the age of the sūtra with a fundamental clash in Indian philosophy. Rather than the tendentious "orthodox vs. heterodox" framing, the series prefers a cleaner contrast: on one side, systematic works of inquiry written as aphorisms-with-commentary (sūtra and bhāṣya); on the other, traditions that challenge the very terms of those systems.
    • [00:01:02] The clash had political consequences — there are stories of rulers persecuting Buddhists to uphold Brahminical teaching (for the history, Adamson plugs Kit Patrick's History of India podcast). But for this series the rift matters philosophically: broadly, realist movements versus skeptical ones.
    • [00:01:35] Buddhist and Jaina thinkers attacked the core notions of the other schools — language, mind, the self, action, the efficacy of ritual — and went further, doubting the very possibility of systematic inquiry. That worry haunted the commentators who expounded the sūtras.

    The sūtras presuppose that knowledge is possible¶

    • [00:01:59] The sūtras plainly assume knowledge can be had and promise to deliver it through inquiry — you only have to read their first sentences.
    • [00:02:12] The Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra and the Brahma-Sūtra (the founding texts of Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta) are complementary — one aimed at the duties of Vedic ritual, the other at knowledge of the unifying cosmic principle brahman already found in the Upaniṣads — so their openings are nearly identical: "Now then, an inquiry into dharma," and "Now then, an inquiry into brahman." (Adamson's aside to undergraduates: in your essays, cut to the chase like this.)
    • [00:02:57] The Vaiśeṣika-Sūtra and Yoga-Sūtra open not with a promise of inquiry but with an exposition: the Yoga-Sūtra begins "Now an exposition of yoga" — yoga meaning psychophysical discipline — so, as in a well-run yoga class, you know where you stand. Even the lost founding text of the naturalist Cārvāka school began with still greater confidence: "Now then, we will explain reality."
    • [00:03:37] All of which the skeptic threatens to dissolve: if inquiry and explanation are impossible, we could stop reading every one of these sūtras on the spot.

    The paradox of inquiry — Śabara and Kumārila¶

    • [00:03:45] Knowing the best defence is a good offence, the commentators raise the worry themselves. Expounding the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra, Śabara ventriloquizes a critic: what dharma is must be either commonly known or not. If it's commonly known, there's no need for inquiry; if it isn't commonly known, then inquiry into it is pointless.
    • [00:04:20] The word for inquiry is jijñāsā, literally "desire to know." Śabara's point: a desire to obtain something presupposes you already know what you want — so a desire to know something would require that you already know it. A paradox.
    • [00:04:40] Kumārila Bhaṭṭa, a later Mīmāṃsā writer, restates it: what is commonly known can be known, but being already known there's no desire to know it; what is not commonly known is impossible to know, and so is no possible object of a desire to know either.
    • [00:05:02] The same worry crosses into Vedānta. Because the Brahma-Sūtra opens with that promise to inquire into brahman, Śaṅkara (8th c.) begins his commentary with the identical challenge: if we don't already know what brahman is, how could we search for it? — but if we do, there's nothing left to discover.

    Nyāya shrugs it off — and Plato's parallel¶

    • [00:05:27] Curiously, the realists most invested in epistemology, the Nyāya, never confront the paradox head-on. They simply take for granted that there's a way to investigate and reach knowledge. Their interest is in the method for ordinary questions — "is that thing over there a man or a tree stump?" — not the more abstract question of whether such a method could exist at all.
    • [00:06:05] For Nyāya, doubt is not epistemic paralysis but a state in which one has already partially grasped an object, so that it can be a target for further questioning. On their picture the puzzle of inquiry never even arises.
    • [00:06:22] Faithful listeners of the main podcast will recognize the puzzle from elsewhere: Plato's Meno, where Socrates faces the identical problem — you'd need knowledge of a thing in order to inquire into it. Plato's radical fix is recollection: we already know everything knowable, having learned it before birth, and merely need reminding.
    • [00:06:56] But that isn't the only or most obvious solution. Inquiry into something you already know perfectly is fruitless; inquiry into something of which you haven't the foggiest clue is impossible (you couldn't even get started). The natural thought is that our starting point lies in the middle: inquiry begins not from a blank slate or from mastery, but from partial understanding or uncertain belief.

    Śabara's real answer: too many beliefs, not too few¶

    • [00:07:33] That middle-ground move is exactly Śabara's reply to the skeptic. Inquiry into dharma arises not because we know nothing but because we have too many beliefs — there is conflict between rival conceptions of duty, the very sort of conflict dramatized in the Mahābhārata. We investigate in order to decide between them.
    • [00:07:59] A good account of how moral philosophy avoids starting from scratch — but it has a gap. It gives no reason to think two people who disagree about dharma (or brahman) are even disagreeing about the same thing. Is a Vedāntin's clash with a Buddhist critic resolvable on common assumptions, or are they just talking past one another, using the same words in different senses?

    Śaṅkara's three replies: common idea, etymology, direct self-knowledge¶

    • [00:08:22] Śaṅkara's commentary anticipates that further worry. He grants people disagree about brahman's qualities, but insists we all share a common idea of it that gives partial knowledge.
    • [00:08:41] His first proof is an etymology. It was widely held that linguistic analysis — vyutpatti or nirvacana — explains why we use a given word for a given thing (so dharma, from its verbal root, means "that which upholds"). Śaṅkara derives brahman from a root meaning "to grow," showing brahman is maximally great — "all grown up." Another reminder of how central Sanskrit grammar is for Indian philosophers.
    • [00:09:47] That looks flimsy, so Śaṅkara has a stronger, more European-sounding move: the paradox poses no threat here because brahman is a case of direct knowledge. "Everyone knows of the existence of his own self and does not think, 'I am not.'" If one's own self weren't perfectly well known, anyone could doubt his own existence — and no one does.
    • [00:10:16] The parallel with Augustine and Descartes is almost too obvious to mention — but unlike Descartes' "I think therefore I am," Śaṅkara isn't refuting global skepticism. He only wants to show the inquiry into brahman is possible. He invokes the Upaniṣadic "you are that," read as "this self is brahman": since the self can't be doubted, and the self is brahman, brahman can't be doubted either — so there is something there to investigate.
    • [00:11:00] But possibility isn't enough; he must show inquiry is also needed. If we already have immediate knowledge of self/brahman, why inquire? Because our intimate awareness of brahman does not include its true nature. Witness the disputes about the self: materialists and common folk think it's the body; Buddhists that it's just the flow of cognitions; Nyāya that it's an agent and subject of experience distinct from the body; Sāṃkhya that it differs from the body but is not an agent — and so on. Plenty of dispute, hence plenty of reason to investigate.

    Skepticism is as old as Indian philosophy itself¶

    • [00:11:59] These weren't merely hypothetical foes. The history of skepticism in India is at least as old as the history of philosophy in India. The ancient Ṛg Veda's hymn of creation asks: "Who knows for certain... whence came this creation? The gods were born after this creation, then who can know whence it has arisen?... He who surveys it in the highest heaven, he only knows — or perhaps he does not." The secrets of creation may elude even the gods; mere humans certainly won't do better.
    • [00:12:54] Skeptical thoughts surface in the Upaniṣads too, some suggesting mystical insight is the only way past the limits of human knowledge. The Kena Upaniṣad: "If you think 'I know it well,' perhaps you know ever so little... I do not think that I know it well, but I know not that I do not know."

    The challenge from outside: the Śramaṇa movements¶

    • [00:13:31] The skepticism that really worried Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta wasn't in their own sacred texts. From the moment they arose, the dissident Śramaṇa movements used philosophical arguments to undermine Vedic authority — enough to rattle even the epic poets.
    • [00:13:48] In the Mahābhārata, the god Indra ruefully confesses: "I used to be... a reasoner, a scorner of the Veda. I was pointlessly fond of critical inquiry and the science of argument... I was an unbeliever, skeptical about everything, and, though stupid, I thought myself wise."
    • [00:14:19] The Buddhists, who would soon adopt the skeptic's role themselves, nonetheless preserved condemnations of skeptics. The Buddhist Nikāya describes one Sañjaya who, "by reason of his dullness," resorts to verbal eel-wriggling: "I do not say so. I do not say thus. I do not say otherwise. I do not say no. I deny the denials." Unlike Nancy Reagan's "just say no," Sañjaya's policy was to disavow even the naysaying.

    Three great skeptics and the regress of the pramāṇas¶

    • [00:15:06] Alongside the rise of systematic philosophy in the age of the sūtra came rival forms of skepticism. Three great critical thinkers span the periods: Nāgārjuna (2nd c. CE), Jayarāśi (9th c.), and Śrīharṣa (12th c.).
    • [00:15:31] Strictly, only Jayarāśi is a skeptic in the full sense of withholding all belief. Nāgārjuna was a Buddhist — founder of the Madhyamaka branch — and Śrīharṣa seems to have had underlying metaphysical commitments. But all three deploy skeptical arguments against a cherished idea of their opponents: that we can not only know things but also know that we know them.
    • [00:15:58] It seems obvious that knowledge is "reflective" — if I know there's a giraffe in the room, surely I know that I know it. The three skeptics deny it's obvious at all. Suppose you know there's a giraffe in the room only on a zookeeper friend's say-so; pressed, you'd admit testimony can mislead, so you might still believe it while conceding you aren't sure you know it.
    • [00:16:43] Go in and look for yourself, and the only puzzle left seems to be how they got the giraffe through the front door. But not so fast: how do you know your sense experience gives you knowledge? Eyesight, an Indian philosopher would explain, is a pramāṇa — a way of knowing — and there are others (inference, sometimes testimony); which count depends on your school.
    • [00:17:33] And here comes "the elephant in the room": the viability of the whole idea of a pramāṇa. The skeptic asks how any pramāṇa can be trusted unless we first establish it is reliable. As Nāgārjuna puts it: "If just such objects are established for you by way of a means of knowing, tell me how you establish those means of knowing themselves" — for if they are established by other means of knowing, there is an infinite regress.
    • [00:18:06] Saying "my eyes work, I'm not dreaming" won't satisfy Nāgārjuna — you don't know your eyes work or that you aren't hallucinating; perception can't ratify its own trustworthiness. Appeal instead to inference (my eyes proved dependable before) and you've merely invoked another pramāṇa, whose reliability is no better established than your eyes'. "You can already see why skeptics in India were regarded as very irritating people."

    Jayarāśi: you can't even define knowledge¶

    • [00:19:08] And it gets worse. Jayarāśi challenges the realist epistemologists to define knowledge itself and argues it can't be done: the means of knowing depend on their true definition, and the things known depend on the means of knowing; when the one is absent, how can you talk of the other two as real? Otherwise you might as well talk of "color in the soul" or "pleasure in a pot."
    • [00:19:39] His point isn't quite that you can't know that you're knowing; it's the deeper worry that you can't even know what knowledge is. Before claiming to know anything you must define knowledge — but how could you be satisfied that any definition counts as knowledge unless you already know what knowledge is?
    • [00:20:01] Like Nāgārjuna, Jayarāśi aims at an abstract, highly philosophical target — the epistemologists, not the person with pressing practical concerns about clearing giraffes from her front room. He cheerfully grants that ordinary people have an everyday knowledge that makes life possible: "the worldly path should be followed; with respect to everyday practice, the wise resembles the fool."

    Śrīharṣa against Udayana's counterfactual reasoning¶

    • [00:20:31] A few centuries on, Śrīharṣa (12th c.) responds to a new proposal from the 11th-century Vaiśeṣika Udayana: that knowledge can be reached through what we'd now call counterfactual reasoning. In doubt whether a giraffe is in the next room, I reason: "If there were a giraffe in there, I would smell it; no giraffe-fragrance hangs in the air; therefore the room is giraffe-free." Eminently reasonable — indeed a big part of what reasoning is.
    • [00:21:14] But Śrīharṣa is, predictably, skeptical: counterfactual inferences themselves need justification. I rule out the giraffe only by invoking the rule "if there were a giraffe there, I'd smell it" — and what makes me so certain of that? The regress looms again: every inference seems to call for a further inference to ground it, each no more secure than the last.

    Skepticism that bites: ritual and the supernatural¶

    • [00:21:43] These paralyzing challenges are, in real life, not paralyzing at all. Jayarāśi expected his arguments to leave ordinary everyday knowledge standing; in the European tradition too the skeptics got cheerfully on with life — Sextus Empiricus acted as if he held the beliefs his arguments undercut, and Hume famously dropped his skeptical worries when he left his study.
    • [00:22:14] But the Śramaṇa traditions pressed other skeptical challenges with real practical bite: they questioned the efficacy of Vedic ritual and the reality of the supernatural entities those rituals presuppose. This was no abstract musing about knowledge-in-general but a gauntlet thrown down against the elite of the Brahminical tradition.
    • [00:22:40] The Mīmāṃsā and Nyāya schools rose to it — Mīmāṃsā especially insisting that the Vedas are an indispensable source of vital truths attainable in no other way. The next episode turns to those debates over ritual and the supernatural.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • sūtra and bhāṣya — aphorism-text plus commentary; the form of the systematic schools, contrasted with traditions that challenge the systems' terms.
    • paradox of inquiry — if you already know X there's nothing to inquire into; if you don't know X at all, you can't inquire into it; so inquiry seems impossible.
    • jijñāsā — "desire to know," the word for inquiry; desiring to know presupposes already knowing what you want, hence the paradox.
    • Śabara — Mīmāṃsā commentator; raises the paradox, then answers that inquiry arises from too many conflicting beliefs about dharma, not too few.
    • Kumārila Bhaṭṭa — later Mīmāṃsā writer who restates the paradox in sharpened form.
    • Śaṅkara — 8th-c. Vedāntin; answers the paradox with a common idea of brahman, an etymology ("to grow" → maximally great), and the indubitable direct knowledge of one's own self ("you are that").
    • Nyāya — realist epistemologists who never confront the paradox; for them doubt is partial grasp of an object, a target for further questioning, not paralysis.
    • vyutpatti / nirvacana — semantic/etymological analysis; held to explain why a word fits its thing (e.g. dharma = "that which upholds").
    • pramāṇa — a means or way of knowing (perception, inference, sometimes testimony); which ones count depends on your school.
    • regress of the means of knowing — Nāgārjuna's argument that a pramāṇa can't certify its own reliability, and certifying it by another pramāṇa launches an infinite regress.
    • Nāgārjuna — 2nd-c. CE Buddhist, founder of Madhyamaka; presses the pramāṇa regress against realist epistemology.
    • Jayarāśi — 9th-c. thoroughgoing skeptic; argues knowledge cannot even be defined, yet grants ordinary everyday knowledge.
    • Śrīharṣa — 12th-c. skeptic; turns the regress against Udayana's counterfactual reasoning.
    • Udayana — 11th-c. Vaiśeṣika; proposed counterfactual ("if there were a giraffe I'd smell it") reasoning as a route to knowledge.
    • Sañjaya — skeptic condemned in the Buddhist Nikāya for "eel-wriggling": "I deny the denials."
    • Śramaṇa movements — dissident (Buddhist/Jaina) traditions whose practical skepticism targeted the efficacy of Vedic ritual and the reality of the supernatural, challenging Brahminical authority directly.

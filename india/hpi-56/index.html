    HoPWaG India · Ep 56 — "Who's Pulling Your Strings: Buddhaghosa"¶

    ← Ep 55

    Ep 57 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:19:30 In brief: Buddhaghosa reads the Buddhist doctrine of no-self not as the claim that a person is just a heap of experiences but as a denial of any inner controller — and replaces the "charioteer" model of agency with a marionette, then argues that activity and attention are built into every moment of consciousness, yielding a strikingly modern philosophy of mind that grounds moral responsibility and empathy. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit/Pali terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Gernardin Gennary"; Buddhaghosa ≈ "Buda Gosa / Budagosa / Buddha Gusa / Budagossa"; Visuddhimagga ≈ "Path of Purification"; Anurādhapura ≈ "Anura-Dapura"; Nikāya ≈ "Nikaya/Nicaea"; abhidhamma ≈ "Abidharma"; Madhusūdana Sarasvatī ≈ "Madhusadana Saras Vati"; Dignāga ≈ "Dignaga"; Theravāda ≈ "Theravada"; Kṛṣṇa ≈ "Krishna"). 📖 In the book: this episode is ch. 41 of Classical Indian Philosophy (Adamson & Ganeri, OUP 2019). Its sources & a reading list are under Further reading below.

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Buddhaghosa — the Buddhist philosopher whose theory of agency and mind drives the episode; author of the Visuddhimagga ("Path of Purification") [00:01:18].
    • The Visuddhimagga — Buddhaghosa's independent systematic treatise and training manual for the Buddhist path [00:02:49].
    • Kṛṣṇa in the Bhagavad-Gītā — invoked as the rival "divine puppet-master" model of agency, with the later commentator Madhusūdana Sarasvatī [00:04:44].
    • Dignāga — recalled for his idea of perception prior to conceptual construction [00:16:06].

    Who's pulling your strings? The problem of self-control¶

    • [00:00:28] Nobody likes to be pushed around. We'd like to think that when we do things it's because we chose to, and could have chosen otherwise. If I speed up while walking, it's because I decided to go faster; if I look down, it's because I want to watch my step; and even when I think about a problem without moving at all — even one I'd rather not think about — isn't that also something I am doing myself?
    • [00:00:55] It seems natural, even inevitable, to frame this in terms of control. It's like a flying drone whose movements are directed from the ground by a pilot — except that here the thing doing the controlling and the thing being controlled are both inside one person. On this picture, self-control is just a more intense version of the ordinary causal relationship involved in remote control.

    Buddhaghosa: no-self as the denial of a controller¶

    • [00:01:18] For the Buddhist philosopher Buddhaghosa, this would be a huge mistake. He thinks the whole point of the Buddha's refusal to acknowledge a self — to allow that there is anything that can be called "I" — is precisely a denial of the controller self.
    • [00:01:34] This is a new perspective within the tradition. Earlier Buddhists understood the theory of no-self to mean that the person is just a stream or heap of individual experiences and feelings. Buddhaghosa instead insists that the doctrine of no-self is about our capacity for agency.

    A life of will, a doctrine of no will: Buddhaghosa's biography¶

    • [00:01:47] Though born in India and, by all accounts, thoroughly educated in the various Indian philosophical systems, Buddhaghosa decided that the true Buddhist teachings were best preserved on the island of Sri Lanka. There the teachings were still recorded and studied in the actual language of the Buddha, Pali, rather than Sanskrit, the language of elite discourse.

    From the book — ch. 41

    The book dates his birth more precisely: Buddhaghosa was born in fifth-century India (the spoken episode just says "born in India").

    • [00:02:13] So Buddhaghosa travelled to the great monastery of Anurādhapura and embarked on an enormously thorough study of the Buddhist canon — both the Nikāya and abhidhamma treatises — and all the commentaries written over centuries by a series of talented Sinhala philosophers.
    • [00:02:31] So impressed were the monks that they charged him with writing definitive explanations that would organize and order the teachings. With a team of assistants and apprentices, he did exactly that, in a monumental project of textual production.
    • [00:02:49] For good measure he also composed an independent treatise, the Visuddhimagga ("Path of Purification"), which both presents the doctrine systematically and serves as a training manual for aspirants on the Buddhist path.
    • [00:02:58] So Buddhaghosa was plainly a willful, motivated individual. Yet when it came to describing what it means to be willful and motivated, he had no truck with the idea that each of us is or has a self — an origin of willed directives.

    Against the charioteer: the marionette metaphor¶

    • [00:03:14] A deeply entrenched model encourages us to think of the self along the lines of a charioteer who controls his horses from the chariot's platform. In this picture of human agency Buddhaghosa finds only "metaphysical magic." Time and again he rejects the idea that there is something inside which controls our bodily movements or our psychological processes. The self of the sectarians, he likes to say, "just does not exist."
    • [00:03:45] The best way to counter the hold a metaphor has on the imagination is to replace it with another. To combat the charioteer, he compares the human being to a mechanical doll / marionette whose limbs, torso and head are held together by strings. He writes: "Just as a mechanical doll is empty, soulless, and undirected, and while it walks and stands merely through the combination of strings and wood, yet it seems as if it had direction and occupation, so too this minded body is empty, soulless, and undirected… yet it seems as if it had direction and occupation."

    Living dangerously: the divine puppet-master objection¶

    • [00:04:26] Buddhaghosa is living dangerously, since this metaphor can easily work against him. If we are just marionettes, surely someone must be pulling our strings — and who could that be other than a divine agency, a being with its hands on each person's control units?
    • [00:04:44] That is, in fact, exactly the picture of agency encouraged by a thinker already met in this series: Kṛṣṇa in the Bhagavad-Gītā. "Oh, Arjuna," Kṛṣṇa says, "the Lord resides in the heart area of all beings, making every being revolve through his magical power as if they were mounted on a machine."
    • [00:05:05] The later commentator Madhusūdana Sarasvatī identified that machine as precisely "our friend the marionette": the Lord is "just like a magician who causes completely non-independent wooden human forms to revolve, seated on a machine… moved by a rope."

    Why Buddhaghosa is not committed to automata¶

    • [00:05:26] But it cannot possibly be Buddhaghosa's view that human beings — or "minded bodies," as Buddhists prefer to call us — are automata controlled from the outside. Not just because he doesn't believe in a divine agency (he certainly doesn't), nor only because in the Nikāya the Buddha himself is reported to speak of "self-doing" in the performance of stepping forward or back.
    • [00:05:56] More fundamentally, it is because of the very nature of ethics on the Buddhist view. It is central to Buddhist ethics that the moral value of an action is constrained by the intention with which it is performed. If actions were merely the result of some divine puppet-master, then nobody could be praised or blamed for their intentions, since those intentions would play no causal role in what they do.
    • [00:06:17] So in deploying the risky marionette metaphor, Buddhaghosa simply wants to push back against the insidious grip of the charioteer metaphor, which leads us to think there must be some inner controller.

    A better simile: the self-driving car¶

    • [00:06:27] If Buddhaghosa lived today, Adamson suggests, he might compare humans to the self-driving cars we're promised will soon fill the streets. Like a self-driving car, the human being is a complex mechanism containing a variety of instruments: some detect what is in the environment, some monitor the functioning of the mechanism itself, others search for objectives or destinations and calculate the best ways of reaching them.
    • [00:06:57] That is the difference between a self-driving car and a remote-controlled drone, whose operator is external — and also the difference between a self-driving car and an ordinary car, or a chariot, "which is like a drone whose operator happens to be on board." The control is internal and distributed, not lodged in an onboard pilot.

    Turning the old rope metaphor on its head¶

    • [00:07:10] Buddhaghosa develops his alternative by arguing that all consciousness is already active — that every conscious mental state is an expression of agency. He does this by inverting a more ancient Buddhist description of the mind.
    • [00:07:28] In that older description, the flow of mental life was a rope of many interwoven strands: one strand the series of sensory perceptions; another the series of feelings and affective reactions; another habits and dispositions; still another our concepts; all woven together with a final strand of the body's physical states. Together they produce a human being — a rope stretched across time. Within this complex appear "moments of intention and choice," which were said to give the whole rope its agency and direction.
    • [00:08:08] Since this picture was drawn by Buddhists, it gives no place to a unitary inner self — but it still suggests control emanates from discrete elements within the person, namely the volitions woven into it. Instead, Buddhaghosa insists that control and activity are built into every single moment of conscious experience. Our awareness is built up out of many activities which together produce the mindedness of the complex minded-body that is the human being.

    The blind man and the lame man¶

    • [00:08:38] Buddhaghosa offers a vivid simile: imagine an odd couple — someone blind but able to walk, with a sighted but lame person sitting on the first person's shoulders. Each deliberate action, such as moving in a chosen direction, is the joint product of several capacities working together.

    From the book — ch. 41

    The book attributes this simile: Buddhaghosa is recalling the blind-and-lame image from the Sāṃkhya-kārikā (the Sāṃkhya school covered earlier in the series), not coining it himself.

    • [00:09:02] Conscious awareness is therefore no passive response to the environment but "the activity of the mind bending itself onto the world." There is an interplay between mind and body, and between the minded-body and its worldly environment.

    Attention as a window frame: the components of experience¶

    • [00:09:13] Buddhaghosa identifies several kinds of activity that make up experience. There is awareness, which brings us into touch with our environment in a way we are not when asleep. We can also reach out or direct attention to specific objects — which is what gives thought an object, a goal, an orientation. Once something comes to our attention we can evaluate it, judging it worth pursuing or avoiding, or classifying it under a category.
    • [00:09:39] For Buddhaghosa, being minded is being attentive: we direct ourselves toward certain things and not others. Attention is like a window frame — it sets a boundary to what we see, though we can focus on particular features within that frame. Experience is thus not passive being-affected but active engagement.
    • [00:10:03] And this active engagement is not the job of one part of the mind "in charge." We need no self as a top-down source of motivation, intention, evaluation or awareness. All these features are already present in the various components of mental activity, built in from the ground up.

    The objection from passivity: noise and pain¶

    • [00:10:28] As often in philosophy, the account works better for some examples than others. It sounds convincing for reading a book: you decide to read up on Buddhism, go to the library, find the volume, sit, and attend to a sentence — a life full of activity.
    • [00:10:51] But aren't there cases where mental life seems passive, at the mercy of the outside world? A loud noise may force itself on your attention — a car backfiring outside the library, making you look up. Or pain: on a meditative walk you step on a thorn. Here active attention seems to come in only secondarily — apparently you involuntarily become aware of the noise or pain, and only then direct your attention, thinking "what was that?" or "boy, that hurt."
    • [00:11:26] Buddhaghosa denies this. He thinks the mind must already be actively involved for us to become aware of the distracting noise or the stabbing pain at all.

    Three analogies for backstage activity¶

    • [00:11:40] The mango. A man asleep under a mango tree is woken by the sound of a falling mango; he grabs it, squeezes it, smells it, and finally eats it. He sees the mango "only because he heard it first" — awareness often requires coordination between the senses.
    • [00:11:54] The king and the gift. A king sits contentedly inside his palace. There's a knock at the gate; a villager has come bearing a gift. The doorkeeper is deaf and doesn't hear it, but the king's attendant does and instructs the doorkeeper to open the gate, letting the gift reach the king. Consciousness is the king receiving and enjoying the gift; sophisticated mental activity has already unfolded in the background.
    • [00:12:17] The spider. The unperturbed mind is like a spider nestled in the centre of its web. An insect touches the web, sending vibrations to the centre; detecting them, the spider shoots off to suck out its prey's juices. Here the web is "exploited twice" — first to transmit the quiver of the insect's impact, then to transport the spider out to it. So a single sense modality can do double duty: sounding a subliminal alert that a stimulus is present, and then reaching out to it in conscious awareness.

    Revising the doctrine of the six senses¶

    • [00:13:49] All this helps Buddhaghosa to a major revision of ancient Buddhist theory. Traditionally there were held to be six sense faculties, not five: besides sight, taste, touch, smell and hearing, there is a faculty called mind, whose job is to perceive what goes on in our heads. Since every faculty has its own domain of objects (sight grasps colours), a sixth faculty would require inner mental objects to grasp.
    • [00:14:21] Earlier Buddhists also held that each faculty has its own physical basis — the eye for vision — and so assumed a physical basis for the sixth sense, the "heart base" (a term, Adamson jokes, evoking "the headquarters of a group of militant cardiologists").
    • [00:14:42] Buddhaghosa offers a wholesale revision: the word "mind" cannot be reduced to an additional sense faculty. Rather it names a range of functions associated with sensory perception — four types in all.
    • [00:15:00] Orientation. The first role of mind is like the king's attendant: mental activity is needed to get the senses to orient toward something — as when you turn back from a loud noise to focus on your book, or stop watching television to listen to your spouse (Adamson advises against the reverse). In this role the mind is not a distinct sixth perception but spans all five senses, embedded in their normal functioning.
    • [00:15:54] Bare receiving. The basic reception of a sensory stimulus into perception, "as yet involving no attempt to classify." Here we may recall Dignāga's idea of perceptions that have not yet received the constructions placed on them at the level of concepts.
    • [00:16:10] Classification. "This thing I am seeing is oval in shape," or "is a mango."
    • [00:16:24] Working memory. A further role resembling the modern psychologist's notion of working memory: the mind can "run across" the processed perceptions from the various senses, putting them in order so we can have a unified awareness in consciousness. In this last capacity the mind is more like a workspace than an inner sixth sense.

    A modern philosopher — but always with an eye on liberation¶

    • [00:16:42] This is a formidably sophisticated account of the mind. Given the proliferation of distinctions, the compelling analogies and examples, and the fierce critique of rivals, Buddhaghosa "wouldn't be out of place in a modern-day analytic philosophy department," and would hold his own there.
    • [00:17:11] Nonetheless, as always in the Indian tradition, such technical theories were advanced with an eye on how to live and how to be liberated. Some account of motivation was needed to preserve our intuitions about moral praise and blame.
    • [00:17:28] There is a further ethical payoff. Buddhaghosa's account could have emerged from reflection on his own mind, but we can also turn our notice to other people. For Buddhaghosa those others have no selves — yet that does not make them unworthy of attention. On the contrary, each human is a unique complex of mental and bodily activities with its own internal interactions and characteristics.
    • [00:17:57] Understanding this should lead to the development of empathy: one conscious creature focuses its attention on another and becomes aware of that other's particular traits and states of mind. Adamson recommends "trying this at home" and in society at large — for what is a flourishing community but humans empathizing with one another, "quite literally keeping each other in mind," as they symbiotically interact with a shared environment?

    Where we go next¶

    • [00:18:24] This aspect of Buddhaghosa's thought, and his philosophy as a whole, was dispersed widely through the world of Theravāda Buddhism, which today encompasses not just Sri Lanka but much of South and Southeast Asia — shaping ideas about human nature and community across a vast share of the global population. That larger story the series won't try to tell in detail.
    • [00:18:49] In fact the tour through Indian thought is "slowly coming to an end." It will conclude with several broader issues: the philosophy of animals in ancient India, and the possibility of influence between ancient European and ancient Indian thought.
    • [00:19:04] Next time kicks off these general-topic episodes with a theme readily associated with Indian culture, though not necessarily with philosophy: the ritual practices known as Tantra — practices that span the divide between the Vedic schools, Buddhism and Jainism, much as Buddhaghosa's "mind" spans the divide between sight, hearing and the other senses. "So make it your mantra to join us."

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Buddhaghosa — India-born Buddhist philosopher who settled at Anurādhapura in Sri Lanka; reinterprets no-self as the denial of an inner controller and treats agency/attention as built into every moment of consciousness.
    • no-self — the Buddhist doctrine that there is no "I"; earlier read as "person = a heap/stream of experiences," but for Buddhaghosa it specifically denies a controlling self that directs our actions.
    • minded body — the Buddhists' preferred term for a human being: "empty, soulless, and undirected," yet seeming to have direction and occupation.
    • Visuddhimagga ("Path of Purification") — Buddhaghosa's independent systematic treatise and training manual for aspirants on the Buddhist path.
    • Pali — the actual language of the Buddha, in which the teachings were preserved in Sri Lanka, contrasted with elite Sanskrit.
    • Anurādhapura — the great Sri Lankan monastery where Buddhaghosa studied the canon and its Sinhala commentaries.
    • Nikāya & abhidhamma — the two bodies of Buddhist canonical treatises Buddhaghosa studied; the Nikāya reports the Buddha speaking of "self-doing."
    • charioteer metaphor — the entrenched (rejected) model of the self as a driver controlling the body's "horses" from within; Buddhaghosa sees in it only "metaphysical magic."
    • marionette / mechanical doll — Buddhaghosa's counter-metaphor: a strung doll that moves with apparent purpose though empty and undirected.
    • Kṛṣṇa / Bhagavad-Gītā / Madhusūdana Sarasvatī — the rival "divine puppet-master" picture, where the Lord makes beings revolve "as if mounted on a machine"; the commentator Madhusūdana glosses that machine as the marionette moved by a rope.
    • intention (and moral responsibility) — Buddhist ethics ties an action's moral worth to its intention, which is why we cannot be mere externally-controlled automata.
    • rope of strands — the older Buddhist image of mental life as interwoven strands (perceptions, feelings, dispositions, concepts, body) with volition supplying agency; Buddhaghosa turns it on its head.
    • attention as a window frame — being minded is being attentive: directing oneself toward some things rather than others, within a bounded but focusable field.
    • the six senses / "heart base" — the traditional doctrine of a sixth "mind" faculty with its own objects and bodily seat; Buddhaghosa rejects this, recasting "mind" as four functions (orientation, bare receiving, classification, working memory) spread across the five senses.
    • Dignāga — recalled for the idea of perception prior to conceptual construction, paralleling Buddhaghosa's stage of "bare receiving."
    • empathy — the ethical upshot: since each selfless human is a unique complex of activities, attending to others should breed empathy and flourishing community ("keeping each other in mind").
    • Theravāda Buddhism — the tradition through which Buddhaghosa's thought spread across South and Southeast Asia.
    • Tantra — the ritual practices spanning Vedic, Buddhist and Jain schools, previewed as the next episode's topic.

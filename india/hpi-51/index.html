    HoPWaG India · Ep 51 — "Change of Mind: Vasubandhu and Yogācāra Buddhism"¶

    ← Ep 50

    Ep 52 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:43 In brief: The two-act career of Vasubandhu (c. 400 CE) used as a tour through the Buddhist schools: first the Abhidharma "presentist" who argues only the present is real, then — converted to Mahāyāna by his half-brother Asaṅga — the co-founder of Yogācāra, the idealist "consciousness-only" school that dissolves even the momentary external dharmas into mind.     Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Vasubandhu — Buddhist thinker, c. 400 CE; whose one career "ties together numerous strands of the ancient Buddhist tradition," moving from Abhidharma controversialist to Yogācāra idealist [00:01:06].
    • Asaṅga — Vasubandhu's older half-brother, who converts him to the Mahāyāna; co-founder with him of Yogācāra [00:01:18].
    • Yogācāra (also Vijñānavāda, "the teaching of consciousness") — the idealist branch of Mahāyāna founded by the brothers [00:01:41].
    • Sarvāstivāda / Vaibhāṣika — the Abhidharma school the early Vasubandhu attacks; holds all dharmas (past, present, future) are real [00:04:37].
    • Nāgārjuna and the Madhyamaka — the other wing of Mahāyāna, the comparison-point for placing Yogācāra [00:08:24].

    Big Brother, and the career of Vasubandhu¶

    • [00:00:36] Today's episode, "Change of Mind: Vasubandhu and Yogācāra Buddhism." Adamson opens with Orwell's 1984 and its sinister repurposing of "Big Brother": the totalitarians play on the fact that big brothers are objects of love and admiration — you fight with your older brother but also look up to him, hoping to benefit from his protection, experience, and know-how.
    • [00:01:06] This may explain something about the intellectual career of Vasubandhu, a Buddhist thinker who lived around 400 CE. By legend he was converted to the Mahāyāna branch of Buddhism through the good offices of his own older half-brother, Asaṅga.
    • [00:01:23] Before that, Vasubandhu had been a controversialist within the Abhidharma branch, attacking rival Abhidharmikas for their views on past and future reality. Once brought around to the Mahāyāna way of seeing things, he joined Asaṅga as co-founder of one branch within Mahāyāna, Yogācāra — often seen as a kind of idealist version of Buddhism that rejects even the minimal concessions to extra-mental reality made by the Abhidharma Buddhists.
    • [00:01:57] So this one man's career ties together numerous strands of the ancient tradition. The welter of schools and sub-schools is confusing — especially since some have more than one name: Yogācāra is also known as the Vijñānavāda, "the teaching of consciousness," for reasons that will become clear.

    One question to organize the schools: continuity¶

    • [00:02:19] The branches were explained back in episode 43, but Adamson re-frames them around one central philosophical question debated between the schools: how do we account for the apparent continuity of things in the world — inanimate objects, animate beings, or our own selves?
    • [00:02:43] Everyday common sense gives a straightforward answer: these things seem continuously real because they are real. Suppose I see my big brother, the art historian, reading an art-history book. The book in his hand, my brother, and I myself are all substances that persist through time. We also interact — I look over his shoulder; he asks whether I shouldn't have a philosophy book to work through. Such causal interactions even cross time: the next day I remember what he was reading and how many nice pictures it had.
    • [00:03:18] The Buddhists, of course, reject this commonsensical view. Belief in the enduring self is indelibly linked to suffering, so we must give it up to achieve liberation.
    • [00:03:35] More generally, per Abhidharma teaching, continuous substances have only apparent reality. What is really real are atomic, momentary qualities — the so-called dharmas. When we seem to see a book or a brother stably existing from moment to moment, we are assembling fleeting elements into a whole, and that whole has only conventional reality — like the famous chariot that is no more than the sum of its parts.

    The Sarvāstivāda answer: past and future are real too¶

    • [00:03:59] Yet the Buddhists still need to explain the causal relations between apparent things — especially hard for causation across time. When my only-conventionally-real brother tosses his conventionally-real book onto the conventionally-real table and it bangs (art-history books being impressively large), how do the elemental features I register as his body and its throwing motion produce the noise a second later?
    • [00:04:31] In his early career Vasubandhu sets out to refute a group of Buddhists who gave a surprising answer. To maximize confusion this school also has two names — the Sarvāstivāda, also known as Vaibhāṣika. Like other Abhidharmikas they deny the persistent reality of objects, which are nothing but fictitious combinations of momentary dharmas.
    • [00:04:51] However, for them all the dharmas are real — whether past, present, or future. This guarantees causation across time: if a now-past dharma were non-existent, it could not give rise to a present dharma.
    • [00:05:11] Here Vasubandhu enters: he studied Sarvāstivāda and wrote a treatise setting out its doctrines in verses — then subjected those same teachings to a thorough critique in a commentary on his own verses.

    From the book — ch. 40

    The book names this self-commentary: the Abhidharmakośabhāṣya — the "commentary on his own verses" that carries the critique. (The spoken episode leaves it unnamed.)

    Vasubandhu the "presentist": only the present is real¶

    • [00:05:31] Vasubandhu calls his own version of Abhidharma Sautrāntika, meaning that it follows the Buddhist sūtras. Like modern philosophers who call themselves presentists, he insists only present things are real. That sounds like a refreshing concession to common sense in the midst of an intra-Buddhist debate — but he is not merely relying on common sense; he has arguments.
    • [00:06:01] From his point of view the Sarvāstivāda theory ironically sounds like a form of eternalism: if past, present, and future things are all permanently real, that is hardly a suitable doctrine for Buddhists. Besides, if all these things are real, why are only some of them present and currently active? Unless we can explain that, we can't account for the fact that you can only read a history-of-philosophy book presently in your hands — not one still in the future, or already lost to history.
    • [00:06:28] The Sarvāstivādins may reply that past things still have present effects — memories of previous events, or the tossed book that bangs only now when it lands. But Vasubandhu can explain how past things keep having effects even once they stop existing (which, being instantaneous, they always do immediately): there is a causal continuum from one dharma to the next, the throwing-motion causing further motion through the air, the chain culminating in a bang.
    • [00:07:05] Or — a case that interests him more — past actions cause present karmic fruits indirectly, through an unbroken chain of momentary dharmas. He compares this to the way a seed that no longer exists has given rise to a sprout, then a mature plant, then the flower we see now.
    • [00:07:24] The same analysis applies to the apparent self that initiated a previous action and now reaps its karmic reward or punishment. There is no real enduring self, but the elements we falsely take to constitute it are part of a chain of causal influence passing second to second — like a flame being passed from one candle to another.

    The conversion: from Abhidharma to Mahāyāna¶

    • [00:07:47] Per the traditional story, Vasubandhu wrote this defence of Sautrāntika Abhidharma — attacking Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma — before he came over to Mahāyāna under his brother Asaṅga's influence. (If you're struggling to keep up, don't worry: all these people and schools were merely conventional realities anyway.)
    • [00:08:05] Once converted, Vasubandhu wrote a series of powerfully argumentative works attacking the Abhidharma approach as a whole. This is not our first contact with Mahāyāna: that branch includes the Madhyamaka philosophy, ably if perplexingly represented by Nāgārjuna. Asaṅga and Vasubandhu may have seen themselves as defending something like Nāgārjuna's position, "to the extent that he had a position."
    • [00:08:36] The brothers' approach is prefigured in a text called the Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra (the "Sūtra of Profound Secrets"), which offers a third turning of the wheel of Buddhist doctrine, claiming to bring together the teachings of the first two wheels — Abhidharma and (the earlier) Mahāyāna.

    Dharmas themselves become mental constructions¶

    • [00:08:59] This later Vasubandhu shares with Nāgārjuna a frankly critical approach to the dharma-theory of Abhidharma. He does to the dharmas exactly what Abhidharma had done to everyday objects: just as they rejected books and brothers as figments of the mind, now Vasubandhu suggests that even the momentary elemental dharmas are mental constructions.
    • [00:09:17] This seems a radical shift from his earlier Sautrāntika doctrine, but it actually retains something of it. Before, he insisted past and future things are unreal — only the present phenomena, both of the world outside us and of mental life, exist. Now he realizes he can do without the external phenomena while retaining the internal, mental ones. Things outside us are merely apparent — comparable to the hair-like images seen by someone with an eye disease. Since everything is a construction of the mind, mental reality is the only reality there is.
    • [00:09:52] How does he justify this breathtaking move? As an application of a principle of philosophical economy already used by other Buddhists: if we can explain our experiences without recognizing something as real, we should eliminate it from our metaphysics. The Abhidharmikas had eliminated chariots, books, and brothers; the earlier Vasubandhu had shown we can do without past and future reality; now the later Vasubandhu contends we don't need present external reality either.

    Affected by unreal things: the reductionist, not the skeptic¶

    • [00:10:29] He argues mostly by answering objections meant to force him to accept that something is externally existent. His hypothetical opponent complains: if external things are unreal, they could have no effect on us.
    • [00:10:43] Vasubandhu rejoins that people are affected by unreal things — in dreams and hallucinations, or in images the damned see in hell. The point is not that "life is but a dream": dreaming is itself a distinctive mental state, as is being in a faint, so he need not appeal to the idea that we can't tell whether we're awake.
    • [00:11:09] Rather, his point is that our mental experiences can arise without any real external stimulus. His approach is not skeptical but reductionist: mental events are caused, and in fact can only be caused, by other mental events. An external dharma could never be the thing I am perceiving right now, because the dharma is only momentary and would have ceased existing by the time I register awareness of it.
    • [00:11:31] Instead there is a stream of mental experience, which Vasubandhu calls citta, manas, or vijñāna — words for mind or consciousness, for him basically equivalent. Hence Yogācāra's alternative name, the Vijñānavāda: the teaching of consciousness, the doctrine of mind/consciousness only — cittamātra.

    The storehouse consciousness¶

    • [00:11:55] Vasubandhu still holds his earlier idea of a chain of causality, but now the chain consists only of mental events: everything about our current mental life results from previous mental events. A past event may take time to make itself felt in current awareness — a beautiful botanical metaphor: like dye pulled in through the stem of a growing flower, eventually coloring the petals as they unfold.
    • [00:12:32] At a more technical level he invokes the storehouse consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna), already proposed in the Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra. Mental events are like currents coming to the surface of a stream, or seeds planted earlier finally coming to fruit. These metaphors capture how we can direct attention to impressions received in past moments.
    • [00:12:55] Consider a childhood memory — the time your big brother defended you from a bully on the playground. Though not continuously remembered, the memory remains in storage, available as possible content for consciousness.
    • [00:13:13] Vasubandhu is sensitive to the fact that the storehouse-consciousness idea is not found in the Buddhist scriptures — but shrugs it off: plenty written about the Buddha has been lost, and perhaps a reference to this doctrine went missing too.

    Still a Buddhist: liberation from suffering through non-duality¶

    • [00:13:28] Any doubts about his Buddhist motivation are dispelled by his frequent references to liberation from suffering. The reductionist metaphysics of Buddhism always aimed at eliminating suffering: suffering comes from attachment, and if we dispel belief in the reality of things we can wean ourselves off attachment to them — though this also requires extensive spiritual training, not just following arguments to their conclusion.
    • [00:14:04] These features are alive in Yogācāra, whose very name comes from its emphasis on spiritual practices, or yoga — meditation and mindfulness.
    • [00:14:13] Vasubandhu names the attitude that gives rise to attachment: duality. We suffer because we contrast ourselves as subjects against the objects of perception and desire. In reality this contrast is an illusion. Common sense is wrong to suppose there is a world of persisting objects outside us — and Abhidharma Buddhism does not go far enough, since it still accepts external objects of awareness, namely the dharmas.
    • [00:14:44] Yogācāra takes a further step — or two. First, for Vasubandhu the objects of the mind are internal to the mind, themselves part of mental life. Then comes the still more challenging step: giving up even a mental version of the subject–object contrast. The correct non-dual attitude is one in which the mind no longer treats anything as an object of its awareness. So fundamental is this undermining of everything we take ourselves to know that Vasubandhu calls his teaching of non-duality a "revolution at the basis."

    The three natures (svabhāva)¶

    • [00:15:19] The revolution of this third wheel has, appropriately, three stages, which Vasubandhu sets out: What is it that appears? A construction of the non-existent. How does it appear? Through dualities. What is its non-existence? A state of non-existent duality. He frames these as the three kinds of independent nature, or svabhāva.
    • [00:15:51] First, an entirely fictive nature — fabricated or constructed by the mind — the natures of the apparent things in our external environment; compared to an elephant conjured out of wooden props by a magician's sleight of hand.
    • [00:16:09] Second, an interdependent nature: subject and object of awareness depending on one another — for example, an act of sight and that which is seen.
    • [00:16:22] Third and finally, an awareness that has given up duality and become perfected or fulfilled — even the mind is not an object for itself. At this stage one is finally liberated from suffering by being liberated from illusion.

    From the book — ch. 40

    The book attaches the Sanskrit technical name to each of the three natures: the fictive/constructed nature is parikalpita, the interdependent one paratantra, and the perfected one pariniṣpanna.

    Where Yogācāra sits: alongside and against Madhyamaka¶

    • [00:16:33] That Vasubandhu sets out his three stages using the familiar word svabhāva is significant: we saw this word in Nāgārjuna, who targeted the very notion in his critique. This raises the question of where Yogācāra sits in the history of Buddhism.
    • [00:16:53] It is grouped with Nāgārjuna's middle way (Madhyamaka) under the broader heading of Mahāyāna, and seems to have much in common with it. Yogācāra too styles itself a middle way, and for the same reason Nāgārjuna gave: it tries to steer between the extremes of nihilism and eternalism (reification) — banishing all things as non-existent, on one side, and taking all things to have stable independent reality, on the other.
    • [00:17:28] Both Mahāyāna traditions express their middle way as an acknowledgement of the emptiness of things. For Nāgārjuna this was the upshot of his skeptical attack on substance, motion, and perception; for Yogācāra it is the upshot of the critique of subject–object duality in consciousness. As Asaṅga puts it: "the non-existence of duality is indeed the existence of non-existence — this is the definition of emptiness."
    • [00:17:52] Nonetheless, the later tradition saw disagreement and even harsh polemic between the two schools. A 6th-century Madhyamaka thinker, Bhāviveka, argues that Yogācāra falls into both extremes on either side of the correct middle way:
    • Where Madhyamaka grants the legitimacy of conventional attitudes toward reality, Yogācāra rejects conventional beliefs entirely — which Bhāviveka counts as a form of nihilism.
    • On the other hand, Yogācāra is reificationist because it accepts the genuine reality of some phenomena — those occurring in the mind.
    • [00:18:37] Bhāviveka has a good point. Vasubandhu does aim to expose the fictionality of everyday objects, so conventional reality seems completely undermined in his philosophy. And even if Yogācāra's mental forms are empty, they constitute a reality that is mental — and Nāgārjuna would presumably not hesitate to attack this mental version of reality as itself empty.

    Idealism — and beyond it¶

    • [00:19:08] Adamson closes on yet another name modern scholars give Yogācāra: idealism. Vasubandhu makes reality exclusively mental — constituted by nothing but presently-occurrent mental events caused by earlier mental events — so Yogācāra certainly seems to merit the name; it may be one of the earliest forms of idealism in the whole history of philosophy.
    • [00:19:37] But calling Vasubandhu an idealist may underestimate his radicalism. Remember that the third, perfected nature is not just one in which we realize all the objects of our mental activity are themselves mental — it is one in which there is no duality between mind and its objects at all. It is hard, perhaps impossible, to say what lies beyond mental activity structured dualistically; but whatever that reality is, it does not seem to consist of ideas. If Vasubandhu is an idealist, his idealism is "only the last step but one on the path to enlightenment."
    • [00:20:13] Likewise, this is not the last step in the journey through Yogācāra. Next time: another major figure of the school, Dignāga, active in the early 6th century, who continues the "revolution at the basis" with a profound critique of the idea that we have knowledge of external objects. "Remember, Big Brother is watching — or rather listening, and so should you."

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Vasubandhu — Buddhist thinker, c. 400 CE; his two-phase career (Abhidharma "presentist" → Yogācāra idealist) frames the whole episode.
    • Asaṅga — Vasubandhu's older half-brother; converts him to Mahāyāna and co-founds Yogācāra with him; coiner of the quoted definition of emptiness as "the existence of non-existence."
    • Yogācāra — the Mahāyāna branch the brothers found; "consciousness-only" idealism; named for its emphasis on yoga (meditation, mindfulness).
    • Vijñānavāda — alternative name for Yogācāra: "the teaching of consciousness."
    • Abhidharma — the broader analytic Buddhist tradition that reduces ordinary objects to momentary dharmas; both Sarvāstivāda and Sautrāntika fall under it.
    • dharmas — the atomic, momentary qualities that are "really real," out of which the merely conventional whole (book, brother, self) is assembled.
    • conventional vs. real — persisting substances (chariot, book, self) have only conventional reality; only the momentary elements are real.
    • Sarvāstivāda / Vaibhāṣika — Abhidharma school the early Vasubandhu refutes; holds all dharmas (past, present, future) are real, to secure causation across time — which Vasubandhu charges sounds like eternalism.
    • Sautrāntika — Vasubandhu's own early "presentist" position: only present things are real; past effects persist via an unbroken causal chain of dharmas (seed → sprout → flower; flame passed candle to candle).
    • karma — past actions cause present karmic fruits indirectly, through the momentary causal chain; no enduring self underlies it.
    • citta / manas / vijñāna — words for mind/consciousness, for Vasubandhu basically equivalent; the stream of mental experience.
    • cittamātra — "consciousness-only / mind-only"; the doctrine that mental reality is the only reality.
    • ālaya-vijñāna (storehouse consciousness) — the repository where past impressions are stored and made available to later awareness; from the Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra; not in the older scriptures.
    • Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra ("Sūtra of Profound Secrets") — text prefiguring Yogācāra; offers a "third turning of the wheel" uniting Abhidharma and earlier Mahāyāna.
    • svabhāva (independent / own nature) — Vasubandhu's "three natures": fictive/constructed (the magician's wooden elephant), interdependent (subject and object of an act of sight), and perfected/non-dual; the same term Nāgārjuna had targeted.
    • non-duality / "revolution at the basis" — the abolition of the subject–object contrast; the perfected stage where the mind treats nothing as its object; the cure for attachment and suffering.
    • emptiness — shared upshot of both Mahāyāna wings: for Nāgārjuna from the critique of substance/motion/perception, for Yogācāra from the critique of subject–object duality.
    • Nāgārjuna / Madhyamaka — the other Mahāyāna wing, a "middle way" between nihilism and eternalism; comparison-point and later polemical rival of Yogācāra.
    • Bhāviveka — 6th-century Madhyamaka critic who charges Yogācāra with falling into both extremes — nihilism (rejecting conventional reality) and reification (granting real mental phenomena).
    • idealism — modern label for Yogācāra; apt, but possibly underselling Vasubandhu, whose perfected non-dual reality lies beyond even ideas.
    • Dignāga — early-6th-century Yogācāra figure, subject of the next episode; critic of our supposed knowledge of external objects.

    HoPWaG India · Ep 31 — "Where There's Smoke There's Fire: Gautama's Nyāya-Sūtra"¶

    ← Ep 30

    Ep 32 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:21:17 In brief: An introduction to Nyāya, the Indian school of logic and epistemology: its founding text the Nyāya-Sūtra and Vātsyāyana's commentary, its four pramāṇas (means of knowledge), its "default trust" answer to Buddhist skepticism, its theory of honest vs. tricky debate, and its five-limbed inference scheme — "where there's smoke, there's fire." Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gannery"; Nyāya ≈ "Nayaya/Niyaya"; Nyāya-Sūtra ≈ "Nayaya Sutra"; Gautama/Akṣapāda ≈ "Akhapada"; Vaiśeṣika ≈ "Vyusheshachika/by Sheshika"; Vātsyāyana ≈ "Vatsyayana/Batzayana/Butz Yayana"; Uddyotakara ≈ "Ujjjutakara/Udiotakara"; Vācaspati Miśra ≈ "Vachaspati Mishra"; anvīkṣikī ≈ "anvikshiki"; Kauṭilya ≈ "Kautilya"; Arthaśāstra ≈ "Atashastra"; Caraka Saṃhitā ≈ "Shadaka's Samita"; pramāṇa ≈ "pramana"; prameya ≈ "pramaya"; Pāṇini ≈ "Ponony"; Nāgārjuna ≈ "Nagarjuna"; Madhyamaka ≈ "Madyamika"; prasaṅga ≈ "Pasanga"; bhāṣya ≈ "Basha").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Nyāya — the school specializing in logic and epistemology; one of the final pair of post-Vedic schools [00:01:40].
    • Vaiśeṣika — its partner school, specializing in metaphysics [00:01:42].
    • Gautama (Akṣapāda) — the man to whom the foundational Nyāya-Sūtra is ascribed [00:03:48].
    • The Nyāya-Sūtra — the canonical sūtras (1st–2nd c. AD), with Vātsyāyana's bhāṣya commentary (c. 450) and Uddyotakara's later sub-commentary (late 6th c.) [00:03:22].
    • Nāgārjuna — the Buddhist master of pure skeptical debate, posed as Nyāya's great challenger [00:10:10].

    Indian philosophy anticipated our age of specialists¶

    • [00:00:36] Today most philosophers are specialists — metaphysics, ethics, epistemology, philosophy of mind — and wouldn't claim expertise across all of them. In earlier European thought philosophers were more often all-rounders: Plato did political theory and the metaphysics of forms, Hume did the critique of causation and a theory of ethical sentiment.
    • [00:01:06] Early Indian philosophy, by contrast, seems to have anticipated our contemporary scene of specialization. Given its devotion to scriptural exegesis it was natural for Mīmāṃsā to focus on language; Vedānta, following the Upaniṣads, investigated the nature of the self; Sāṃkhya and Yoga formed a "double act," theoretical and practical. The same holds for the final pair of post-Vedic schools: Nyāya specializes in logic and epistemology, Vaiśeṣika in metaphysics.
    • [00:01:46] Not to exaggerate: there were no ancient Indian philosophy departments "with one chair for each of the six schools and controversies over whether to think about finally hiring a Buddhist." Some figures spanned the supposed boundaries — the ultimate example being Vācaspati Miśra, who in the 10th century wrote within Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta, earning the title "Master of All Systems."
    • [00:02:16] Each school also explored a range of issues rather than sticking to its distinctive topic. Nyāya itself has a good deal to say about liberation and rebirth. Still, it is first and foremost a tradition of logical and epistemological reflection, which grew out of a longer history of thinking about knowledge and its sources.

    The texts: Gautama's sūtras and their commentators¶

    • [00:02:40] We've already seen the medical treatise the Caraka Saṃhitā list four pramāṇas, or sources of knowledge, to explain how doctors came to learn about the body and its treatment. Nyāya will specialize in expounding the pramāṇas and in facing up to the skeptical attack on the very possibility of knowledge — coming especially from the Buddhists.
    • [00:03:07] This mission is announced in the foundational text, which (as in the other schools) consists of canonical sūtras, followed by a bhāṣya (commentary), and then further exegetical works. The episode focuses on the first two: the Nyāya-Sūtra, written (on the latest estimates) in the first or second century AD, and the commentary on it by Vātsyāyana, around 450. Occasional mention is made of Uddyotakara, who wrote a further commentary on Vātsyāyana in the late 6th century.
    • [00:03:48] The Nyāya-Sūtra is ascribed to a man named Gautama, also known as Akṣapāda — or, more cautiously, the sūtras "eventually collected under his name." They are divided into five books: alongside the analysis of argument structure and the sources of knowledge, they give advice on the proper conduct of debate, and occasionally extend into metaphysical issues more associated with Vaiśeṣika — parts and wholes, atoms, universals.

    What makes Nyāya distinctive: anvīkṣikī, the examination by evidence¶

    • [00:04:24] Vātsyāyana is in no doubt about what makes Nyāya distinctive: "Nyāya is the examination of things with the help of evidence." An argument based on observation and received belief is called an anvīkṣā (a "reconsideration"), and the discipline concerned with such arguments is anvīkṣikī — i.e. Nyāya.
    • [00:04:48] We've met that word before — back in the second episode. In his political treatise the Arthaśāstra, Kauṭilya named anvīkṣikī as one of the four branches of learning. There it means something like "rational investigation," in a broad sense. Vātsyāyana is now appropriating the term for his own school, narrowing it specifically to the study of arguments based on evidence.

    The sixteen items and the term that opens the treatise: pramāṇa¶

    • [00:05:24] Vātsyāyana makes these remarks while commenting on the opening sentence of the Nyāya-Sūtra, which lists sixteen items comprising the subject matter of the system. The list spans epistemological concepts — beginning with pramāṇa itself — and elements of inquiry and debate, like destructive criticism and the intentional distortion of an opponent's view.
    • [00:05:48] Vātsyāyana notes that, without the explicit mention of these items, anvīkṣikī "would have been a mere study of the self, or spiritual discipline, like the Upaniṣads." By specifying its own topics, Nyāya is shown to have its unique subject matter — distinguishing the critical, investigative discipline from the "philosophies of path and purpose" examined early in the series, and from the agendas of the other Vedic schools.
    • [00:06:30] First term: pramāṇa, roughly a means of getting at truth or acquiring knowledge. Gautama's Nyāya recognizes four: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. The next term in the list is prameya — whatever one comes to know by using a pramāṇa. Perception counts as a pramāṇa because it lets me know the objects around me; an object I actually see or hear is a prameya.

    Knowledge as action — the influence of Pāṇini¶

    • [00:07:09] In Nyāya, and especially in Vātsyāyana, knowledge is regarded as a form of activity. Just as someone cuts a tree with an axe, so someone sees a tree with her eyes — and in general someone knows something by means of some pramāṇa.
    • [00:07:29] This shows the influence of the grammatical reflections of Pāṇini, who made action central to the analysis of sentences and explained other grammatical elements as contributing factors — the instrument, the purpose of the action, and so on.
    • [00:07:45] For Vātsyāyana the purpose of knowledge is crucial. He opens by saying knowledge is needed in order to secure any desired objective: each of us exerts effort only for the sake of some objective. (Compare the Mīmāṃsā idea that it is the sacrificer's desire that makes a ritual incumbent on him — "no desire, no action." Vātsyāyana adds: "no knowledge, no result." How can you get what you want when you literally don't know what you're doing?)

    Default trust: answering the skeptic's regress¶

    • [00:08:21] Vātsyāyana uses this point against the standard skeptical argument that any means of knowledge must be ratified by some further means of knowledge — leading to an infinite regress. The skeptic says we cannot trust perception unless some further perception tells us it is trustworthy.
    • [00:08:43] "No," replies Vātsyāyana. If that were true, the activities of practical life would be impossible, since the only way we ever achieve what we want is by knowing how to get it. This applies to mundane goals — wealth, pleasure — but also to liberation.
    • [00:09:01] So Nyāya competes with the Buddhists on two fronts: the epistemological front (refuting skeptical arguments) and what — "with apologies to Monty Python" — we may call the liberation front. The elimination of suffering, promised by Buddhists and Naiyāyikas alike, is one more objective achievable through knowledge, and through knowledge alone.

    The theory of debate: honest, tricky, and skeptical¶

    • [00:09:22] In Indian philosophy it was never enough to be right — you had to prove your opponents wrong. Hence Nyāya's other great interest, the theory of debate. Gautama distinguishes two kinds: honest debate, aimed at ascertaining the truth, and tricky debate, aimed at defeating the opponent at all costs, even by underhanded tactics.
    • [00:09:49] A third style is that used by skeptics, who wish to refute all philosophical doctrines without advancing any positive stance of their own. The acknowledged master of this third style "lived at about the same time the Nyāya-Sūtra was given its current form" — the Buddhist Nāgārjuna, who posed a formidable challenge to the whole Nyāya project.
    • [00:10:18] Vātsyāyana dismisses this general skepticism as incoherent: merely by rejecting the positive theories of others, the skeptic is forced into positive claims of his own, "because to deny a proposition is to assert its opposite." If the Buddhist denies that there is an enduring self, that is the philosophical claim that there is no enduring self. (Whether Nāgārjuna can rise to this challenge is left for a later episode.)

    A subtler parting of ways — and the post mistaken for a person¶

    • [00:10:47] A more subtle point: given how central the pramāṇas are, you'd expect Nyāya to be at pains to defend their legitimacy. But not exactly. The Nyāya thinkers tend simply to assume the pramāṇas are reliable — a theory of default trust in our cognitions. This is the "innocent until proven guilty" epistemology already met in Mīmāṃsā; the two schools agree on it.
    • [00:11:25] Nyāya adds a further argument against skeptics who refuse the assumption. Uddyotakara: "False cognitions are imitations of correct cognitions; therefore you must provide some example of correct cognition." False cognitions — illusions, dreams — can only be called false in contrast to true ones.
    • [00:11:56] Vātsyāyana's example makes the same point: suppose I see a wooden post from a distance and take it for a person. Here perception has gone wrong. Yet the very same case shows perception sometimes succeeds: if I had never correctly perceived a human being, I could not now misperceive the post as a person. The error presupposes prior success.

    The seven ingredients of philosophical inquiry¶

    • [00:12:27] So much for the destructive debate of the skeptic, which Uddyotakara denounced as mere quibbles (while taking it seriously enough to defend Gautama). What of the honest sort that actually seeks truth? This forms the basis of the Nyāya method of inquiry: a process moving from initial uncertainty about a thing to understanding that it has certain properties.
    • [00:12:58] The procedure has seven essential ingredients: doubt, purpose, observational data, doctrinal basis, a syllogistic inference, hypothetical argument, and a final decision.
    • [00:13:12] (1) Doubt launches the investigation — always wondering whether some object has some feature or not. There are five specific types: (i) the ordinary doubt when an object is perceived in outline but its specific character is unclear (the post mistaken for a person); (ii) doubt where an object's essence is known but its properties unknown (is a breaking sound a quality of air, or a movement in the air?); (iii) uncertainty when two contradictory statements are made about the same object; (iv) doubt about whether a thing is real at all or an illusion, like water in a desert mirage; (v) doubt whether an unseen object is really there — "such as the giraffe that may or may not be in the kitchen."
    • [00:14:23] You wouldn't worry about a giraffe in the kitchen without good reason to think — or hope — it's so, and rightly: Gautama is not encouraging idle speculation or exploring every remote doubt. So (2) Purpose: in a rational inquiry there must be some purpose for inquiring. Like knowing, inquiring is an action and always has a goal — and here it's a goal pursued by more than one person, since this is framed as a method of debate.
    • [00:14:54] For debate to proceed, the parties need shared common ground. Hence (3) observational data and (4) a doctrinal basis — background principles plus empirical data, the "corroborative instances" either side can appeal to. Political disputes are often fruitless precisely because this fails: if two sides disagree about all the facts, they can't usefully discuss how to respond to them.
    • [00:15:22] Even unshared assumptions can be useful: if a disputant produces an argument that contradicts a doctrine of his own school, and the opponent points this out, his argument is undermined. Some proofs work only on a school's own premises — useful among allies but cutting "no ice" against rivals — and sometimes we grant premises merely for the sake of argument.

    Where there's smoke there's fire: the five-limbed inference¶

    • [00:15:54] Now the ground rules. For the first time in India we get, in the Nyāya-Sūtra, a schematic analysis of valid argument patterns. In general: we know an object has a first property because it has a second property always found together with the first — the second property pervades the first.
    • [00:16:21] The classic example: where there is smoke, there is fire. If I see smoke on a mountain, I can infer fire on the mountain. The inference is good only if the two properties stand in the right relation — I can be confident of fire only if there is really fire wherever there is smoke.
    • [00:16:38] Gautama formalizes this as a five-limbed argument scheme: (1) state the thesis — there is fire on the mountain; (2) cite the reason — because I see smoke up there; (3) invoke a universal rule and give an example — where there is smoke, there is fire, just like at home in the kitchen; (4) apply the rule — there is smoke on the mountain; (5) draw the conclusion — so there must be fire there.
    • [00:17:11] Two odd things. Why mention an example? Pointing out that we've seen fire produce smoke in kitchens (like the time "the giraffe forgot to turn the oven off") isn't logically necessary — but it makes sense in a debate, where the goal is to convince the other side the universal rule is a good one. Why repeat the thesis at the end? The last step seems superfluous since the thesis was stated first — but again it's dialectical: it's one thing to announce what I'm going to prove, or state it as a conjecture, another to declare that I have proved it, even in the same words.
    • [00:17:59] The procedure also works in philosophical contexts. The Nyāya-Sūtra has a case showing sound is non-eternal: the universal rule is that everything produced is eventually destroyed (as a jar is) — remember, you have to give an example; since sound is produced, it too must eventually be destroyed; therefore sound is non-eternal.

    Tarka: counterfactual reasoning, and why it's not a pramāṇa¶

    • [00:18:23] This isn't the only argument form. One of the most interesting is tarka — hypothetical or counterfactual reasoning: if this were true, then that would be true; but that isn't true, so this isn't either. For example: if there were really a giraffe in the kitchen, it would be making a lot of noise; but I hear nothing; so there's no giraffe in there. The Madhyamaka Buddhists use this style too, calling it prasaṅga. It lets philosophers eliminate rival theories.
    • [00:19:06] We've already seen tarka in action this episode: if (as the skeptic claimed) there were no valid means of acquiring knowledge, we could never get through everyday life — but we do — so there must be valid means of knowing.
    • [00:19:24] Tarka is so powerful you might think it would itself count as a means of knowing. Yet Nyāya denies that counterfactual reasoning is a pramāṇa. Why? Not, as you might think, because tarka can only rule things out — that seems wrong, since we just used it to establish the positive, significant thesis that there are valid means of knowing.
    • [00:19:55] More likely the problem is that tarka gives no direct basis for knowing or understanding that something is the case; it merely shows something must be true given other things we know. I perceive directly that the kitchen is quiet, and only indirectly infer there's no giraffe. Another worry: tarka requires me to hypothesize a falsehood ("if there were a giraffe, which there isn't…") — and the Naiyāyikas may have hesitated to let their "precious pramāṇas" be tainted by the assumption of a falsehood, even en route to a truth.

    Where we go next¶

    • [00:20:38] We're "well on the way to understanding the Nyāya theory of truth," having looked at the system's basic ideas — its grounds for confidence that knowledge is possible, and its strategies for prevailing in honest debate. But the pramāṇas themselves need closer examination, so next time we'll try to understand what Nyāya means by perception and by inference.
    • [00:21:06] A closing tarka: if you weren't interested in Nyāya epistemology you wouldn't bother to tune in — but since you are, "we'll see you next time."

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Nyāya — the school of logic and epistemology; "the examination of things with the help of evidence"; identical with anvīkṣikī, the discipline of evidence-based argument.
    • Vaiśeṣika — Nyāya's partner school, specializing in metaphysics (atoms, universals, parts and wholes).
    • Gautama (Akṣapāda) — the man credited with the Nyāya-Sūtra, the school's five-book foundational text.
    • Nyāya-Sūtra — canonical sūtras of 1st–2nd c. AD; lists the sixteen items of the system and gives the first Indian schematic analysis of valid argument.
    • Vātsyāyana — author of the bhāṣya (commentary) on the Nyāya-Sūtra, c. 450; defines Nyāya, defends default trust in the pramāṇas, treats knowledge as activity.
    • Uddyotakara — late-6th-c. sub-commentator on Vātsyāyana; "false cognitions are imitations of correct cognitions, so you must give an example of a correct one."
    • Vācaspati Miśra — 10th-c. polymath who wrote in five schools and was titled "Master of All Systems."
    • pramāṇa — a means of getting at truth / acquiring knowledge; Nyāya recognizes four: perception, inference, comparison, testimony.
    • prameya — what is known by means of a pramāṇa (e.g. the object I see).
    • anvīkṣikī — "rational investigation"; named by Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra as a branch of learning, narrowed by Vātsyāyana to evidence-based argument = Nyāya.
    • default trust — Nyāya's "innocent until proven guilty" assumption that the pramāṇas are reliable, shared with Mīmāṃsā; the answer to the skeptic's regress.
    • honest vs. tricky debate — debate aimed at truth vs. debate aimed at defeating the opponent at all costs; a third, purely skeptical style refutes all without asserting anything.
    • Nāgārjuna — the Buddhist master of pure skeptical debate, Nyāya's great challenger; Vātsyāyana calls such general skepticism incoherent ("to deny a proposition is to assert its opposite").
    • five-limbed argument — thesis, reason, universal rule + example, application, conclusion; classic case "where there's smoke there's fire."
    • pervasion — the relation that makes inference valid: the evidence-property (smoke) must be found only where the inferred property (fire) is.
    • tarka — hypothetical/counterfactual reasoning (Madhyamaka prasaṅga); eliminates rival theories but is not counted a pramāṇa, since it gives only indirect knowledge and assumes a falsehood.
    • Pāṇini — the grammarian whose action-centred sentence analysis underlies Nyāya's treatment of knowing as an action done "by means of" an instrument.

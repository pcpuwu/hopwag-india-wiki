    HoPWaG India · Ep 53 — "Follow the Evidence: Dignāga's Logic"¶

    ← Ep 52

    Ep 54 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:23:29 In brief: Dignāga's theory of inference (anumāna): why he recognizes only perception and inference as means of knowledge and demotes testimony to a kind of inference; his distinction between reasoning-for-oneself and demonstration-for-others; the mountain-smoke-fire schema of pakṣa / sādhya / hetu; the celebrated trairūpya "three conditions" for good evidence; and how his commentator Dharmakīrti reformulated those conditions and tackled the problem of induction by demanding a real causal or essential link between properties. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gennary"; Dignāga ≈ "Dignaga/Dignago"; Nyāya ≈ "Niyaya/Nayaas"; pramāṇa ≈ "pramana"; Brahminical ≈ "brachmanical"; Mīmāṃsā ≈ "Mimamsa"; Yogācāra ≈ "Yoga-Chara"; Uddyotakara ≈ "Ujotakara"; pakṣa ≈ "Paksha/Paxha"; sādhya ≈ "sadya/sadia"; hetu ≈ "hethu"; trairūpya ≈ "Treyrupa/trirupia"; pervasion (vyāpti) ≈ "pervasion"; Gaṅgeśa ≈ "Gangesha"; Dharmakīrti ≈ "Darmakirti/Dama Kirti"; eva ≈ "Eva"; svabhāva-hetu ≈ "swavava hetu"; Nāgārjuna ≈ "Nagadruna"; śiṃśapā ≈ "shimshapha/Shimshapa"; Graham Priest ≈ "Graham Priest").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • Dignāga — Buddhist epistemologist; recognizes only perception and inference as pramāṇas, and the first Indian thinker to separate the rules of reasoning from the rules of debate [00:01:24].
    • The Nyāya & Mīmāṃsā schools — the Brahminical opponents who count testimony as an independent source of knowledge [00:01:06].
    • Dignāga's collected verses on the sources of knowledge — where he sets out good vs. bad inference [00:04:29].
    • Dharmakīrti — Dignāga's hugely influential commentator who reformulated the three conditions and addressed induction [00:15:34].
    • Gaṅgeśa — 14th-century thinker who would later review over a dozen accounts of "pervasion" [00:11:17].

    Testimony on trial: why Dignāga keeps only two pramāṇas¶

    • [00:00:34] A juror is doing "applied epistemology" of unusually high stakes: deciding whether to be persuaded by evidence, often in the form of testimony. Suppose a witness claims to have seen the defendant set a fire. If she's truthful, she knows he's guilty — she saw it with her own eyes. Can she transfer that knowledge to you by giving a convincing account?
    • [00:01:06] The Nyāya school would say yes: testimony is one of the fundamental sources of knowledge and so is designated a pramāṇa (means of knowledge). But as a juror you may doubt the witness's credibility, and as a philosopher you may doubt whether testimony is a knowledge-source at all.
    • [00:01:24] If so, call as your own witness the Buddhist Dignāga. As noted last time, he admits only two means of acquiring knowledge — perception and inference — and refuses to dignify testimony (the assertions of authoritative persons) with the title pramāṇa.
    • [00:01:42] His point is not that testimony never gives knowledge, but that when it does, an inference is required. To get knowledge from the arson witness you must combine her testimony about the fire with the further knowledge that she is reliable; from those two pieces you may infer the defendant's guilt.
    • [00:02:03] This may sound like "pedantic bookkeeping" — a way of trimming the pramāṇas from three to two — but it is really a Buddhist refusing to keep the books venerated in the Brahminical tradition. Prosecuting the case against the vaunted testimony of Vedic texts, Dignāga insists they provide no independent source of knowledge.
    • [00:02:26] His position answers the Nyāya most directly, but it also contrasts vividly with Mīmāṃsā epistemology: testimony is not "innocent until proven guilty" (as Mīmāṃsā held), but must be evaluated for reliability on each occasion.
    • [00:02:39] To this extent Dignāga is a rationalist — every knowledge-claim must rest on evidence directly available to the individual knower. Knowledge by testimony is no counterexample, because each of us must judge the testimony's credibility, and only if it passes muster may we infer that what it asserts is true.

    Two kinds of inference: reasoning vs. demonstration¶

    • [00:03:09] We saw last time that for Dignāga direct perception grasps unique particulars. Reflection at the general or universal level — e.g. that smoke billows from houses set on fire — is imposed onto perceptual experience, an act of human construction. Inference lives at this general level.
    • [00:03:29] So: "This house is billowing smoke; when houses do that they're on fire; so it's on fire. Good thing it isn't mine." Dignāga calls this inferring to oneself — basically his term for reasoning, deciding whether to be convinced yourself.
    • [00:03:45] He contrasts it with inferring for others: what you do when trying to convince someone else, as when you calmly explain that their house is on fire because smoke is pouring out of it. If inferring-to-oneself is reasoning, inferring-for-others is demonstration — producing an explicit argument.
    • [00:04:08] Here is one of Dignāga's signal achievements: he is the first Indian thinker to distinguish the rules of reasoning (which arguments are formally valid) from the rules of debate (how to persuade people).
    • [00:04:29] A further achievement: in his collected verses on the sources of knowledge, he explains the difference between good and bad inferences — both in reasoning to oneself (reasoning well vs. badly) and in arguing for others (debating fairly vs. unfairly).

    A neutral theory — adopted even by his opponents¶

    • [00:04:43] So far Dignāga's Yogācāra Buddhism has been the obvious backdrop: the demotion of testimony implies a critique of Vedic tradition, and the claim that general concepts are human constructions places him in the Buddhist tradition of philosophical skepticism.
    • [00:05:04] But when he comes to his theory of inference itself, he seems to aim at neutrality — philosophers of various schools could adopt it, and they did. His analysis was assimilated widely, including the Nyāya school from Uddyotakara onwards.
    • [00:05:28] In honour of this rare instance of Naiyāyikas and Buddhists getting on "like a house on fire," the episode shifts from the arson case to the classic Nyāya example: smoke that reveals the presence of fire on a mountain.

    The shape of an inference: pakṣa, sādhya, hetu¶

    • [00:05:38] For Dignāga, any inference can be rendered as: "This mountain has fire because it has smoke." First a proposition is stated (the mountain has fire), then a reason is given (smoke).
    • [00:05:52] Note this is the reverse of the deductive pattern in European logicians from Aristotle on, where premises come first and a conclusion follows: "the mountain has smoke, therefore it has fire."
    • [00:06:06] A second contrast with Aristotle: Dignāga's reason is not explanatory. The smoke reveals the fire, or lets us predict it — but there's no asking why there's a fire. Still, Dignāga agrees with Aristotle that the proposition to be proved is something not yet known and not immediately settled by perception. (Aristotle's memorable example: standing on the moon you could just see the cause of an eclipse and need no explanation.)
    • [00:06:40] The inference patterns all share one structure. There is always an object that is the locus or basis of the inference — here the mountain — in Sanskrit the pakṣa.
    • [00:06:53] The inference seeks to show the locus has a certain property — e.g. having fire — called the sādhya (the property-to-be-proved).
    • [00:07:00] We establish this by citing a hetu: another property also possessed by the locus, which is the evidence or sign — here, having smoke.
    • [00:07:17] So every inference has the form: the locus (pakṣa) has the inferred property (sādhya) because it has the evidential property (hetu) — the mountain has fire because it has smoke.

    When evidence fails — and the need for a rule¶

    • [00:07:28] Many inferences fit this form yet are bad — not compelling moves "in the space of reasons." You shouldn't infer the mountain is covered in grass because it is green: many green things aren't grass (moss, green paint, a St. Patrick's Day parade). Being green is not good evidence for grass; the evidential and inferred properties aren't connected in the right way.
    • [00:08:04] Dignāga's goal is to specify what the right way is — to define the relation among pakṣa, sādhya and hetu. That is to ask: when is evidence sufficient to convince us that one property always comes together with another?

    The trairūpya: the triple-conditioned sign¶

    • [00:08:17] Dignāga's answer: the evidence must satisfy a set of three conditions — his celebrated trairūpya theory, the theory of the triple-conditioned sign.
    • [00:08:29] First condition: the evidential property must actually occur in the locus. Obvious enough — I can't use smoke as evidence the mountain is on fire unless there really is smoke on the mountain.
    • [00:08:46] The other two are subtler and bring in terminology. Dignāga introduces the similar case (homologue / sapakṣa) and the dissimilar case (heterologue / vipakṣa):
    • A similar case is any object other than the locus that has the inferred property — in our example, something else that is on fire.
    • A dissimilar case is some other object that lacks the inferred property — something not on fire.
    • [00:09:17] This is effectively a method of investigation: to learn whether a thing has a property, notice a second property it has, then check whether other things with that second property also have the first.
    • [00:09:31] So with smoke as the second property: consider a smoky kitchen — there's fire there too (smoke is looking like good evidence). Now consider something dissimilar — your own smoke-free house: no arsonist came, so it lacks smoke, and its lack of smoke coincides with its not being on fire. This confirms smoke really correlates with fire.
    • [00:10:05] These are Dignāga's second and third conditions:
    • Second condition (positive-example): the evidential property must occur together with the inferred property in at least one similar case (like the smoky kitchen).
    • Third condition (no-counterexample): in every known dissimilar case where the evidential property is absent, the inferred property is absent too (like your smoke-free, fire-free house).
    • [00:10:34] Taken together: whatever has the evidential property must have the inferred property. If every locus of smoke is a locus of fire, we can soundly infer fire from smoke in a particular place.

    Pervasion, generality, and the leaning toward induction¶

    • [00:10:46] Notice Dignāga keeps his promise that inference operates at a general level, with universal concepts. Good inferences are those where a general rule or law connects the properties: "whatever is like this is like that," or in variables, "whatever is X is Y."
    • [00:11:11] Dignāga calls such a connection a relation of pervasion (vyāpti) — a topic of huge importance in later centuries. Gaṅgeśa, in the 14th century, would review more than a dozen accounts of pervasion that emerged after Dignāga.
    • [00:11:29] Could we not just rephrase Dignāga's inference as a pure deduction? Take "the mountain has smoke," add "whatever has smoke has fire," conclude "the mountain has fire." Not quite. Dignāga is primarily interested in inference as a means of acquiring knowledge — the conditions under which an inference's result is a rationally justified belief.
    • [00:12:10] So he's not interested only in form, like a logician noting the validity of "this is an X, every X is a Y, so this is a Y." He is more demanding: a good inference must be not only valid but sound — with true premises. And further still, the premises must be known to be true; only then does the inferrer know the conclusion.
    • [00:12:46] In keeping with this focus on what the reasoner actually knows, Dignāga doesn't quite require the strong "every X is Y." His demand is in one respect weaker: the reasoner must satisfy herself that every known X is known to be Y. She needn't claim all smoky things past, present and future have been or will be on fire — only that every time she has experienced smoke, there was fire.
    • [00:13:19] "The lack of exceptions proves the rule." This shows Dignāga isn't really after pure deduction but something closer to induction: looking to previous experience to justify inferences about the present and future. To that extent his trairūpya can seem familiar and even straightforward.

    Two worries about the three conditions¶

    • [00:13:43] Worry one — redundancy. Saying "anything with smoke has fire" seems equivalent to "anything lacking smoke lacks fire." So why give the second and third conditions as two distinct criteria rather than one criterion stated positively or negatively?
    • [00:14:13] Perhaps because the second criterion requires us to find at least one actual similar case, whereas the third only tells us to verify that where smoke is absent, fire is absent. The third alone wouldn't guarantee there are any cases (besides the mountain itself) having both smoke and fire; the second adds a positive basis for induction.
    • [00:14:36] Worry two — the locus-exclusion problem. Dignāga always tells us to look at things other than the locus when checking similar/dissimilar cases. Fine for a one-off topic like this mountain. But consider: "Odd numbers are divisible by two because they are integers." If we exclude the class of odd numbers (the locus) from the search, then every remaining integer is divisible by two — so being an integer (evidential property) would count as good evidence for being divisible by two (inferred property). A clearly bad inference passing Dignāga's conditions.
    • [00:15:23] This unwelcome result was pointed out by Jain philosophers, who criticized him and tried to repair it. Criticism and modification also came from within the Buddhist tradition.

    Dharmakīrti's reformulation¶

    • [00:15:34] By far the most influential critic was Dignāga's commentator Dharmakīrti — so influential his writings effectively replaced Dignāga's among Sanskrit readers. For this reason Dignāga's collected verses (and his own auto-commentary) had until very recently to be read in Tibetan translation, until evidence of the Sanskrit original was uncovered.
    • [00:16:02] Later Buddhist epistemology came mostly as commentaries on Dharmakīrti or manuals digesting his thought. Dharmakīrti had two main complaints: an imprecision in the formulation of the triple condition, and Dignāga's failure to address the problem of induction.
    • [00:16:29] The imprecision. When the locus is a class rather than a single item, Dignāga's first condition — that the evidential property "must be present" — is ambiguous. If a policeman testifies that "arsonists like candles," he might mean all arsonists do, or merely that they tend to, or just that he's met candle-loving arsonists now and again. So when the locus is a class, "the evidential property is present in the locus" could mean it belongs to every member or only some.
    • [00:17:10] The second and third conditions are similarly ambiguous: must I examine all potentially similar and dissimilar cases, or only a few examples? This bears on the redundancy question: if it suffices to find one similar case but I must be exhaustive about counterexamples, the two conditions clearly differ; but if both searches must be exhaustive, they collapse into one and the same search.
    • [00:17:54] Dharmakīrti's fix: introduce the Sanskrit restrictive particle "eva" — meaning only, exactly, really. His reformulated conditions:
    • First (locus condition): the evidential property is present exactly in the locus.
    • Second (agreement) & third (disagreement): the evidential property is observed only in similar cases and is really absent from dissimilar cases.
    • [00:18:28] The "eva" clears each ambiguity: the property is present in the whole locus; present in some (not necessarily all) similar cases; and absent from all dissimilar cases.
    • [00:18:39] On this updated theory it is really the third (last) condition that does the work. Pervasion of one property by another means the evidential property is absent from whatever lacks the inferred property — smoke is absent from whatever lacks fire. This is much stronger than the second condition, which merely asks for one similar case like the smoky kitchen.

    Dharmakīrti on the problem of induction¶

    • [00:19:08] The second worry strikes at the heart of inductive reasoning — and arises whether we justify positively (finding things with both smoke and fire) or negatively (finding things lacking both). How can we support a universal generalization from a limited number of examples? The 99 smoky things I've seen all had fire — but what of the hundredth, thousandth, millionth?
    • [00:19:39] Dharmakīrti's solution: go beyond merely observing that properties always come together, and demand a link that explains why they do. He identifies different kinds of reason that link properties.
    • [00:19:51] (1) Cause-and-effect reason. The case we've discussed throughout: because smoke is an effect of fire, it never appears without fire. Alternatively, two properties may both be effects of a shared cause — "night follows day" holds not because day causes night, but because both are caused by the rotation of the earth. Another example (cited by some Buddhists): inferring lemon colour from lemon taste, since both are products of the same cause, the lemon itself.
    • [00:20:24] (2) Natural reason (svabhāva-hetu). Dharmakīrti's illustration: being a śiṃśapā tree is a reason for being a tree. Being a śiṃśapā (or a lemon tree) isn't a cause of being a tree, yet the relation isn't accidental either. In the term svabhāva-hetu you recognize svabhāva — the essence or nature of a thing — the very target of Nāgārjuna's critical philosophy. So the nature of the śiṃśapā tree includes or implies the nature of tree.
    • [00:21:09] Puzzlingly, Dharmakīrti says such inferences rest on the relation of identity rather than causality. Perhaps he means the śiṃśapā tree and the tree are the same thing (whereas cause and effect are always distinct); or that the tree has really one property described more and less fine-grainedly — as someone's being "tall" and being "six foot three" are really identical.
    • [00:21:39] (3) Reason based on non-observation. Met in an earlier episode (the Nyāya claim that we can perceive absences): the pattern "there is no pot here because it is not observed." Such inferences rest on a counterfactual: if there were a pot here, it would be observed — assuming normal lighting, working sense faculties, and so on.
    • [00:22:09] Like the first two, this third kind shows not just that two properties do come together in experience, but that they must. A good inference requires a real link or tie: nothing tastes of lemon without smelling of lemon (both caused by lemons); nothing can be a lemon tree without being a tree. "When you don't find that life has given you lemons, you can't make lemonade."
    • [00:22:31] This is Dharmakīrti's solution to the problem of induction: a genuine link between properties explains why we've always seen them together and guarantees they will keep coming together in future.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Dignāga — Buddhist epistemologist who admits only perception and inference as pramāṇas; first Indian thinker to separate the rules of reasoning from the rules of debate.
    • pramāṇa — a means or source of knowledge; Dignāga keeps two (perception, inference), refusing the title to testimony.
    • testimony — for Nyāya an independent pramāṇa; for Dignāga it yields knowledge only via an inference (combining the report with knowledge that the witness is reliable).
    • inferring to oneself / inferring for others — reasoning (deciding whether to be convinced) vs. demonstration (producing an explicit argument to convince others).
    • pakṣa — the locus or basis of an inference (the mountain).
    • sādhya — the inferred property to be proved (having fire).
    • hetu — the evidential property or sign cited as reason (having smoke).
    • trairūpya — the "triple-conditioned sign": Dignāga's three conditions a good piece of evidence must meet.
    • similar / dissimilar case (homologue–sapakṣa / heterologue–vipakṣa) — other objects that respectively have or lack the inferred property; used to test the evidence.
    • pervasion (vyāpti) — the general relation "whatever is X is Y" connecting evidential and inferred properties; later elaborated at length (Gaṅgeśa, 14th c., reviewed a dozen accounts).
    • Nyāya & Mīmāṃsā — Brahminical schools Dignāga argues against (Nyāya counts testimony as a pramāṇa; Mīmāṃsā treats testimony as innocent until proven guilty); Nyāya later adopted Dignāga's inference theory from Uddyotakara on.
    • Dharmakīrti — Dignāga's dominant commentator; reformulated the three conditions using the restrictive particle eva ("only/really") and solved the induction problem by demanding a real link between properties.
    • eva — Sanskrit restrictive particle ("only, exactly, really") with which Dharmakīrti removed the ambiguity in Dignāga's conditions.
    • svabhāva-hetu — Dharmakīrti's "natural reason," where one property's nature implies another's (śiṃśapā ⇒ tree); built on svabhāva (essence), the target of Nāgārjuna's critique.
    • three kinds of reason (Dharmakīrti) — cause-and-effect, natural (essential/identity), and non-observation; each supplies the "must" that mere observed correlation lacks.
    • problem of induction — how a universal generalization can be supported from finitely many cases; Dharmakīrti answers it by requiring an explanatory link, not mere constant conjunction.

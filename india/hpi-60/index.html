    HoPWaG India · Ep 60 — "The Buddha and I: Indian Influence on Islamic and European Thought"¶

    ← Ep 59

    Ep 61 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:37 In brief: A survey of how Indian ideas travelled west — first into the Islamic world (al-Bīrūnī's pioneering, if homogenizing, comparative study of India), then into early-modern and modern Europe (Locke, Leibniz, Hume, Hegel, Schopenhauer, down to 20th-c. Oxford) — with the recurring flaw that Western readers flattened India's diversity into one "monolithic teaching" and saw it through what they expected rather than what it said. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit/Arabic terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Gernardin Gannery"; al-Bīrūnī ≈ "Al-Biruni/Arbiruni/Al-Beruni"; Barmakids ≈ "Barma kids/Badma kids/Bodhma kids"; Ibn al-Nadīm ≈ "Ibn Nadim/Imnanadi Dim"; al-Kindī ≈ "Arkindi"; śramaṇa/Shamaniyya ≈ "shramana/Shamania"; Jahm ibn Ṣafwān ≈ "Jahm Im Safwan"; Maḥmūd of Ghazna ≈ "Mahmoud O'Hazna"; Gauḍapāda ≈ "Gaurapada"; Suśruta ≈ "Sushuta"; Pañcatantra ≈ "Pachatantra"; al-Shahrastānī ≈ "Ashah Hastani"; Rashīd al-Dīn ≈ "Rashid Adin"; Suhrawardī ≈ "Suhavardi"; Dārā Shukoh ≈ "Dada Shikou/Dadaashikou"; Charles Dolu ≈ "Charles Dolou"; Pierre Bayle ≈ "Pierre Bélle/Bale"; Francis Bernier ≈ "Vanieres/Banyé"; Colebrooke ≈ "Colbrook"; Bṛhaspati ≈ "Brihaspati"; Schopenhauer ≈ "Schovenhauer"; Radhakrishnan ≈ "Sarvepaly Radha Krishnan"; Matilal ≈ "Bimal-Khrishnan Matilal"; Strawson ≈ "Strasson"; Dummett ≈ "Dummit").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • al-Bīrūnī — medieval scientist whose Treatise on India (completed 1030) was the first ambitious comparative study of Indian thought for an Arabic readership [00:00:50].
    • The Barmakids — wealthy Bactrian-descended clan in the late-8th-c. caliphate who actively sponsored the transmission of Indian science [00:02:23].
    • Dārā Shukoh — 17th-c. Mughal prince who translated the Upaniṣads into Persian and wrote Confluence of the Oceans [00:08:30].
    • Hume, Hegel, Schopenhauer — the three European moderns through whom Indian (especially Buddhist) ideas are traced, and who repeatedly misread India [00:10:01].

    al-Bīrūnī: the first great comparativist¶

    • [00:00:32] Last time covered modern scholars who noticed parallels between ancient Indian and ancient Greek thought. But searching for such resonances is not distinctively modern — it was already the project of the medieval scientist al-Bīrūnī, whose treatise on India was completed in 1030.
    • [00:00:56] The treatise was aimed at an Arabic-speaking readership who would thereby be equipped for encounters with Indian culture. His approach is comparative: he systematically juxtaposes Indian ideas with those of Greek philosophy and science, and even flags some of the same parallels noted by contemporary historians — such as the transmigration of souls. Indeed al-Bīrūnī identifies belief in reincarnation as the distinctive marker of "the Indian creed," much as the Trinity marks Christianity or the Sabbath marks the Jews.

    Why a Greek-savvy readership existed: the translation movements¶

    • [00:01:34] al-Bīrūnī could presume his readers knew Greek ideas because a prodigious Greek-to-Arabic translation movement had already made them available, in the 8th–10th centuries AD.
    • [00:01:44] At around the same time some Indian texts were translated too, focused on scientific works: Arabic versions of medical texts like the Suśruta, and in the mid-8th century an astronomical handbook called in Arabic the Sindhind, produced at the behest of the caliph al-Manṣūr. Indian astronomy and astrology were an important source for those disciplines in the Islamic world.
    • [00:02:14] Beyond the sciences there was the Kalīla wa-Dimna, a literary work derived (via a Persian translation) from the Indian book of animal fables, the Pañcatantra.

    The Barmakids and the earliest awareness of Indian ideas¶

    • [00:02:23] Readers of Arabic could especially thank one family, the Barmakids, who were influential in the late-8th-c. caliphate. They hailed from Ṭukhāristān in Bactria — the only area under Arab rule where Buddhism and Sanskrit literature were still actively studied.
    • [00:02:45] The wealthy and powerful Barmakids took an active interest in Indian science: they summoned Indian doctors and philosophers and even dispatched a fact-finding mission to India. A document on Indian culture written for one of them is lost, but the 10th-c. bibliophile Ibn al-Nadīm claimed to have seen a copy written out by none other than al-Kindī — the first Muslim thinker to make explicit use of Greek philosophical sources.
    • [00:03:15] The same Ibn al-Nadīm knew of the Buddhists in Transoxiana, calling them the Shamaniyya — a term traceable to the Indian word for the renouncer movement, śramaṇa.
    • [00:03:28] Particularly intriguing are reports of a debate between the early-8th-c. theologian Jahm ibn Ṣafwān and a group of Buddhists, who argued that his belief in the god of Islam could not be substantiated on empirical evidence. So a full century before al-Kindī — usually credited with initiating philosophy in the Islamic world — there was at least fleeting awareness of Indian ideas about the sources of knowledge (pramāṇas).

    al-Bīrūnī's small-scale translation movement¶

    • [00:04:01] al-Bīrūnī went far beyond his predecessors, made possible because he was attached to the court of the warlord Maḥmūd of Ghazna, who made destructive incursions into India. al-Bīrūnī is frank about the enmity Indians bore toward Muslims as a result; he worked with scholars who had presumably been brought to Maḥmūd's court under duress.
    • [00:04:27] The result was a small-scale Sanskrit–Arabic translation movement. With his Indian advisors he prepared translations of Patañjali's Yoga-Sūtra and the Sāṃkhya-kārikā, drew on Gauḍapāda's commentary on the latter, and used an Arabic Bhagavad-Gītā (which, by his quotations, often diverged from the version we know). They also translated the other way, from Arabic into Sanskrit — Euclid, Ptolemy, and al-Bīrūnī's own works.
    • [00:05:05] So his treatise is a remarkably well-informed document with extensive Sanskrit quotation, relating its sources not only to Greek thought but to Christianity, Judaism, and Manichaeism. Islam is implicitly exempted from the comparison and given special status — though he does note an affinity between Indian philosophy and Sufism.

    al-Bīrūnī's flaw: India as a single doctrine¶

    • [00:05:36] What al-Bīrūnī does not do well is convey the diversity of Indian thought. He treats the ideas of the Indian elite as a single body of doctrine, which he contrasts with "vulgar popular beliefs" he openly disdains. But he is an "equal-opportunity elitist": he says the Greeks too were idolaters, and that ordinary Muslims would venerate images of the Prophet or Mecca given half a chance.
    • [00:06:09] al-Bīrūnī is impressed by tendencies toward monotheism in Indian philosophy — the first thing he reports of the Indian religion reads like a statement of Islamic monotheism (God eternal, free, omnipotent, resembled by none of his creatures). Taking Patañjali as his key, he presents Indian thought as a single harmonious teaching aimed at liberating the soul from ignorance and attachment to matter so as to attain union with God.
    • [00:06:38] This may remind us of Vedānta — yet that tradition goes unmentioned by al-Bīrūnī. His focus is the soul's relation to a single all-powerful God, achieved by detaching action from desire, as the Bhagavad-Gītā instructs. His tendency to lump everything together and see it echoing other cultures set the tone for all subsequent engagement with Indian thought.

    Later Islamic engagement, down to Dārā Shukoh¶

    • [00:07:13] In the Islamic world it was a while before another such engagement. The 12th-c. theologian-philosopher al-Shahrastānī wrote a survey of religious doctrines and did discuss India, but his treatment is disappointingly sketchy and seems not to draw on al-Bīrūnī. Better informed is the historian Rashīd al-Dīn (writing around 1300), aided by new information from a Buddhist scholar.
    • [00:07:38] There was also interest in India's mystical traditions: the 12th-c. Illuminationist philosopher Suhrawardī insisted his own teachings accorded with the sages of India, and yogic meditation reached Arabic readers through translations of a work called the Pool of Nectar — though a study concludes it offered only a narrow window, barely distinguishable from the standard occult and mystical practices already current in Islamicate society.
    • [00:08:12] It was really the Islamic invasions of India that recreated the conditions for renewing al-Bīrūnī's project. His hunch that Sufism matched Indian belief was borne out by the success of Islamic mysticism in the subcontinent. The syncretic trend climaxed with Dārā Shukoh, a 17th-c. prince who translated some of the Upaniṣads into Persian and wrote Confluence of the Oceans — the title pointing to the agreement between Indian and Islamic culture, the latter understood mainly as philosophical Sufism (covered in Ep 189 of the main HoP podcast).

    India reaches Europe — Locke and Leibniz¶

    • [00:08:58] By this time information about Indian philosophy was reaching Europe too — and the results were "not always edifying." Dārā Shukoh's younger contemporary John Locke mocked the notion of a mysterious underlying substance by comparing it to a "poor Indian philosopher" who imagined the earth needed something to bear it up and so rested it on an elephant, supported in turn by a tortoise.
    • [00:09:28] Leibniz rose to India's defense, chastising Locke: this Indian conception of substance, "for all its apparent thinness, is less empty and sterile than it is thought to be," with consequences of the greatest importance that could give philosophy "an entirely new face." Neither passage suggests deep knowledge — but opportunities to learn more were increasing.

    Hume and the Buddhist back-channel¶

    • [00:10:01] Alison Gopnik has raised the possibility that David Hume was acquainted with Buddhist philosophy. While writing his Treatise of Human Nature, Hume was in residence at the Jesuit academy of La Flèche, where he could have met the much older Charles Dolu, who had been on a trip to Siam in the 1680s.
    • [00:10:22] Dolu in turn knew Ippolito Desideri, who had done missionary work in Tibet. Both were well informed about Buddhism; Desideri even wrote a treatise on "a false and peculiar religion observed in Tibet," urging (like al-Bīrūnī before him) that people should know more of it in order to contest it, and highlighting its "Pythagoreanism" — presumably its commitment to reincarnation. Gopnik: in 1735 Hume, rusticating in a small French town, was "only one remove away" from ideas thousands of miles distant in Siam and Tibet. Perhaps it is no coincidence that Hume's empiricism and skepticism about the self strikingly recall Buddhism.
    • [00:11:22] Hume's Treatise was also influenced by Nicolas Malebranche and Pierre Bayle, who in turn knew something of Chinese philosophy. Malebranche wrote a Conversation Between a Christian Philosopher and a Chinese Philosopher (1708); Bayle's Historical and Critical Dictionary (1703) described Chinese philosophy essentially via the Buddha's life and thought. Again a skeptical attitude toward the soul comes to the fore — another conduit for the Buddhist no-self doctrine into Europe.

    Francis Bernier and the spider¶

    • [00:11:58] Yet another source was the well-travelled Francis Bernier, who had been to India and served as court physician to the Mughal prince Dārā Shukoh. Bernier exchanged ideas with one of the court intellectuals who had helped translate the Upaniṣads.
    • [00:12:17] Writing from Shiraz in 1667, Bernier said his three-year collaboration with a pandit — facilitated by his own Persian translations of Gassendi and Descartes — let him report many things from Sanskrit books though he knew no Sanskrit.
    • [00:12:41] Here too a link to Hume: Bernier publicized the Indian metaphor comparing God to a spider that extends filaments from itself and then withdraws them, so that creation will ultimately collapse back into God. In his Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion Hume cites this analogy and ascribes it to "the Brahmins" — but is dismissive, calling it a cosmogony that "appears to us ridiculous, because a spider is a little contemptible animal" whose operations we'd never take as a model for the universe.

    19th-c. Indology: Colebrooke, the Mills, the Schlegels¶

    • [00:13:17] In the 19th century Europeans finally caught up with al-Bīrūnī by making Indology a serious enterprise. A key figure was Henry T. Colebrooke, who translated mathematical and philosophical works from India into English.
    • [00:13:35] A tantalizing connection runs from Colebrooke to John Stuart Mill. One might expect Mill to know much about India — his father James Mill was an East India Company administrator and author of the colonial-minded History of British India. Yet the younger Mill's philosophy makes no explicit use of Indian thought. He would, though, probably have known Colebrooke's 1827 lecture on the materialist theory of mind of the Cārvāka school.
    • [00:14:05] This Cārvāka theory is comparable to emergentism in philosophy of mind — and Mill sounds rather like a follower of Bṛhaspati, the school's founder, when he writes in his System of Logic that "all organized bodies are composed of parts similar to those composing inorganic nature… the phenomena of life result from the juxtaposition of those parts in a certain manner."
    • [00:14:42] Indology was also emerging in Germany with the Schlegel brothers: Friedrich Schlegel learned Persian and Sanskrit and published On the Language and Wisdom of the Indians, while his brother August Wilhelm Schlegel took up the first German chair of Indology in Bonn, 1818.

    Hegel: extravagant praise, casual dismissal¶

    • [00:14:58] The Indologists' impact shows in Hegel, who knew Colebrooke's essays (published in the Royal Asiatic Society's transactions, 1824) and reviewed August Wilhelm Schlegel's work on the Bhagavad-Gītā — putting him in a position (or at least the impression of one) to assess India's philosophical contribution.
    • [00:15:25] For Hegel the Indians had reached only a primitive stage in the development of spirit (Geist): a "completely abstract notion of substance as a single underlying principle," identified with an "equally abstract and empty subjecthood." Once again Indian philosophy is reduced to a monolith, with all weight placed on the monist strand of Brahminical thought.
    • [00:15:50] His verdict combines extravagant praise with casual dismissal: in the Oriental world "we do find philosophizing too, indeed the most profound philosophizing — but insofar as it remains the most profound, it remains also abstract. For us, the real philosophy begins only in Greece."

    Schopenhauer: "The Buddha, Eckhart, and I"¶

    • [00:16:13] A more positive assessment comes from Schopenhauer, who on occasion called himself a Buddhist and was hugely impressed by the Upaniṣads — which he read in an 1801 Latin translation based on the Persian version of (you guessed it) Dārā Shukoh. Of this text he said it was "the most profitable and sublime reading that is possible in the world… the consolation of my life and will be that of my death."
    • [00:16:45] Whether admiration meant real influence is hard to say. He claimed agreement with Buddhism and with the medieval mystic Meister Eckhart, writing: "The Buddha, Eckhart, and I all teach essentially the same" — Eckhart bound by Christian mythology, Buddhism clearer for lacking it, and "complete clarity lies with me." Yet he insisted that when he began The World as Will and Representation he was as yet unacquainted with the Buddhist philosophy that resonated with his system.
    • [00:17:34] Like the others, Schopenhauer still lumped Indian traditions together as one doctrine. Our own tour, by contrast, has shown a stark opposition between Vedānta's monism of consciousness and Buddhist skepticism about the self. Schopenhauer saw little difference between union with the Upaniṣadic brahman and the Buddhist liberation through abandonment of will: for him brahman was simply the will of his own philosophy — a self or subject able to view things as a whole, sub specie aeternitatis.

    Wittgenstein, and the abiding distortion¶

    • [00:18:07] That idea was later taken over by Wittgenstein, who writes in the Tractatus: "The view of the world sub specie aeternitatis is the view of it as a limited whole. The feeling of the world as a limited whole is the mystical feeling."
    • [00:18:32] But this does not do justice to Schopenhauer's Indian sources. As the earlier Upaniṣad episodes argued, the Upaniṣadic brahman concerns the phenomenology of consciousness — the underlying subject of thought and perception, not a principle of agency or willing.
    • [00:18:50] This is typical of the European reception. By Schopenhauer's day vastly more was known about Indian philosophy than the Greeks could ever have known — the Sanskrit translations missing in antiquity were now available, and scholars like Colebrooke and the Schlegels gave India sustained, careful attention. Yet with Hume, Hegel, and Schopenhauer we still see Europeans influenced as much by what they assumed Indian philosophy should say as by what it really said.

    Into the 20th century — and where we go next¶

    • [00:19:18] There is much more to the engagement between Europe and ancient India; only a few famous names have been touched, and the story has not reached the 20th century, when two holders of Oxford's Spalding Professorship — Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan and Bimal Krishna Matilal — worked to win British philosophers' attention for India.
    • [00:19:45] Radhakrishnan's effort to showcase India's tradition of philosophical idealism was mistimed — British idealism was already falling out of favour. Matilal had greater success introducing his Oxford colleagues to Indian epistemology: Peter Strawson, Derek Parfit, and Michael Dummett all paid tribute to his brilliance and to the Indian ideas he brought them — for Parfit the Buddhist analysis of persons, for Strawson the descriptive metaphysics of Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika.
    • [00:20:24] In the 21st century there is "really no excuse" to imagine, with Hegel, that real philosophy began only with the Greeks. In that spirit, next time the series draws to a close: a quick review of philosophical developments in India since antiquity, and final thoughts on the contemporary relevance of the history of philosophy in India.

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • al-Bīrūnī — medieval scientist at Maḥmūd of Ghazna's court; his Treatise on India (1030) was the first richly informed comparative study of Indian thought for Arabic readers, but flattened India into one doctrine.
    • Suśruta / Sindhind / Kalīla wa-Dimna — early Indian works that reached Arabic: a medical text, an astronomical handbook (commissioned by caliph al-Manṣūr), and animal fables derived from the Pañcatantra.
    • Barmakids — late-8th-c. caliphal family from Bactria's Ṭukhāristān (where Buddhism and Sanskrit still flourished) who sponsored the transmission of Indian science.
    • Ibn al-Nadīm — 10th-c. bibliophile; recorded al-Kindī copying an Indian document and called the Buddhists Shamaniyya (from śramaṇa, "renouncer").
    • pramāṇas — Indian "sources/means of knowledge"; awareness of them shows in the Buddhist demand that Jahm ibn Ṣafwān ground belief in God empirically.
    • Yoga-Sūtra / Sāṃkhya-kārikā / Bhagavad-Gītā — Sanskrit texts al-Bīrūnī translated or used (with Gauḍapāda's commentary), reading them as one teaching of union with God.
    • Dārā Shukoh — 17th-c. Mughal prince; translated the Upaniṣads into Persian and wrote Confluence of the Oceans on Indian–Islamic (Sufi) agreement; his version seeded the Latin text Schopenhauer read.
    • Locke vs. Leibniz — Locke mocked Indian "substance" via the earth-on-elephant-on-tortoise image; Leibniz defended it as fertile for philosophy.
    • David Hume — possibly exposed to Buddhism via La Flèche (Dolu, Desideri); his no-self skepticism and empiricism recall Buddhism; cites and dismisses the Indian "God as spider" analogy.
    • Francis Bernier — physician to Dārā Shukoh; relayed Sanskrit ideas (via a pandit) to Europe, including the spider cosmogony.
    • Cārvāka / Bṛhaspati — Indian materialist school (its founder Bṛhaspati); its theory of mind, publicized by Colebrooke (1827), is comparable to emergentism and echoed by J. S. Mill.
    • Hegel — judged India only a "primitive stage" of Geist; "real philosophy begins only in Greece"; praise mixed with dismissal.
    • Schopenhauer — called himself a Buddhist; revered the Upaniṣads; "The Buddha, Eckhart, and I teach essentially the same"; wrongly equated brahman with his will (the self viewed sub specie aeternitatis).
    • Wittgenstein — inherited the sub specie aeternitatis "world as a limited whole = mystical feeling" idea in the Tractatus.
    • Radhakrishnan & Matilal — 20th-c. Oxford Spalding Professors who pressed India on British philosophy; Matilal's Indian epistemology influenced Strawson (Nyāya/Vaiśeṣika), Parfit (Buddhist persons), and Dummett.

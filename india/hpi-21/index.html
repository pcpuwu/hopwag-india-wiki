    HoPWaG India · Ep 21 — "Innocent Until Proven Guilty: Mīmāṃsā on Knowledge and Language"¶

    ← Ep 20

    Ep 22 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:20:00 In brief: How the Mīmāṃsā school defends the absolute authority of the Veda — not by appealing to a divine author but by denying the Veda has any author at all — and underpins this with a hard-nosed, empiricist epistemology ("cognitions are innocent until proven guilty") and a theory that language itself is beginningless. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Gannery"; Mīmāṃsā ≈ "Mimamsa"; Mīmāṃsaka(s) ≈ "Mimamsakas"; Jaimini ≈ "Jiamini"; pramāṇa ≈ "Pramana/promana"; Veda ≈ "Vedah"; Śabara ≈ "Shabara/Shavarazya"; Kumārila Bhaṭṭa ≈ "Kumara-Bata/Kumaraela/Kumadela"; svataḥ prāmāṇya ≈ "svata pramagna"; Prabhākara ≈ "Prabakara"; Nyāya ≈ "Niyaya"; Pāṇini ≈ "Panani"; dharma ≈ "Dharma"; Dārā Shukōh ≈ "Dada Shiku"; Elisa Freschi ≈ "Eliza Fresci").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • The Veda(s) — Mīmāṃsā's holy books, held to be authorless and beginningless, the unique source of knowledge about dharma [00:01:15].
    • Jaimini — author of the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra, source of the school's empiricist account of perception [00:02:52].
    • Śabara — author of the authoritative early commentary on Jaimini [00:04:27].
    • Kumārila Bhaṭṭa — commentator who develops the "intrinsic validity" (svataḥ prāmāṇya) doctrine [00:05:08].
    • Prabhākara — the rival commentator; the Mīmāṃsā split follows him vs. Kumārila on words and sentences [00:14:18].
    • Elisa Freschi — the scholar who will be next episode's guest on Mīmāṃsā [00:19:54].

    Revelation vs. an authorless scripture¶

    • [00:00:37] The episode opens with the Islamic idea that God sends different revelations to different peoples — the Torah for the Jews, the Gospels for the Christians, and finally and definitively the Quran for the Muslims. This was extended to India: Buddhists and Hindus, too, were treated as "peoples of the book," and the Muslim prince Dārā Shukōh, living in India in the 17th century, even claimed to detect an allusion to the Upaniṣads inside the Quran itself.
    • [00:01:08] But Mīmāṃsā would reject this well-meaning analogy. Where Torah, Gospels and Quran are thought to be sent to humankind by God through divine revelation, the Mīmāṃsakas held that their holy books, the Vedas, have no author at all. The Veda did not come from the gods or from any prophet, and it was not sent. It is permanent — in the sense of having always existed, or having no beginning.
    • [00:01:37] Since the Veda consists of words and sentences, this implies that language itself is permanent — a consequence the Mīmāṃsakas were more than happy to embrace.

    Why deny the Veda an author?¶

    • [00:01:50] This idea (raised already last time) is part of the Mīmāṃsā attempt to establish unimpeachable authority for the Veda. But why adopt such a radical proposal — one rejected even by other Brahminical schools like Nyāya?
    • [00:02:06] The short answer: if the Veda is permanent and has no author, then it cannot contain any errors. Errors only arise where there is someone — an author — in a position to make a mistake. With no author, the Veda must be entirely trustworthy.
    • [00:02:24] The long answer is the rest of the episode: it requires looking at Mīmāṃsā's ideas about knowledge and language.

    The empiricist foundation: perception as a pramāṇa¶

    • [00:02:32] The Mīmāṃsakas ground their account of the Veda in an empiricist and naturalist account of human knowledge. Roughly, their reasons for accepting the truth of the Veda are the same kind of reasons you have for accepting that you are hearing this voice right now, and that the objects around you are what they seem to be.
    • [00:02:52] The roots lie in a passage of the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra where Jaimini writes: "Perception is the knowledge which one has by the senses coming into contact with something existent." He straightforwardly endorses that sense perception gives accurate information about things. In later terms, sensation is a pramāṇa — a source of knowledge or justification.
    • [00:03:20] But (as we know from earlier discussion of skepticism in ancient India) such claims won't go unchallenged — and the skeptic looks especially well placed against this pramāṇa. She need only point out that sensation is often misleading: the stuff cooking in the pan looks like rice but is actually tapioca; that man looks like Jim Rice who played baseball for the Red Sox, but it's actually his twin brother.

    "Rice-in-the-pan" reasoning and the skeptic's leverage¶

    • [00:03:48] The empiricist can reply that sensation is very rarely misleading, so we can generally depend on it. But the Mīmāṃsakas seem badly placed to say this, because they themselves are happy to use so-called "rice-in-the-pan" reasoning in other contexts: they say all the rice is cooked if one grain is soft, and that the details of one ritual can be transferred to another.
    • [00:04:12] So how can they stop the skeptic from arguing in the same way — from the misleading nature of one sense experience to the doubtfulness of sensation as a whole?

    Innocent until proven guilty¶

    • [00:04:22] The Mīmāṃsā answer: sensations are innocent until proven guilty. The idea enters the commentary tradition early, before Śabara's authoritative commentary on Jaimini. We should take every cognition to be true unless it is trumped by another cognition that overturns it.
    • [00:04:39] The commentators, including Śabara, readily admit many factors can undermine a sensation: you might taste the stuff in the pan and find it too sweet to be rice, or you might be drunk or deranged (which undermines belief generally). Today philosophers would call such things defeaters. In the absence of any defeater, the perception should be taken as true.

    Kumārila and intrinsic validity (svataḥ prāmāṇya)¶

    • [00:05:08] Developing this, Kumārila Bhaṭṭa states in his commentary on Śabara that sensations possess svataḥ prāmāṇya — intrinsic validity. Later Mīmāṃsakas couldn't agree on what this meant.
    • [00:05:23] A minority read it as: a sensation is valid so long as it arose in a reliable way — provided you're not in fact deranged or drunk, your cognitions are reliable. It's then up to each of us to check whether a cognition arose trustworthily, and if so, to trust it.
    • [00:05:45] Others took Kumārila to mean valid sensations simply present themselves as true. If you see rice in a bowl in normal conditions, you have no hesitation in taking yourself to know there's rice there; only a warning sign — a strange taste, an awareness of being drunk — would even prompt doubt.
    • [00:06:08] This second reading is "exactly right," per Kumārila: "That cognition which is unshaken and does not conflict with cognitions occurring at other times and places is a pramāṇa — a source of knowledge." In normal conditions we should not fret about possible falsehood, and certainly not surrender to the skeptic's worry that all cognitions may be unreliable.

    Turning the skeptic's regress against her¶

    • [00:06:34] But isn't this just an unjustified leap of faith? It's no good for Kumārila to insist dogmatically that we can trust perception — that's precisely what the skeptic denies, and she has reason, since perceptions are occasionally misleading.
    • [00:06:51] The Mīmāṃsakas have another move: borrow a weapon from the skeptic's own arsenal. If we demand confirmation for every cognition, a regress looms — each cognition needs another to confirm it. If I double-check the bowl is rice by tasting it, how do I know I'm tasting accurately? I could ask you to taste it too, but your agreement is also open to challenge.
    • [00:07:28] The skeptic draws the moral that no pramāṇa is reliable. Mīmāṃsā draws the reverse: there must be some foundational cognitions, since otherwise we could never be confident of knowing anything — yet of course we are confident in having knowledge.

    Back to the authorless Veda¶

    • [00:07:44] Now return to the Veda having no author. Though this became a distinctive Mīmāṃsā claim, it doesn't appear explicitly in Jaimini, who says only that words are always prior to their speakers — which can be taken to imply that the Veda, and language in general, are permanent.
    • [00:08:03] The Veda's absolute authority can only be challenged on the basis of some defeater. Had it been authored, we could always fear the author — even a divine one — made a mistake. We trust sensation unless we have reason to think it arose defectively; we trust the Veda in exactly the same way, except that here there can be no worry it arose defectively, because it did not arise at all.
    • [00:08:37] It may seem this buys the Veda's authority too cheaply — its validity is supreme simply because it has no competition, so it wins by default. But put yourself in Jaimini's or Kumārila's position. The Vedic tradition was under fire from Buddhists, Jainas and others, so the Mīmāṃsakas were not oblivious to religious pluralism — yet Kumārila was confident the alternatives could be exposed as invalid. The critics of Vedic ritual had given no good reasons to doubt the ritual's efficacy (or none the Mīmāṃsakas rated). And if there were good reasons to doubt, we would know about them.
    • [00:09:24] Kumārila is so confident in the sources of knowledge that for him the absence of a cognition is itself a reason to reject a thing, given favorable conditions: if you're positioned to know a pan's contents yet have no evidence of rice there, that is reason to believe there's no rice. Likewise, there is no cognition — nor even the possibility of one — that could overturn Vedic authority. With no defeaters in sight, Mīmāṃsā declares victory.

    Against the Buddhists: an enduring self¶

    • [00:09:58] The stance has other implications, including renewed conflict with the Buddhists. Where Buddhism denies an enduring self, Mīmāṃsā insists on it. We recognize automatically that there's an "I" who experienced the things we remember and feel that this is still who we are: just as I see a dirty pan in the sink waiting to be washed, so I remember myself eating the rice prepared in that pan.
    • [00:10:28] Making short shrift of the Buddhists' nuanced critique, Mīmāṃsā simply assumes we are right to take ourselves as enduring subjects of experience, since there is nothing to defeat that impression — and indeed nothing could overturn the impression that one has a self.

    Language has no beginning¶

    • [00:10:46] The most far-reaching and celebrated part of the view on the beginninglessness of the Veda is the inference that language itself must have no beginning.
    • [00:10:58] One argument: children learn language by watching adults use language. We have no evidence things were ever otherwise, so — basing ourselves on experience — we may assume language, like the Veda, has always been passed down from generation to generation.
    • [00:11:21] A second argument that things cannot be otherwise. Take the word "cow," Sanskrit go. (Aside: you can tell a lot about a culture by what it means by the syllable "go" — for ancient Indians, cow; in Japan, a board game of almost infinite complexity; for Americans, where you start when building a monopoly.) The word "cow" refers not to any particular cow but to cows universally, and when I say it on many occasions I'm using the same word over and over, not a new word each time. Or, appealing to Mīmāṃsā epistemology: it seems I'm using the same word repeatedly, and there's no reason to doubt it, so we take it as true.

    The "no way to introduce language as such" argument¶

    • [00:12:10] The argument may seem unconvincing, since it assumes "cow" has either always existed or is a new word every utterance. There's a third possibility: each word is introduced at some point, then used with a stable meaning by convention and agreement.
    • [00:12:32] Against this, Mīmāṃsā argues that even if one can learn the meaning of individual new words, there is no way to introduce language as such. It's impossible to learn to speak or understand speech without already engaging in and observing linguistic activity. Children learning to talk must discover each word's meaning by watching their elders; once competent, we can learn new words by hearing them explained in other words — but it's inconceivable the whole process could have begun from a situation with no language at all.

    Universal meaning vs. "bring the cow"¶

    • [00:13:11] A critic resists: on the theory, words have universal meaning, yet if I tell my ritual assistant "bring the cow" (a standard Mīmāṃsā example), I mean a specific cow. How, if "cow" refers universally?
    • [00:13:38] The answer: a word on its own cannot refer to anything particular. For that you need context — at least the rest of the sentence, and probably surrounding sentences and circumstances. By putting "bring the" in front of "cow," as I'm erecting a sacrificial post, I get my assistant to fetch the particular cow about to be tied to the post and sacrificed.

    Kumārila vs. Prabhākara: words and sentences¶

    • [00:14:05] That satisfies the critic but not the Mīmāṃsakas themselves. As the tradition developed, a dispute arose over the relation between words and sentences — one aspect of a split following the commentators Kumārila and Prabhākara.
    • [00:14:24] Kumārila accepts that words have a general meaning standing alone — the utterance "cow" does signify something — but that meaning is affected by being placed in a complete sentence, connecting words to produce a new composite meaning. His view is the "theory of the connection of what has been expressed."
    • [00:14:48] Prabhākara holds that words must appear within a sentence to have any meaning at all — the "theory of expression through what has been connected." This seems implausible (surely "cow" alone calls something to mind), but shouldn't be dismissed: many words lack fixed meaning apart from context. Consider "at" across "the cow is at the sacrificial post," "we will sacrifice her at sunrise," "the cow looked at me with her big brown eyes, and all at once I decided I couldn't go through with it."

    Ritual injunctions and the centrality of the verb¶

    • [00:15:36] Prabhākara's view fits Mīmāṃsā's overall aim: inquiry into ritual injunctions like "one who desires heaven should perform the fire ritual." The injunction is the most important of the four kinds of statement in the Veda — the others being the mantra (formula spoken during the ritual), the statement that indicates a name, and the explanation of a goal pursued in ritual.
    • [00:16:04] Jaimini stated that a ritual injunction is a meaningful unity composed of its words — and not just any string will do. Here Mīmāṃsā draws on Sanskrit grammar, carrying forward Pāṇini's idea that the action expressed by the verb is central to a sentence's meaning. The distinctions can be subtle — e.g., verbs go in the middle voice when the action is performed by the sacrificer (for himself) and not by the attending priest.

    Injunctions point to an unobservable future — to dharma¶

    • [00:16:40] A more basic point returns to Mīmāṃsā's core mission, inquiry into Vedic language. Where everyday language typically describes the world we see, an injunction inevitably refers to something not yet present — the goal sought in the ritual. Because ritual injunctions are directed to the future, their validity is not grounded in sense perception. When the Veda tells you how to sacrifice to have children, get rich, or reach heaven, it looks to an unobservable future reward — distinctive, even definitive, of the language that reveals dharma.
    • [00:17:27] This gives another reason the Veda can't be overturned: how could sensation conflict with the Veda, when sensation tells us nothing about the questions at stake in ritual — questions of dharma?

    Ordinary words, no supernaturalism¶

    • [00:17:44] Despite this, Mīmāṃsā insists the Veda's words are used in their normal sense: "tie a cow to a post" is not allegorical or extended — it calls a cow a cow and a post a post. Still, the Veda is a unique source of knowledge, one that could only be delivered as language, since we need language to articulate goals not yet present and explain how to reach them.
    • [00:18:20] This is typical Mīmāṃsā: a theory devoted to interpreting the sacred Veda, yet offering an epistemology grounded in sense experience and an analysis of language that applies just as well to sentences uttered by cowherds (assuming the cowherds speak Sanskrit). It makes no appeal to supernatural knowledge and impatiently rejects such claims by others — dismissing that the Buddha was omniscient, and that yoga confers special insight. Such supposed cognitions have no basis in normal experience, and it's normal experience we should follow, along with testimony in the form of language — unless we have good reason not to.

    A scriptural empiricism: Augustine meets Hume¶

    • [00:19:05] Mīmāṃsā exemplifies Indian philosophy's potential to overturn assumptions natural to European traditions. We may suppose that devotion to scriptural authority must come bundled with belief in higher supernatural cognition and in a revelation that erupts into history, dividing an ignorant past from a new age graced with previously inaccessible truth.
    • [00:19:30] But no European thinker beats Mīmāṃsā for respect for scriptural authority, yet its adherents vehemently rejected the rest of that supernaturalist picture — fusing a hermeneutics worthy of Augustine with an empiricism so hard-nosed it might impress David Hume.
    • [00:19:47] Reason enough to give the tradition one more episode: next time the guest is Elisa Freschi, "an outstanding interpreter of this school of interpretation."

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • Mīmāṃsā / Mīmāṃsakas — the school devoted to interpreting the Veda; defends its supreme authority via empiricist epistemology and the doctrine that the Veda (and language) is authorless and beginningless.
    • Veda — Mīmāṃsā's holy books; held to have no author and no beginning, hence to contain no errors and to be the unique source of knowledge about dharma.
    • Jaimini — author of the Mīmāṃsā-Sūtra; defines perception as knowledge from the senses contacting "something existent," and says words are prior to their speakers.
    • Śabara — author of the authoritative early commentary on Jaimini; admits factors (defeaters) can undermine sensation.
    • Kumārila Bhaṭṭa — commentator; champions svataḥ prāmāṇya (intrinsic validity) and holds that words have meaning alone but combine into a composite sentence-meaning ("connection of what has been expressed").
    • Prabhākara — rival commentator; holds words have meaning only within a sentence ("expression through what has been connected").
    • pramāṇa — a source of knowledge/justification; for Mīmāṃsā, perception is one, valid unless defeated.
    • svataḥ prāmāṇya — "intrinsic validity"; a cognition is valid in itself (because reliably produced, or because it presents itself as true) absent a defeater.
    • defeater — modern term the episode uses for anything (odd taste, drunkenness, derangement) that overturns a cognition; the Veda is uniquely immune since it never "arose."
    • "innocent until proven guilty" — Mīmāṃsā's epistemic principle: take every cognition as true unless trumped by another cognition.
    • dharma — the subject of Vedic injunctions; directed to an unobservable future reward, so beyond the reach of sense perception (which therefore can never contradict the Veda).
    • ritual injunction — most important of the Veda's four statement-types (with mantra, name-indicating statement, goal-explanation); a meaningful unity of words centered, after Pāṇini, on the action of the verb.
    • go ("cow") — the worked example: a word referring to cows universally, needing sentential context to pick out a particular cow ("bring the cow").
    • Pāṇini — grammarian whose principle that the verb's action is central to sentence-meaning is carried into Mīmāṃsā's analysis of injunctions.
    • Elisa Freschi — Mīmāṃsā scholar, the next episode's guest.

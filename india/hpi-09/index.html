    HoPWaG India · Ep 9 — "Suffering and Smiling: The Buddha"¶

    ← Ep 8

    Ep 10 →

    🎧 Listen: official page · 👤 Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri 📄 Read the full transcript

    Speakers: Peter Adamson & Jonardon Ganeri · Runtime: 00:21:49 In brief: The Buddha's diagnosis of the human condition — the Four Noble Truths — built on the claim that suffering is universal, that its cause is craving rooted in ignorance, and that escape requires the radical realization that there is no self to be liberated; with the chariot and the five skandhas as the classic illustrations of how a "person" is only a convenient name for an aggregate. Narration order; [hh:mm:ss] = audio jump point. Sanskrit/Pali terms normalized to standard transliteration; obvious speech-to-text slips fixed against context (Jonardon Ganeri ≈ "Janardin Ganari"; duḥkha ≈ "Dukka/Dukkah"; taṇhā ≈ "Tana"; Ājīvakas ≈ "Ajivacus/Ajivakas/Ajifakas/Ajivokas"; Tripiṭaka ≈ "Tripitaka"; Vinaya Piṭaka ≈ "Vinaya pittaka"; Abhidharma ≈ "Abidharma"; Milindapañha ≈ "Melinda Panya"; Nāgasena ≈ "Nagasena"; skandhas ≈ "scandas/scantas"; Pudgala/Pudgalavāda ≈ "Pugala/Pugalavada"; Nāgārjuna ≈ "Nagajuna").

    Thinkers & texts introduced¶

    • The Buddha — 5th-c. BCE royal prince of the kṣatriya class who renounced his comfort, found enlightenment under a tree, and taught the Four Noble Truths [00:00:28].
    • The Pali canon — earliest writings on the Buddha, in the Pali language; its three "baskets" are the Tripiṭaka [00:03:16].
    • The Nikāyas — collections of the Buddha's sayings and conversations, part of the canon [00:03:32].
    • The Milindapañha ("Questions of Menander") — early Buddhist text staging the sage Nāgasena with the Hellenistic king Menander of Bactria (2nd c. BCE) [00:13:47].

    The first step: admitting the problem¶

    • [00:00:43] "The first step is admitting you have a problem" — advice aimed at addicts, but for the Buddha it applies to all of us. His teaching centres on the Four Noble Truths, and the first is that everyone has a problem so pervasive and so hard to escape that we usually fail even to perceive it as a problem.
    • [00:01:03] The problem is that we are alive, and life is suffering. That is the bad news. The worse news: death is no escape. In keeping with the doctrine of rebirth and karmic retribution, death frees us only from our current portion of suffering and delivers us directly into another.
    • [00:01:20] How can we possibly free ourselves? Only by understanding the predicament better — which crucially includes coming to understand that we have no selves that need to be set free in the first place. With this, the Buddha signalled his disagreement with the Brahminical tradition of the Upaniṣads, which made ātman (the self) its central preoccupation.

    A royal life renounced — the legend of the Buddha¶

    • [00:01:50] The Buddha was no Brahmin. The elaborate legend of his former lives and final incarnation says he chose to be born into the kṣatriya class as a royal prince. His mother dreamed of a white elephant entering the side of her body and conceived the child who would become the Buddha.
    • [00:02:10] At birth he could already speak and walk. His first deed: he took seven steps and proclaimed that life is full of sorrow and that, after innumerable life-cycles, he had come to free both gods and men from this misery.
    • [00:02:21] Despite this precocious declaration, he spent his youth and early adulthood oblivious of his mission, growing up in comfort and luxury. He was set on the path to enlightenment only when, as a young man, he ventured beyond the palace walls and was confronted for the first time by the elderly, the sick, and the dead.
    • [00:02:42] He retreated to the forest and took up the extreme ascetic practices associated with the Ājīvakas, only to find them ineffective. Rejecting that path, he sat beneath a tree and began to meditate. When enlightenment came, he did not keep his new wisdom to himself but gathered students — the first Buddhists, and far from the last.

    The sources: Pali canon, Nikāyas, the three baskets¶

    • [00:03:10] These details and far more are contained in the earliest writings about the Buddha, the Pali canon, so called because written in the Pali language. As with the Upaniṣads, this is an originally oral tradition, set down in writing only centuries after the Buddha's lifetime — probably the 5th century BCE.
    • [00:03:32] His sayings and conversations are collected in the Nikāyas, themselves only one of three bodies of early Buddhist literature, the Tripiṭaka ("three baskets"). The other two: the Vinaya Piṭaka, giving instructions for monks, and the most philosophical, the Abhidharma ("higher teaching").
    • [00:03:58] The Pali canon is the most obvious source but not the only one: there are Chinese biographies of the Buddha from the 2nd–4th centuries CE, and important architectural and iconographical material — notably the ancient burial mounds called stupas, surrounded by decorative railings and statues.
    • [00:04:23] You probably don't need an archaeologist to conjure the familiar image: the Buddha sitting cross-legged, eyes half-shut, smiling beatifically. Which raises the question — what's with the smiling? Why so serene when your message is that life is suffering? It turns out there's good news: three more Noble Truths, concerning the cause of suffering, what escape would mean, and the path to that escape.

    Is the first truth even plausible?¶

    • [00:04:58] Before the solution, examine the problem. Is the Buddha's bleak assessment plausible? Life does involve disease and (if you're lucky) old age, with death as the inevitable conclusion — but in the meantime there's plenty to enjoy: the silent films of Buster Keaton, the occasional almond croissant, philosophy podcasts. The Buddha had none of these, but presumably antique India offered things almost equally worthwhile.
    • [00:05:30] More seriously, set the first truth in its historical setting. Life in pre-modern societies (and less affluent places today) is precarious and often unpleasant — the story of the Buddha shocked by what lay beyond the palace is telling. And other spiritual movements of his time were equally pessimistic: the Jains and Ājīvakas sought to escape the cycle of rebirth entirely rather than aim for the best rebirth.
    • [00:06:12] Some propose a less dramatic translation of the central term duḥkha — not "suffering" but "dissatisfaction." Unfortunately that wouldn't make the teaching more plausible: the problem must be not only inevitable but severe — severe enough to warrant restructuring one's entire life around the Buddhist path. "Into each life some rain must fall, but that doesn't mean we should think of nothing but finding an umbrella."

    The second truth: desire as the cause¶

    • [00:06:49] The second Noble Truth reveals the cause of suffering: in a word, desire — lusting for pleasure, coveting possessions, wanting to avoid death. Both our longing for some things and our aversion to others fall under taṇhā, the general term for attached desire or craving.
    • [00:07:20] Tellingly, the counterproductive goals include the very goods that Brahminical ritual was meant to achieve, such as a large family. For the Buddhist these guarantee duḥkha: before you have them you suffer from unfulfilled craving (and the stronger the desire, the worse), and once you have them you suffer too, fearing their loss. In the longer term a desire-driven life keeps us stuck in the cycle of reincarnation, each rebirth merely another chance to suffer.
    • [00:07:57] This is why the Buddha gave up his life as a prince and, more importantly, the values it entailed — however cosseted, he would still face illness, age, and death. Following him, many Buddhists abandoned the life of the householder (a man devoted to family and material prosperity) to become renouncers living monastically — though Buddhism is also followed by laypeople who support the monks.

    The middle way¶

    • [00:08:32] But "renouncer" can mislead. Recall that the Buddha tried extreme asceticism and found it did not bring enlightenment. He taught instead a middle way between the householding life and the self-abnegation of some anti-Brahminical traditions.
    • [00:08:50] This is highlighted at the very start of his first sermon, which urges students to avoid two extremes: on one side a life devoted to passion, on the other deliberate self-torment and pain. Both, says the Buddha, are ignoble and useless.

    Salvation through knowledge — the chain from ignorance¶

    • [00:09:10] Though rejecting the Brahmins' ideas and values, the Buddha shared one thing with them: the belief that salvation comes through knowledge. The cause of suffering is desire, but desire has a further cause — ignorance.
    • [00:09:28] A chapter of the same Nikāya that transmits the first sermon explains the chain: ignorance gives rise to karma, karma to consciousness, which leads to sensation and therefore to desire; desire in turn leads to rebirth, and once born you are subject to death, age, and grief. The only way off this "train of tears" is to avoid boarding at the first station — by replacing ignorance with understanding.
    • [00:09:59] This understanding has nothing to do with propitiating the gods. Vedic sacrifices are no more effective than the asceticism of other dissident groups. The Buddhists accepted that the gods are real, but the gods too are subject to rebirth — so even the gods need liberation.
    • [00:10:25] Another difference: where the Upaniṣads claim to expose hidden truths and correspondences underlying everyday reality, early Buddhism seeks to unmask reality as merely conventional. This brings us to the third and fourth truths — how life would need to be to escape suffering, and how to get there.

    The third and fourth truths: no-self and nirvāṇa¶

    • [00:10:50] The Buddha's recommendation is as simple as it is radical: you can stop being attached to things that falsely seem to benefit you, now and in the future, if you give up the idea that you have a self that can be benefited. Through embracing and acting on this theory of no-self we reach nirvāṇa, liberation from all suffering.
    • [00:11:15] An apparent contradiction: we're told to give up all desire — but what about the desire for liberation? Couldn't that be just another craving, replacing one desire with another, "as if he told us we could stay dry by coming in out of the rain to take a shower"?
    • [00:11:45] The project need not be self-defeating. The whole teaching is predicated on the desire to avoid suffering. Once we learn that suffering comes from cravings and fears, that initial desire should be transformed into a more useful one: we should desire to lose all desire. This new desire is vital to reaching the goal but may fall away once all other desires are eliminated — when liberation comes, the desire for it no longer serves any purpose. As one scholar put it, "craving is a stepping stone to getting rid of craving altogether."

    The trap of wanting annihilation¶

    • [00:12:26] It looks as if there's at least one other desire the Buddha would approve: the desire to annihilate ourselves, since, like the Jains and Ājīvakas, he wants to help us escape the karmic cycle. But that would be a mistake.
    • [00:12:46] In the first sermon the Buddha lists among the desires that lead to suffering not only the longing to continue existing but also the longing to cease existing. The Buddhist way is instead to realize there is no self to annihilate.
    • [00:13:04] This is diametrically opposed to Brahminism. The Buddhists reject the idea of some unseen, unchanging centre of identity underlying all cognition and awareness. That still leaves room for less metaphysically ambitious notions of self: you needn't accept a permanent self to believe there's some sense in which you're the same person who started listening a few minutes ago, or that you can be aware of yourself listening. Still, the Buddhists clearly wanted to critique and revise our everyday assumptions about the reality of persons and things.

    The chariot and the fist: persons as conventional aggregates¶

    • [00:13:47] A classic text on the issue is the Milindapañha, which depicts the sage Nāgasena in conversation with the Hellenistic king Menander, ruler of Bactria in the 2nd century BCE. The setting recalls the Upaniṣads, but the doctrine does not.
    • [00:14:07] Nāgasena tries to persuade the king that familiar objects are whole substances only by convention. His example: a chariot. There is nothing more to it than its parts — axle, yoke, wheels, reins, and so on. This doesn't mean we can't truthfully say "here is a chariot"; it means "chariot" is a conventional word, a term we find convenient for the whole aggregate of parts.
    • [00:14:40] Another nice example from the Buddhist literature: a fist, evidently nothing more than the hand and fingers temporarily clenched. And so with people too — people are aggregates that can lose and gain parts over time.

    What the parts are: dharmas and the five skandhas¶

    • [00:14:52] A Brahminical sage might respond that every aggregate is made of constituents, those of further constituents — a paragraph of sentences, sentences of words — but in the end we reach fundamental parts, like the letters of the words. So if we seek the fundamental realities making up a person, why not identify the self as one such reality?
    • [00:15:24] Because the Buddhist has a rival answer. The Buddhists applied the name dharma to the simple components of any aggregate. In the case of the person there are five types of dharma. To stress their merely aggregative nature, these are called skandhas ("heaps").
    • [00:15:43] The five skandhas cover all aspects of our being: physical form, bodily feelings, emotions/perceptions, thinking, and consciousness itself. The list sounds familiar because in the Upaniṣads these are precisely the phenomena that conceal the more fundamental self hidden within. But for the Buddhists there is nothing to a person over and above the conglomeration of momentary skandhas — each present only for a fleeting moment before being replaced by others of the same type.
    • [00:16:20] Nor would it be right to identify yourself with any skandha, individually or in aggregate — that would again be attachment to the self. There are just the skandhas, and that is it. So we arrive at perhaps the most notorious doctrine of Buddhism: we are nothing more than flowing streams, with no firm identity from moment to moment. This will later be defended, and the Brahminical unchanging self attacked, with great virtuosity by Nāgārjuna — but it already emerges in the Pali canon, often when discussing reincarnation.

    The puzzle of rebirth without a self¶

    • [00:17:03] Given that they rejected an enduring self, you might expect the Buddhists to drop the cycle of rebirth altogether — but no chance. It was core teaching that desire leads to suffering partly because it triggers rebirth, and a fundamental part of the Buddha's own journey was that he could remember his past lives.
    • [00:17:34] In previous incarnations he had already become a bodhisattva ("enlightened one"), but in an act of extraordinary generosity — the act that makes him not just a sage but the saviour of humans and gods — he determined to return again and again until he could help others reach enlightenment, not only himself.
    • [00:17:56] The rest of us are reborn to less purpose. One Nikāya compares the person going through reincarnation to a dog tied to a stake, fruitlessly running in circles. Even the idea that caste membership reflects one's deeds in former lives appears in early Buddhist literature — the Brahmins had no monopoly on it.
    • [00:18:21] But with their no-self doctrine the Buddhists had an obvious difficulty explaining reincarnation. Their solution seems paradoxical or no answer at all: reflecting on the belief that we'll be requited for our actions in the next life, they denied that he who experiences the fruit of a deed is the same as the one who performed it — yet also denied that the two are different.

    The giraffe: sameness in a weaker sense¶

    • [00:18:50] Is this just embracing a contradiction? No — think more carefully about an aggregate changing over time. Take a giraffe: every day, through its digestive cycle, it loses and gains matter. Strictly, today's giraffe consists of different parts than yesterday's. Yet it's equally obvious that today's giraffe is not completely different — it retains some earlier parts, which is why we can truthfully say the giraffe gambolling in the field now is the same one that slept in the stable last night.
    • [00:19:29] Remember the Buddhists reject the Brahminical idea that the self is completely the same across time, an unchanging foundation enduring through changes of perception and even from one life-cycle to the next. If a person is just an aggregate of changing skandhas, it falls well short of that kind of sameness — yet may be the same in a weaker sense, e.g. because the stream of skandhas is causally interconnected.

    Rival answers and the Buddha's silence¶

    • [00:19:57] Buddhist philosophy was itself a changing agglomeration, with thinkers holding rival views across the centuries — including on this question of what (if any) weaker persistence carries a person from one incarnation to another. A minimal view follows Nāgasena's chariot: calling one person "the same" across time is merely a useful convention.
    • [00:20:28] At the other end, some Buddhists endorsed an underlying subject that overlies or emerges from the skandhas and can undergo rebirth — they called it the Pudgala, and their school the Pudgalavāda. Other Buddhists felt this was tantamount to conceding the Brahminical self.
    • [00:20:54] Similar tensions appear over how exactly actions determine one's fate: sometimes the Buddha mocks the Jain idea that actions cause karma to adhere to us across lives; at other times he insists, like the Ājīvakas, that the right course is to avoid action entirely, precisely because it brings karma.
    • [00:21:15] Perhaps the inevitability of such tensions explains why, when once asked whether there is really a self, the Buddha simply refused to answer — a favourite strategy. Like many great thinkers he had not only original proposals in metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics, but an interesting view about the status of those very proposals — the subject of the next episode, on the philosophical method of early Buddhism, where "the sound of silence can speak louder than words."

    Key thinkers, texts & terms¶

    • The Buddha — 5th-c. BCE kṣatriya prince who renounced palace life, attained enlightenment under a tree, and taught the Four Noble Truths; depicted serenely smiling despite teaching that life is suffering.
    • Four Noble Truths — (1) life is suffering; (2) its cause is desire/craving; (3) escape is possible (nirvāṇa); (4) there is a path to that escape.
    • duḥkha — "suffering," perhaps better "dissatisfaction"; the universal predicament — but it must be severe enough to justify restructuring one's whole life.
    • taṇhā — attached desire or craving (both longing and aversion); the cause of suffering named in the second truth.
    • nirvāṇa — liberation from all suffering, reached by acting on the no-self theory.
    • no-self (theory of no-self) — there is no unchanging centre of identity; a person is only an aggregate, so there is no self to be liberated or annihilated.
    • karma / rebirth — actions bear fruit across lives; death merely delivers one to another portion of suffering. The Buddhists keep rebirth despite denying a persisting self.
    • householder vs. renouncer — the family/prosperity life vs. the monastic life; the Buddha taught a middle way between passion and extreme asceticism.
    • Ājīvakas & Jains — contemporary movements seeking to escape the rebirth cycle entirely (the Ājīvakas' extreme asceticism the Buddha tried and rejected).
    • Pali canon / Nikāyas / Tripiṭaka — earliest Buddhist writings in Pali; the Nikāyas (the Buddha's sayings) sit alongside the Vinaya Piṭaka (monastic rules) and the Abhidharma (higher/philosophical teaching) in the "three baskets."
    • stupas — ancient burial mounds with railings and statues; non-textual source for early Buddhism.
    • dharma — here, the simple/fundamental component of any aggregate.
    • skandhas ("heaps") — the five types of dharma making up a person: physical form, feelings, emotions/perceptions, thinking, consciousness; momentary and the only constituents of a "self."
    • Milindapañha ("Questions of Menander") — text staging Nāgasena and king Menander of Bactria (2nd c. BCE); source of the chariot argument that the person is a conventional aggregate.
    • chariot / fist / giraffe — illustrations that wholes are conventional names for changing parts, yet retain a weaker, causally-connected sameness over time.
    • Pudgala / Pudgalavāda — a posited underlying subject that emerges from the skandhas and can be reborn, and the Buddhist school that affirmed it; criticized by others as conceding the Brahminical self.
    • bodhisattva — an "enlightened one"; the Buddha became one in past lives but chose to return repeatedly to save others.
    • Nāgārjuna — later Buddhist who would defend the no-self doctrine and attack the Brahminical self "with great virtuosity."
